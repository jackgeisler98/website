6. Applications of Integration

Stewart Ch. 6 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 2

Every application in this chapter uses the same idea: slice, approximate, add, take the limit. Chop the quantity you want (an area, a volume, an amount of work) into thin pieces. On each thin piece something is nearly constant, so the piece is easy to compute: a rectangle, a disk, a shell, a force times a small distance. Adding the pieces gives a Riemann sum, and letting the slices get thin turns the sum into a definite integral. If you can write down the volume, mass or work of one typical slice, you can set up the integral. The rest is Calculus I.

Study tip: the universal recipe

(1) Draw the region. (2) Draw one representative slice of thickness \(dx\) or \(dy\). (3) Write the small quantity \(dQ\) for that slice in terms of the slice's position. (4) Integrate over the range of positions. Most errors in this chapter happen at step (2), when the slice is not drawn.

6.1 Area between curves

A thin vertical strip at position \(x\) has height (top curve) minus (bottom curve) and width \(dx\). Adding strips gives the area.

Key formulas

Vertical strips, \(f(x)\ge g(x)\) on \([a,b]\):

\[ A = \int_a^b \big[f(x) - g(x)\big]\,dx = \int_a^b (\text{top} - \text{bottom})\,dx \]

Horizontal strips, \(x = R(y)\) to the right of \(x = L(y)\) on \([c,d]\):

\[ A = \int_c^d \big[R(y) - L(y)\big]\,dy = \int_c^d (\text{right} - \text{left})\,dy \]

If the curves cross, the area is \(\int_a^b |f(x)-g(x)|\,dx\): split the interval at each crossing point.

Common mistake

Writing \(\int_a^b (f-g)\,dx\) when the curves cross inside \([a,b]\). The pieces where \(g\) is on top contribute negatively and cancel real area. Always find every intersection first, then check which curve is on top on each sub-interval (plug in a test point).

Study tip: \(dx\) or \(dy\)?

Choose the direction where the top and bottom (or right and left) boundaries do not change formula. If vertical strips would hit different curves in different parts of the region, horizontal strips often need only one integral.

Example 6.1

Find the area enclosed by \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2 - 2x\).

Show solution

Intersections: \(x = x^2 - 2x \Rightarrow x^2 - 3x = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0, 3\). Test \(x = 1\): the line gives \(1\), the parabola gives \(-1\), so the line is on top.

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= \int_0^3 \big[x - (x^2 - 2x)\big]\,dx = \int_0^3 (3x - x^2)\,dx \\ &= \left[\tfrac{3}{2}x^2 - \tfrac{1}{3}x^3\right]_0^3 = \tfrac{27}{2} - 9 = \tfrac{9}{2}. \end{aligned} \]

Example 6.2

Find the area enclosed by \(x = y^2 - 4\) and \(x = 2 - y\).

Show solution

Both curves are given as \(x\) in terms of \(y\), so use horizontal strips. Intersections: \(y^2 - 4 = 2 - y \Rightarrow y^2 + y - 6 = 0 \Rightarrow (y+3)(y-2) = 0\), so \(y = -3, 2\). At \(y = 0\): the line gives \(x = 2\), the parabola gives \(x = -4\), so the line is on the right.

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= \int_{-3}^{2} \big[(2 - y) - (y^2 - 4)\big]\,dy = \int_{-3}^{2} (6 - y - y^2)\,dy \\ &= \left[6y - \tfrac{1}{2}y^2 - \tfrac{1}{3}y^3\right]_{-3}^{2} \\ &= \left(12 - 2 - \tfrac{8}{3}\right) - \left(-18 - \tfrac{9}{2} + 9\right) = \tfrac{22}{3} + \tfrac{27}{2} = \tfrac{125}{6}. \end{aligned} \]

With vertical strips, the left part of this region would be bounded above and below by the two branches of the parabola and the right part by the parabola and the line, so two integrals would be needed.

6.2 Volumes by slicing: disks and washers

Definition (volume by cross-sections)

Let a solid lie between the planes \(x = a\) and \(x = b\), and let \(A(x)\) be the area of its cross-section perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis. If \(A\) is continuous, the volume is

\[ V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx. \]

When the solid is made by revolving a region about an axis, each cross-section perpendicular to that axis is a disk (region touches the axis) or a washer (region is separated from the axis).

Key formulas: disks and washers

\[ V_{\text{disk}} = \int_a^b \pi R(x)^2\,dx, \qquad V_{\text{washer}} = \int_a^b \pi\big[R(x)^2 - r(x)^2\big]\,dx \]

\(R\) = outer radius, \(r\) = inner radius, both measured from the axis of rotation. The slices are perpendicular to the axis, so a horizontal axis means \(dx\) and a vertical axis means \(dy\).

Common mistake

\(\pi(R^2 - r^2)\) is not \(\pi(R - r)^2\). Square each radius separately. Also, when the axis is a line such as \(y = -1\), the radius is a distance to that line: \(R = (\text{curve}) - (-1)\), not just the curve's \(y\)-value.

Example 6.3

The region bounded by \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\) is revolved about (a) the \(x\)-axis, (b) the line \(y = -1\). Find each volume.

Show solution

The curves meet at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\), and \(x \ge x^2\) on \([0,1]\). A vertical slice revolved about a horizontal axis makes a washer.

(a) About the \(x\)-axis: \(R = x\), \(r = x^2\).

\[ V = \pi\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^4)\,dx = \pi\left(\tfrac13 - \tfrac15\right) = \tfrac{2\pi}{15}. \]

(b) About \(y = -1\): each radius gains 1. \(R = x + 1\), \(r = x^2 + 1\).

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= \pi\int_0^1 \big[(x+1)^2 - (x^2+1)^2\big]\,dx = \pi\int_0^1 \big[(x^2 + 2x + 1) - (x^4 + 2x^2 + 1)\big]\,dx \\ &= \pi\int_0^1 (-x^4 - x^2 + 2x)\,dx = \pi\left(-\tfrac15 - \tfrac13 + 1\right) = \tfrac{7\pi}{15}. \end{aligned} \]

Example 6.4 (known cross-sections)

A solid has as its base the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\). Cross-sections perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis are squares. Find the volume.

Show solution

At position \(x\), the base runs from \(y = -\sqrt{4 - x^2}\) to \(y = \sqrt{4-x^2}\), so the square's side is \(s = 2\sqrt{4 - x^2}\) and \(A(x) = s^2 = 4(4 - x^2)\).

\[ V = \int_{-2}^{2} 4(4 - x^2)\,dx = 4\left[4x - \tfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-2}^{2} = 4\left(\tfrac{16}{3} + \tfrac{16}{3}\right) = \tfrac{128}{3}. \]

6.3 Volumes by cylindrical shells

Instead of slicing perpendicular to the axis, take a thin strip parallel to the axis and spin it. It sweeps out a thin cylindrical shell. Cut the shell open and flatten it: it is nearly a rectangular slab with length \(2\pi r\) (the circumference), height \(h\), and thickness \(dr\). So \(dV \approx 2\pi r\,h\,dr\).

Key formula: shells

\[ V = \int_a^b 2\pi\,(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,dx \]

For rotation about the \(y\)-axis with vertical strips: radius \(= x\), height \(= f(x) - g(x)\). For rotation about \(x = k\): radius \(= |x - k|\). For a horizontal axis, use horizontal strips and integrate in \(y\).

Study tip: disks or shells?

Washers: slice perpendicular to the axis. Shells: slice parallel to the axis. Pick whichever lets you write the boundaries as functions of the variable you integrate in. Revolving \(y = f(x)\) about the \(y\)-axis with washers needs \(x = f^{-1}(y)\); shells avoid the inverse entirely.

Example 6.5

The region between \(y = x^2\), the \(x\)-axis and \(x = 1\) is revolved about the line \(x = 2\). Find the volume using shells, and check it with washers.

Show solution

Shells. A vertical strip at \(x\in[0,1]\) has height \(x^2\) and distance \(2 - x\) from the axis.

\[ V = \int_0^1 2\pi(2 - x)x^2\,dx = 2\pi\int_0^1 (2x^2 - x^3)\,dx = 2\pi\left(\tfrac23 - \tfrac14\right) = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac{5}{12} = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}. \]

Washers. Horizontal slices at height \(y \in [0,1]\): the region runs from \(x = \sqrt{y}\) to \(x = 1\). Distances to \(x = 2\): outer \(R = 2 - \sqrt y\), inner \(r = 2 - 1 = 1\).

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= \pi\int_0^1 \big[(2 - \sqrt y)^2 - 1\big]\,dy = \pi\int_0^1 (3 - 4\sqrt y + y)\,dy \\ &= \pi\left(3 - \tfrac83 + \tfrac12\right) = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}. \checkmark \end{aligned} \]

6.4 Work

For a constant force along a line, \(W = F d\). When the force varies with position, on a short interval \([x, x + dx]\) the force is nearly constant, so \(dW = F(x)\,dx\), and

\[ W = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx. \]

Units: newton-metres = joules (SI), or foot-pounds (US). In pumping and lifting problems a different slicing is natural: the force on each slice is constant (its weight), but each slice moves a different distance. Then \(dW = (\text{weight of slice})(\text{distance that slice moves})\).

Key formulas

Hooke's law (spring stretched \(x\) beyond natural length): \(F = kx\), so \(W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} kx\,dx = \tfrac{k}{2}(x_2^2 - x_1^2)\).

Pumping liquid of density \(\rho\) (slice at height \(y\), horizontal cross-sectional area \(A(y)\), lifted a distance \(D(y)\)):

\[ W = \int_{c}^{d} \rho g\,A(y)\,D(y)\,dy. \]

For water, \(\rho g \approx 9800\ \text{N/m}^3\) (SI) or \(62.5\ \text{lb/ft}^3\) (US, already a weight density).

Common mistake

In spring problems, \(x\) is the displacement from the natural length, not the total length. If a 30 cm spring is stretched to 40 cm, \(x = 0.10\) m.

Example 6.6 (spring)

A force of 6 N holds a spring stretched 0.2 m beyond its natural length. How much work is needed to stretch it from 0.2 m to 0.5 m beyond natural length?

Show solution

\(6 = k(0.2) \Rightarrow k = 30\) N/m.

\[ W = \int_{0.2}^{0.5} 30x\,dx = 15\big(0.5^2 - 0.2^2\big) = 15(0.25 - 0.04) = 3.15\ \text{J}. \]

Example 6.7 (pumping)

A vertical cylindrical tank of radius 2 m and height 5 m is full of water. Find the work needed to pump all the water out over the top rim.

Show solution

Put \(y = 0\) at the bottom. The slice at height \(y\) with thickness \(dy\) has volume \(\pi(2)^2\,dy = 4\pi\,dy\), weight \(9800 \cdot 4\pi\,dy\) N, and must be lifted \(5 - y\) m.

\[ \begin{aligned} W &= \int_0^5 9800\cdot 4\pi\,(5 - y)\,dy = 39200\pi\left[5y - \tfrac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^5 \\ &= 39200\pi(12.5) = 490000\pi \approx 1.54\times 10^6\ \text{J}. \end{aligned} \]

Example 6.8 (winding a cable)

A 20 m cable with mass 3 kg/m hangs from the top of a building. Find the work to wind all of it onto a drum at the top.

Show solution

The piece of cable at depth \(x\) below the top, of length \(dx\), weighs \(3(9.8)\,dx = 29.4\,dx\) N and is lifted \(x\) m.

\[ W = \int_0^{20} 29.4\,x\,dx = 29.4\cdot\tfrac{20^2}{2} = 5880\ \text{J}. \]

Check: total weight \(588\) N, centre of mass 10 m down, so \(W = 588 \times 10 = 5880\) J. Lifting work only depends on how far the centre of mass rises.

Physics connection

\(W = \int F\,dx\) is the one-dimensional version of the line integral \(W = \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\) from vector calculus. The spring result \(\tfrac12 kx^2\) is the stored elastic potential energy; the same integral gives the energy in a capacitor, \(\int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C}\), and in an inductor, \(\int_0^I L i\,di = \tfrac12 L I^2\).

6.5 Average value of a function

Definition

The average value of an integrable \(f\) on \([a,b]\) is

\[ f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

This is the limit of the ordinary average of \(n\) equally spaced samples, \(\frac1n\sum f(x_i) = \frac{1}{b-a}\sum f(x_i)\,\Delta x\), as \(n\to\infty\).

Theorem (Mean Value Theorem for Integrals)

If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), there is some \(c \in [a,b]\) with

\[ f(c) = f_{\text{avg}}, \quad\text{that is,}\quad \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = f(c)(b-a). \]

Proof sketch. By the Extreme Value Theorem \(f\) has a minimum \(m\) and maximum \(M\) on \([a,b]\). Then \(m(b-a) \le \int_a^b f \le M(b-a)\), so \(m \le f_{\text{avg}} \le M\). By the Intermediate Value Theorem, \(f\) takes the value \(f_{\text{avg}}\) somewhere in \([a,b]\).

Example 6.9

Find the average value of \(\sin x\) on \([0,\pi]\) and every \(c\) where it is attained.

Show solution \[ f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \sin x\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\big[-\cos x\big]_0^\pi = \frac{1}{\pi}(1 + 1) = \frac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.637. \]

Solve \(\sin c = 2/\pi\): \(c = \arcsin(2/\pi) \approx 0.690\) and \(c = \pi - \arcsin(2/\pi) \approx 2.451\).

Engineering connection: RMS values

The root-mean-square value is the square root of the average of \(f^2\). For \(v(t) = V_0\sin(\omega t)\) over one period \(T = 2\pi/\omega\), the average of \(\sin^2\) is \(\tfrac12\) (use \(\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\theta)\)), so \(V_{\text{rms}} = V_0/\sqrt2\). The plain average of \(v\) over a period is zero, which is why RMS, not the mean, measures delivered power.

Practice problems

  1. Find the area between \(y = x^2\) and \(y = 4\).
    Answer

    \(\int_{-2}^{2}(4 - x^2)\,dx = 2\left(8 - \tfrac83\right) = \tfrac{32}{3}\).

  2. Find the area between \(y = \sin x\) and \(y = \cos x\) for \(0 \le x \le \pi/2\).
    Answer

    They cross at \(\pi/4\); by symmetry the two pieces are equal: \(2\int_0^{\pi/4}(\cos x - \sin x)\,dx = 2\big[\sin x + \cos x\big]_0^{\pi/4} = 2(\sqrt2 - 1)\).

  3. The region under \(y = e^x\), \(0 \le x \le 1\), is revolved about the \(x\)-axis. Find the volume.
    Answer

    Disks: \(\pi\int_0^1 e^{2x}\,dx = \tfrac{\pi}{2}(e^2 - 1) \approx 10.04\).

  4. The region under \(y = \sqrt x\), \(0 \le x \le 4\), is revolved about the \(y\)-axis. Find the volume.
    Answer

    Shells: \(2\pi\int_0^4 x\cdot x^{1/2}\,dx = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac25\big[x^{5/2}\big]_0^4 = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac25\cdot 32 = \tfrac{128\pi}{5}\).

  5. A 10 kg bucket is lifted 15 m by a rope of mass 0.5 kg/m that is wound up as the bucket rises. Find the total work (\(g = 9.8\)).
    Answer

    Bucket: \(10(9.8)(15) = 1470\) J. Rope: \(\int_0^{15} 0.5(9.8)x\,dx = 4.9\cdot 112.5 = 551.25\) J. Total \(2021.25\) J.

  6. Find the average value of \(f(x) = x^2\) on \([0,3]\) and the point \(c\) guaranteed by the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals.
    Answer

    \(f_{\text{avg}} = \tfrac13\int_0^3 x^2\,dx = \tfrac13\cdot 9 = 3\); \(c^2 = 3 \Rightarrow c = \sqrt3\).

7. Techniques of Integration

Stewart Ch. 7 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 3

Differentiation is mechanical: there is a rule for every combination of functions. Integration is not. There is no product rule or quotient rule for antiderivatives, and many elementary functions (such as \(e^{-x^2}\) or \(\sin x / x\)) have no elementary antiderivative at all. This chapter is a toolkit of transformations, each one undoing a differentiation rule or using an algebraic identity, that turn a hard integral into one you already know. When no transformation works, numerical methods give an answer to any required accuracy. Finally, improper integrals extend the definite integral to infinite intervals and unbounded integrands.

7.1 Integration by parts

Theorem (Integration by parts)

If \(u\) and \(v\) are differentiable with continuous derivatives,

\[ \int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du, \qquad \int_a^b u\,dv = \big[uv\big]_a^b - \int_a^b v\,du. \]

Proof. The product rule says \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\). Integrate both sides: \(uv = \int u'v\,dx + \int uv'\,dx\). Rearrange to \(\int uv'\,dx = uv - \int vu'\,dx\), and write \(dv = v'\,dx\), \(du = u'\,dx\). So integration by parts is the product rule run backwards, just as substitution is the chain rule run backwards.

Study tip: choosing \(u\)

Choose \(u\) so that \(du\) is simpler than \(u\), and \(dv\) so that you can integrate it. A useful priority order for \(u\) is LIATE: Logarithmic, Inverse trig, Algebraic (polynomials), Trigonometric, Exponential. It is a heuristic, not a law. For \(\int x^n e^{ax}\,dx\) or \(\int x^n \sin(ax)\,dx\), repeated parts can be organised in a table: differentiate \(u\) down to zero in one column, integrate \(dv\) repeatedly in the other, and multiply along the diagonals with alternating signs (the tabular method).

Example 7.1

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int x e^{2x}\,dx\).

Show solution

Let \(u = x\), \(dv = e^{2x}\,dx\). Then \(du = dx\), \(v = \tfrac12 e^{2x}\).

\[ \int x e^{2x}\,dx = \tfrac12 x e^{2x} - \int \tfrac12 e^{2x}\,dx = \tfrac12 x e^{2x} - \tfrac14 e^{2x} + C = \tfrac14 e^{2x}(2x - 1) + C. \]

Check by differentiating: \(\tfrac14\big[2e^{2x}(2x-1) + 2e^{2x}\big] = \tfrac14 e^{2x}(4x) = xe^{2x}\). \(\checkmark\)

Example 7.2 (definite integral)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^e x^2 \ln x\,dx\).

Show solution

The log goes in \(u\): \(u = \ln x\), \(dv = x^2\,dx\), so \(du = \frac{dx}{x}\), \(v = \frac{x^3}{3}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_1^e x^2\ln x\,dx &= \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\ln x\right]_1^e - \int_1^e \frac{x^3}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{x}\,dx = \frac{e^3}{3} - \left[\frac{x^3}{9}\right]_1^e \\ &= \frac{e^3}{3} - \frac{e^3}{9} + \frac19 = \frac{2e^3 + 1}{9} \approx 4.57. \end{aligned} \]

Example 7.3 (the integral comes back)

Evaluate \(I = \displaystyle\int e^x \cos x\,dx\).

Show solution

Parts with \(u = \cos x\), \(dv = e^x\,dx\): \(I = e^x\cos x + \int e^x \sin x\,dx\).

Parts again with \(u = \sin x\), \(dv = e^x\,dx\) (keep the same kind of choice, or you will undo the first step): \(\int e^x\sin x\,dx = e^x \sin x - \int e^x\cos x\,dx = e^x\sin x - I\).

\[ I = e^x\cos x + e^x\sin x - I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = \tfrac12 e^x(\sin x + \cos x) + C. \]

Example 7.4 (tabular method)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int x^3 e^{x}\,dx\).

Show solution

Derivatives of \(x^3\): \(x^3, 3x^2, 6x, 6, 0\). Repeated integrals of \(e^x\): \(e^x\) each time. Signs alternate \(+,-,+,-\).

\[ \int x^3e^x\,dx = x^3e^x - 3x^2e^x + 6xe^x - 6e^x + C = e^x(x^3 - 3x^2 + 6x - 6) + C. \]

7.2 Trigonometric integrals

The tools are the Pythagorean identities and the half-angle (power-reduction) formulas:

\[ \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1,\quad 1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x,\quad \sin^2 x = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2x),\quad \cos^2 x = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2x). \]

Strategy for \(\int \sin^m x\cos^n x\,dx\)

  • \(n\) odd: save one \(\cos x\), convert the remaining even power of cosine with \(\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x\), substitute \(u = \sin x\).
  • \(m\) odd: save one \(\sin x\), convert with \(\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x\), substitute \(u = \cos x\).
  • Both even: use the half-angle formulas (possibly repeatedly).

Strategy for \(\int \tan^m x\sec^n x\,dx\)

  • \(n\) even (\(n \ge 2\)): save \(\sec^2 x\), convert the rest with \(\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x\), substitute \(u = \tan x\).
  • \(m\) odd: save \(\sec x\tan x\), convert with \(\tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1\), substitute \(u = \sec x\).

Useful results: \(\int\tan x\,dx = \ln|\sec x| + C\), \(\int\sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + C\).

Example 7.5

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int \sin^3 x\cos^2 x\,dx\).

Show solution

The sine power is odd: save one \(\sin x\). Let \(u = \cos x\), \(du = -\sin x\,dx\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int \sin^2 x\cos^2 x\,\sin x\,dx &= \int (1 - \cos^2 x)\cos^2 x\,\sin x\,dx = -\int (1 - u^2)u^2\,du \\ &= -\frac{u^3}{3} + \frac{u^5}{5} + C = \frac{\cos^5 x}{5} - \frac{\cos^3 x}{3} + C. \end{aligned} \]

Example 7.6 (both powers even)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^\pi \sin^4 x\,dx\).

Show solution \[ \begin{aligned} \sin^4 x &= \left(\frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}\right)^2 = \frac14\left(1 - 2\cos 2x + \cos^2 2x\right) \\ &= \frac14\left(1 - 2\cos 2x + \frac{1 + \cos 4x}{2}\right) = \frac38 - \frac12\cos 2x + \frac18\cos 4x. \end{aligned} \] \[ \int_0^\pi \sin^4 x\,dx = \left[\frac{3x}{8} - \frac{\sin 2x}{4} + \frac{\sin 4x}{32}\right]_0^\pi = \frac{3\pi}{8}. \]

Example 7.7

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int \tan^3 x\sec^4 x\,dx\).

Show solution

The secant power is even: save \(\sec^2 x\) and let \(u = \tan x\), \(du = \sec^2 x\,dx\).

\[ \int \tan^3 x\,(1 + \tan^2 x)\sec^2 x\,dx = \int (u^3 + u^5)\,du = \frac{\tan^4 x}{4} + \frac{\tan^6 x}{6} + C. \]

Products with different frequencies

\[ \begin{aligned} \sin A\cos B &= \tfrac12\big[\sin(A - B) + \sin(A + B)\big] \\ \sin A\sin B &= \tfrac12\big[\cos(A - B) - \cos(A + B)\big] \\ \cos A\cos B &= \tfrac12\big[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)\big] \end{aligned} \]

Physics connection: orthogonality

Using the product formulas, for positive integers \(m \ne n\), \(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin mx\sin nx\,dx = 0\), and for \(m = n\) the integral is \(\pi\). The same holds for cosines, and \(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin mx\cos nx\,dx = 0\) for all \(m, n\). This orthogonality is exactly what makes Fourier series work: it lets you isolate one harmonic of a signal by multiplying and integrating.

7.3 Trigonometric substitution

A square root of a quadratic is the hypotenuse or a leg of a right triangle. Substituting a trig function for \(x\) turns the radical into a single trig function via a Pythagorean identity. This is an inverse substitution: we set \(x = g(\theta)\) rather than \(u = g(x)\), so \(g\) must be invertible on the chosen \(\theta\)-interval.

Example 7.8

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{9 - x^2}}\).

Show solution

Let \(x = 3\sin\theta\), \(dx = 3\cos\theta\,d\theta\), \(\sqrt{9 - x^2} = 3\cos\theta\) (positive because \(|\theta| \le \pi/2\)).

\[ \int \frac{3\cos\theta\,d\theta}{9\sin^2\theta\cdot 3\cos\theta} = \frac19\int\csc^2\theta\,d\theta = -\frac19\cot\theta + C. \]

Triangle: opposite \(x\), hypotenuse 3, adjacent \(\sqrt{9 - x^2}\), so \(\cot\theta = \sqrt{9 - x^2}/x\).

\[ \int \frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{9 - x^2}} = -\frac{\sqrt{9 - x^2}}{9x} + C. \]

Example 7.9

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 \frac{dx}{(x^2 + 4)^{3/2}}\).

Show solution

Let \(x = 2\tan\theta\), \(dx = 2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\), \((x^2 + 4)^{3/2} = (4\sec^2\theta)^{3/2} = 8\sec^3\theta\). Limits: \(x = 0 \Rightarrow \theta = 0\); \(x = 2 \Rightarrow \theta = \pi/4\).

\[ \int_0^{\pi/4} \frac{2\sec^2\theta}{8\sec^3\theta}\,d\theta = \frac14\int_0^{\pi/4}\cos\theta\,d\theta = \frac14\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\sqrt2}{8}. \]

Example 7.10 (area of an ellipse)

Show that the ellipse \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) encloses area \(\pi ab\).

Show solution

By symmetry, \(A = 4\int_0^a \frac{b}{a}\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\,dx\). Let \(x = a\sin\theta\), \(dx = a\cos\theta\,d\theta\), with \(\theta\) from \(0\) to \(\pi/2\):

\[ \int_0^a\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\,dx = \int_0^{\pi/2} a^2\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = a^2\left[\frac{\theta}{2} + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\right]_0^{\pi/2} = \frac{\pi a^2}{4}. \]

So \(A = 4\cdot\frac{b}{a}\cdot\frac{\pi a^2}{4} = \pi ab\). With \(a = b = r\) this recovers \(\pi r^2\).

Study tip

For a general quadratic \(ax^2 + bx + c\) under a root, complete the square first, then substitute for the shifted variable. Example: \(x^2 + 2x + 5 = (x+1)^2 + 4\), so let \(x + 1 = 2\tan\theta\).

7.4 Partial fractions

To integrate a rational function \(P(x)/Q(x)\), break it into a sum of simple fractions, each of which integrates to a log, a power, or an arctangent. This is the algebra of adding fractions run in reverse.

Procedure

  1. Make it proper. If \(\deg P \ge \deg Q\), long-divide: \(P/Q = S(x) + R(x)/Q(x)\) with \(\deg R \lt \deg Q\).
  2. Factor \(Q\) completely into linear factors and irreducible quadratics (\(b^2 - 4ac \lt 0\)).
  3. Write the decomposition, one group of terms per factor:
    • Distinct linear \((x - r)\): \(\dfrac{A}{x - r}\).
    • Repeated linear \((x - r)^k\): \(\dfrac{A_1}{x - r} + \dfrac{A_2}{(x - r)^2} + \cdots + \dfrac{A_k}{(x - r)^k}\).
    • Irreducible quadratic \(ax^2 + bx + c\): \(\dfrac{Bx + C}{ax^2 + bx + c}\) (repeated: one such term for each power).
  4. Find the constants by multiplying through by \(Q\), then substituting convenient \(x\)-values (roots) and/or matching coefficients.

Common mistake

Over a quadratic factor the numerator must be linear, \(Bx + C\), not a constant. And a repeated factor \((x-1)^2\) needs both \(\frac{A}{x-1}\) and \(\frac{B}{(x-1)^2}\); using only the second makes the system unsolvable.

Example 7.11 (distinct linear factors)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int \frac{5x - 4}{x^2 - x - 2}\,dx\).

Show solution

\(x^2 - x - 2 = (x - 2)(x + 1)\). Write \(\frac{5x - 4}{(x-2)(x+1)} = \frac{A}{x - 2} + \frac{B}{x + 1}\), so \(5x - 4 = A(x + 1) + B(x - 2)\).

\(x = 2\): \(6 = 3A \Rightarrow A = 2\). \(x = -1\): \(-9 = -3B \Rightarrow B = 3\).

\[ \int \left(\frac{2}{x - 2} + \frac{3}{x + 1}\right)dx = 2\ln|x - 2| + 3\ln|x + 1| + C. \]

Example 7.12 (repeated linear factor)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int \frac{x^2 + 1}{x(x - 1)^2}\,dx\).

Show solution

\(\frac{x^2 + 1}{x(x-1)^2} = \frac{A}{x} + \frac{B}{x - 1} + \frac{C}{(x - 1)^2}\), so \(x^2 + 1 = A(x - 1)^2 + Bx(x - 1) + Cx\).

\(x = 0\): \(1 = A\). \(x = 1\): \(2 = C\). Coefficient of \(x^2\): \(1 = A + B \Rightarrow B = 0\).

\[ \int\left(\frac1x + \frac{2}{(x - 1)^2}\right)dx = \ln|x| - \frac{2}{x - 1} + C. \]

Example 7.13 (irreducible quadratic)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int \frac{2x^2 + x + 4}{x^3 + 4x}\,dx\).

Show solution

\(x^3 + 4x = x(x^2 + 4)\), and \(x^2 + 4\) is irreducible. Write \(\frac{2x^2 + x + 4}{x(x^2 + 4)} = \frac{A}{x} + \frac{Bx + C}{x^2 + 4}\), so

\[ 2x^2 + x + 4 = A(x^2 + 4) + (Bx + C)x = (A + B)x^2 + Cx + 4A. \]

Matching: \(4A = 4 \Rightarrow A = 1\); \(A + B = 2 \Rightarrow B = 1\); \(C = 1\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int\left(\frac1x + \frac{x}{x^2 + 4} + \frac{1}{x^2 + 4}\right)dx &= \ln|x| + \tfrac12\ln(x^2 + 4) + \tfrac12\arctan\frac{x}{2} + C. \end{aligned} \]

Engineering connection

Inverting a Laplace transform such as \(\frac{s + 3}{(s + 1)(s + 2)}\) is the same partial-fraction decomposition: each simple pole \(\frac{A}{s + a}\) becomes a decaying exponential \(Ae^{-at}\) in the time domain, and irreducible quadratics become damped sinusoids. The "cover-up" trick (plug in the root of each linear factor) is exactly the residue calculation.

7.5 Strategy for integration

A checklist

  1. Simplify first. Expand, cancel, split fractions, use an identity (\(\sin x\cos x = \tfrac12\sin 2x\), etc.).
  2. Look for an obvious substitution: a function whose derivative also appears (up to a constant).
  3. Classify by form:
    • Product of a polynomial with \(e^{ax}\), \(\sin ax\), \(\ln x\), \(\arctan x\): parts.
    • Powers of trig functions: trig-integral strategies.
    • \(\sqrt{\pm x^2 \pm a^2}\): trig substitution.
    • Rational function: partial fractions (after division if needed).
    • \(\sqrt[n]{g(x)}\) alone: try \(u = \sqrt[n]{g(x)}\).
  4. Try again: manipulate the integrand (multiply by a conjugate, rewrite in terms of sine and cosine), or combine methods.

Some integrals, such as \(\int e^{-x^2}\,dx\), \(\int \frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx\) and \(\int\sqrt{1 + x^3}\,dx\), have no elementary antiderivative. For those, use series (Chapter 11) or numerical methods.

7.6 Numerical integration

Split \([a,b]\) into \(n\) subintervals of width \(\Delta x = (b - a)/n\) with endpoints \(x_i = a + i\Delta x\) and midpoints \(\bar x_i = \tfrac12(x_{i-1} + x_i)\).

Key formulas

\[ \begin{aligned} M_n &= \Delta x\big[f(\bar x_1) + f(\bar x_2) + \cdots + f(\bar x_n)\big] &&\text{(midpoint)} \\ T_n &= \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}\big[f(x_0) + 2f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + \cdots + 2f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)\big] &&\text{(trapezoid)} \\ S_n &= \tfrac{\Delta x}{3}\big[f(x_0) + 4f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + 4f(x_3) + \cdots + 4f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)\big] &&\text{(Simpson, } n \text{ even)} \end{aligned} \]

The trapezoid rule replaces \(f\) by straight segments; Simpson's rule fits a parabola through each pair of subintervals. Also \(T_n = \tfrac12(L_n + R_n)\) and \(S_{2n} = \tfrac13 T_n + \tfrac23 M_n\).

Theorem (Error bounds)

If \(|f''(x)| \le K\) on \([a,b]\), then

\[ |E_T| \le \frac{K(b - a)^3}{12n^2}, \qquad |E_M| \le \frac{K(b - a)^3}{24n^2}. \]

If \(|f^{(4)}(x)| \le K\) on \([a,b]\), then

\[ |E_S| \le \frac{K(b - a)^5}{180n^4}. \]

Doubling \(n\) cuts the trapezoid and midpoint error bounds by a factor of 4, and Simpson's by a factor of 16. Simpson's rule is exact for cubics, since \(f^{(4)} = 0\).

Study tip: signs of the error

If \(f\) is concave up (\(f'' \gt 0\)), the chords lie above the graph, so \(T_n\) overestimates and \(M_n\) underestimates. The midpoint error is about half the trapezoid error and of opposite sign, which is why the weighted average \(\tfrac13 T_n + \tfrac23 M_n\) (Simpson) cancels most of both.

Example 7.14

Approximate \(\displaystyle\int_1^2 \frac{dx}{x} = \ln 2 \approx 0.693147\) using \(T_4\), \(M_4\) and \(S_4\). Compare the trapezoid error with its bound. How large must \(n\) be to guarantee Simpson's error is below \(10^{-6}\)?

Show solution

\(\Delta x = 0.25\). Endpoints \(1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2\) give \(f = 1,\ 0.8,\ 0.666667,\ 0.571429,\ 0.5\). Midpoints \(1.125, 1.375, 1.625, 1.875\) give \(f = 0.888889,\ 0.727273,\ 0.615385,\ 0.533333\).

\[ \begin{aligned} T_4 &= 0.125\big[1 + 2(0.8 + 0.666667 + 0.571429) + 0.5\big] = 0.125(5.576190) \approx 0.697024 \\ M_4 &= 0.25(0.888889 + 0.727273 + 0.615385 + 0.533333) = 0.25(2.764880) \approx 0.691220 \\ S_4 &= \tfrac{0.25}{3}\big[1 + 4(0.8) + 2(0.666667) + 4(0.571429) + 0.5\big] = \tfrac{0.25}{3}(8.319048) \approx 0.693254 \end{aligned} \]

Errors (exact minus approximation): \(E_T \approx -0.00388\), \(E_M \approx +0.00193\), \(E_S \approx -0.00011\). Since \(1/x\) is concave up, \(T_4\) is too big and \(M_4\) too small, as predicted.

Trapezoid bound: \(f''(x) = 2/x^3 \le 2\) on \([1,2]\), so \(|E_T| \le \frac{2\cdot 1^3}{12\cdot 16} = \frac{1}{96} \approx 0.0104\). The actual error 0.0039 is within this bound.

Simpson: \(f^{(4)}(x) = 24/x^5 \le 24\). Need \(\frac{24}{180n^4} \lt 10^{-6}\), so \(n^4 \gt 133{,}333\), \(n \gt 19.1\). Since \(n\) must be even, \(n = 20\). (For the trapezoid rule the same accuracy needs \(\frac{2}{12n^2} \lt 10^{-6}\), i.e. \(n \ge 409\).)

7.7 Improper integrals

Definition

Type 1 (infinite interval). If \(\int_a^t f(x)\,dx\) exists for every \(t \ge a\),

\[ \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^t f(x)\,dx, \]

and similarly for \(\int_{-\infty}^b\). The integral converges if the limit exists (as a finite number) and diverges otherwise. \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty f\) is defined as \(\int_{-\infty}^a f + \int_a^\infty f\), and converges only if both pieces converge.

Type 2 (discontinuous integrand). If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b)\) but unbounded near \(b\),

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{t\to b^-}\int_a^t f(x)\,dx, \]

and similarly at the left endpoint. If the discontinuity is at an interior point \(c\), split: \(\int_a^b = \int_a^c + \int_c^b\), and both must converge.

Theorem (\(p\)-integrals)

\[ \int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x^p}\ \text{converges} \iff p \gt 1 \ \left(\text{value } \tfrac{1}{p - 1}\right), \qquad \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{x^p}\ \text{converges} \iff p \lt 1 \ \left(\text{value } \tfrac{1}{1 - p}\right). \]

Proof. For \(p \ne 1\), \(\int_1^t x^{-p}\,dx = \frac{t^{1-p} - 1}{1 - p}\). As \(t\to\infty\), \(t^{1-p}\to 0\) if \(p \gt 1\) and \(\to\infty\) if \(p \lt 1\). For \(p = 1\), \(\int_1^t \frac{dx}{x} = \ln t \to \infty\). The \([0,1]\) case is the same calculation with \(t \to 0^+\), where now \(t^{1-p} \to 0\) needs \(p \lt 1\).

Theorem (Comparison test for integrals)

Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous with \(0 \le g(x) \le f(x)\) for \(x \ge a\).

  • If \(\int_a^\infty f\,dx\) converges, then \(\int_a^\infty g\,dx\) converges. (Smaller than finite is finite.)
  • If \(\int_a^\infty g\,dx\) diverges, then \(\int_a^\infty f\,dx\) diverges. (Bigger than infinite is infinite.)

Knowing that \(g\) is smaller than a divergent integral, or larger than a convergent one, tells you nothing.

Common mistake

Ignoring an interior discontinuity. Naively, \(\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2} = \left[-\frac1x\right]_{-1}^{1} = -2\), a negative answer for a positive integrand. In fact \(\int_0^1 x^{-2}\,dx\) diverges (\(p = 2 \ge 1\)), so the whole integral diverges. Always check the integrand for vertical asymptotes on the interval.

Example 7.15

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^\infty x e^{-x}\,dx\).

Show solution

Parts with \(u = x\), \(dv = e^{-x}dx\): \(\int xe^{-x}dx = -xe^{-x} - e^{-x} + C\).

\[ \int_0^\infty xe^{-x}\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\Big[-te^{-t} - e^{-t} + 1\Big] = 0 - 0 + 1 = 1, \]

using \(te^{-t} = t/e^t \to 0\) (L'Hôpital). In probability this is the mean of an exponential random variable with rate 1.

Example 7.16 (interior discontinuity)

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 \frac{dx}{(x - 1)^{2/3}}\).

Show solution

The integrand blows up at \(x = 1\), so split there. An antiderivative is \(3(x - 1)^{1/3}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{(x-1)^{2/3}} &= \lim_{t\to 1^-}\Big[3(x - 1)^{1/3}\Big]_0^t = 0 - 3(-1) = 3, \\ \int_1^3 \frac{dx}{(x-1)^{2/3}} &= \lim_{s\to 1^+}\Big[3(x - 1)^{1/3}\Big]_s^3 = 3\sqrt[3]{2} - 0. \end{aligned} \]

Both converge, so the integral equals \(3 + 3\sqrt[3]{2} \approx 6.78\).

Example 7.17 (comparison)

Determine whether (a) \(\displaystyle\int_1^\infty \frac{1 + \sin^2 x}{x^2}\,dx\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\int_2^\infty \frac{dx}{\sqrt x - 1}\) converge.

Show solution

(a) \(0 \le \frac{1 + \sin^2 x}{x^2} \le \frac{2}{x^2}\) and \(\int_1^\infty \frac{2}{x^2}dx = 2\) converges, so (a) converges.

(b) For \(x \ge 2\), \(\sqrt x - 1 \lt \sqrt x\), so \(\frac{1}{\sqrt x - 1} \gt \frac{1}{\sqrt x} \gt 0\). Since \(\int_2^\infty x^{-1/2}dx\) diverges (\(p = \tfrac12\)), (b) diverges.

Physics connection: escape energy

The work needed to move a mass \(m\) from a planet's surface (radius \(R\)) to infinity is \(\int_R^\infty \frac{GMm}{r^2}\,dr = \frac{GMm}{R}\). It is finite because \(1/r^2\) has \(p = 2 \gt 1\). Setting \(\tfrac12 mv^2 = GMm/R\) gives the escape speed \(v = \sqrt{2GM/R}\).

Practice problems

  1. Evaluate \(\int x\sin 3x\,dx\).
    Answer

    \(u = x\), \(dv = \sin 3x\,dx\), \(v = -\tfrac13\cos 3x\): \(-\tfrac{x}{3}\cos 3x + \tfrac13\int\cos 3x\,dx = -\tfrac{x}{3}\cos 3x + \tfrac19\sin 3x + C\).

  2. Evaluate \(\int \sin^2 x\cos^3 x\,dx\).
    Answer

    Save \(\cos x\), \(u = \sin x\): \(\int u^2(1 - u^2)\,du = \tfrac{\sin^3 x}{3} - \tfrac{\sin^5 x}{5} + C\).

  3. Evaluate \(\int \frac{\sqrt{x^2 - 9}}{x}\,dx\) for \(x \gt 3\).
    Answer

    \(x = 3\sec\theta\), \(dx = 3\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta\), \(\sqrt{x^2 - 9} = 3\tan\theta\): \(\int \frac{3\tan\theta}{3\sec\theta}3\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta = 3\int\tan^2\theta\,d\theta = 3(\tan\theta - \theta) + C = \sqrt{x^2 - 9} - 3\,\mathrm{arcsec}(x/3) + C\).

  4. Evaluate \(\int \frac{dx}{x^2 - 4}\).
    Answer

    \(\frac{1}{(x-2)(x+2)} = \frac{1/4}{x - 2} - \frac{1/4}{x + 2}\), so the integral is \(\tfrac14\ln\left|\frac{x - 2}{x + 2}\right| + C\).

  5. Evaluate \(\int_1^\infty \frac{\ln x}{x^2}\,dx\) or show it diverges.
    Answer

    Parts: \(u = \ln x\), \(dv = x^{-2}dx\): antiderivative \(-\frac{\ln x}{x} - \frac1x\). As \(t\to\infty\), \(\frac{\ln t}{t}\to 0\), so the value is \(0 - (0 - 1) = 1\). Converges to 1.

  6. Use \(S_2\) to approximate \(\int_0^2 x^4\,dx\). Compare the actual error with the Simpson error bound.
    Answer

    \(\Delta x = 1\): \(S_2 = \tfrac13[0 + 4(1) + 16] = \tfrac{20}{3} \approx 6.667\); exact value \(\tfrac{32}{5} = 6.4\); error \(\approx 0.267\). Bound: \(K = 24\), \(\frac{24\cdot 2^5}{180\cdot 2^4} = \frac{768}{2880} \approx 0.267\). The bound is attained exactly because \(f^{(4)}\) is constant.

8. Further Applications of Integration

Stewart Ch. 8 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 2

The slicing idea from Chapter 6 now meets geometry and physics. The length of a curve is the limit of the lengths of inscribed polygons; each tiny polygon edge is a hypotenuse, which brings in a square root. A surface of revolution is approximated by thin bands (frustums of cones). A center of mass is a weighted average of position, so it is a ratio of two integrals. Fluid pressure grows with depth, so the force on a submerged wall is an integral of pressure times strip area. In every case: find the contribution of one thin piece, then integrate.

8.1 Arc length

Over a short step \(\Delta x\), the curve rises \(\Delta y\), so the chord has length \(\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2} = \sqrt{1 + (\Delta y/\Delta x)^2}\,\Delta x\). By the Mean Value Theorem, \(\Delta y/\Delta x = f'(x_i^*)\) for some point in the step. Summing and taking the limit gives the formula below. The shorthand to remember is \(ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}\).

Theorem (Arc length)

If \(f'\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), the length of \(y = f(x)\), \(a \le x \le b\), is

\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \big[f'(x)\big]^2}\,dx. \]

For \(x = g(y)\), \(c \le y \le d\): \(L = \int_c^d \sqrt{1 + [g'(y)]^2}\,dy\). The arc length function \(s(x) = \int_a^x \sqrt{1 + [f'(t)]^2}\,dt\) measures length from the starting point, and \(ds/dx = \sqrt{1 + (f')^2}\).

Study tip

Textbook arc-length problems are built so that \(1 + (f')^2\) is a perfect square. If you see \(f\) of the form \(\frac{x^2}{4} - \frac{\ln x}{2}\) or \(\frac{x^3}{6} + \frac{1}{2x}\), expect \(1 + (f')^2 = (\text{something})^2\). For most curves (even \(y = x^2\) or \(y = \sin x\)), the integral is hard or impossible in closed form, and Simpson's rule is the practical tool.

Example 8.1

Find the length of \(y = \tfrac23 x^{3/2}\) for \(0 \le x \le 3\).

Show solution

\(y' = x^{1/2}\), so \(1 + (y')^2 = 1 + x\).

\[ L = \int_0^3 \sqrt{1 + x}\,dx = \tfrac23\big[(1 + x)^{3/2}\big]_0^3 = \tfrac23(8 - 1) = \tfrac{14}{3}. \]

Example 8.2 (perfect-square trick)

Find the length of \(y = \dfrac{x^2}{4} - \dfrac{\ln x}{2}\) for \(1 \le x \le e\).

Show solution

\(y' = \frac{x}{2} - \frac{1}{2x}\), so

\[ 1 + (y')^2 = 1 + \frac{x^2}{4} - \frac12 + \frac{1}{4x^2} = \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac12 + \frac{1}{4x^2} = \left(\frac{x}{2} + \frac{1}{2x}\right)^2. \] \[ L = \int_1^e\left(\frac x2 + \frac{1}{2x}\right)dx = \left[\frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{\ln x}{2}\right]_1^e = \frac{e^2}{4} + \frac12 - \frac14 = \frac{e^2 + 1}{4} \approx 2.097. \]

8.2 Area of a surface of revolution

Revolve a short piece of curve of length \(ds\), at distance \(r\) from the axis. It sweeps out a thin band whose area is approximately circumference times width: \(2\pi r\,ds\). (Rigorously, the band is a frustum of a cone with lateral area \(2\pi\bar r\,\ell\), where \(\bar r\) is the average radius and \(\ell\) the slant length.)

Key formulas

\[ S = \int 2\pi r\,ds, \qquad ds = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}dx = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dx}{dy}\right)^2}dy. \]

About the \(x\)-axis, \(r = y\); about the \(y\)-axis, \(r = x\). Use whichever form of \(ds\) matches the variable you integrate in.

Common mistake

Using \(dx\) instead of \(ds\): \(\int 2\pi y\,dx\) is not the surface area. The band is tilted, and its width is the slant length \(ds\), which is longer than \(dx\). (Contrast with volume, where \(\pi y^2\,dx\) is correct because the error from the tilt is of higher order.)

Example 8.3 (sphere)

Derive the surface area of a sphere of radius \(R\).

Show solution

Revolve \(y = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2}\), \(-R \le x \le R\), about the \(x\)-axis. \(y' = -x/y\), so \(1 + (y')^2 = \frac{y^2 + x^2}{y^2} = \frac{R^2}{y^2}\), and \(y\,ds = y\cdot\frac{R}{y}dx = R\,dx\).

\[ S = \int_{-R}^{R} 2\pi R\,dx = 2\pi R\cdot 2R = 4\pi R^2. \]

Bonus: the area of a band between \(x = a\) and \(x = b\) is \(2\pi R(b - a)\), which depends only on the band's width (Archimedes' hat-box theorem).

Example 8.4

Find the area of the surface formed by revolving \(y = x^3\), \(0 \le x \le 1\), about the \(x\)-axis.

Show solution

\(y' = 3x^2\). Let \(u = 1 + 9x^4\), \(du = 36x^3\,dx\); \(x = 0\to u = 1\), \(x = 1 \to u = 10\).

\[ S = \int_0^1 2\pi x^3\sqrt{1 + 9x^4}\,dx = \frac{2\pi}{36}\int_1^{10}u^{1/2}\,du = \frac{\pi}{18}\cdot\frac23\big(10^{3/2} - 1\big) = \frac{\pi}{27}\big(10\sqrt{10} - 1\big) \approx 3.563. \]

Example 8.5 (Gabriel's horn)

Revolve \(y = 1/x\), \(x \ge 1\), about the \(x\)-axis. Show the volume is finite but the surface area is infinite.

Show solution

Volume: \(\pi\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx = \pi\) (a \(p\)-integral with \(p = 2\)).

Surface: \(S = \int_1^\infty \frac{2\pi}{x}\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{x^4}}\,dx\). Since \(\sqrt{1 + x^{-4}} \ge 1\), the integrand is at least \(2\pi/x\), whose integral diverges (\(p = 1\)). By comparison, \(S = \infty\).

8.3 Moments and centers of mass

The center of mass is the balance point: the weighted average of position, weighted by mass. For point masses \(m_i\) at \(x_i\), \(\bar x = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{\sum m_i}\). The numerator is the moment about the origin. For a flat plate (lamina) of uniform density, mass is proportional to area, so the center of mass depends only on shape. It is then called the centroid.

Key formulas: centroid of a plane region

Region between \(y = f(x)\) (top) and \(y = g(x)\) (bottom), \(a \le x \le b\), with area \(A = \int_a^b (f - g)\,dx\):

\[ \bar x = \frac{1}{A}\int_a^b x\big[f(x) - g(x)\big]\,dx, \qquad \bar y = \frac{1}{A}\int_a^b \tfrac12\Big(\big[f(x)\big]^2 - \big[g(x)\big]^2\Big)\,dx. \]

Why: a vertical strip has area \((f - g)\,dx\), its own centroid is at horizontal position \(x\) and at height \(\tfrac12(f + g)\). Its moment about the \(x\)-axis is \(\tfrac12(f + g)(f - g)\,dx = \tfrac12(f^2 - g^2)\,dx\).

Theorem (Pappus)

If a plane region of area \(A\) lies entirely on one side of a line in its plane and is revolved about that line, the volume generated is

\[ V = 2\pi\bar d\,A, \]

where \(\bar d\) is the distance from the centroid to the line (the distance the centroid travels is \(2\pi\bar d\)).

Study tip

Use symmetry first: if the region is symmetric about a line, the centroid lies on that line, and one of the two integrals is unnecessary. Also sanity-check that the centroid lies where your eye says the region "balances".

Example 8.6

Find the centroid of the region between \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\), and use Pappus to recheck Example 6.3.

Show solution

\(A = \int_0^1 (x - x^2)\,dx = \tfrac12 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac16\).

\[ \bar x = 6\int_0^1 x(x - x^2)\,dx = 6\left(\tfrac13 - \tfrac14\right) = \tfrac12, \qquad \bar y = 6\int_0^1 \tfrac12(x^2 - x^4)\,dx = 3\left(\tfrac13 - \tfrac15\right) = \tfrac25. \]

Pappus about the \(x\)-axis: \(V = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac25\cdot\tfrac16 = \tfrac{2\pi}{15}\). About \(y = -1\): \(\bar d = \tfrac25 + 1 = \tfrac75\), \(V = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac75\cdot\tfrac16 = \tfrac{7\pi}{15}\). Both match Example 6.3.

Example 8.7 (quarter disk and a torus)

(a) Find the centroid of the quarter disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le r^2\), \(x, y \ge 0\). (b) Use Pappus to find the volume of a torus made by revolving a disk of radius \(a\) about a line at distance \(R \gt a\) from its center.

Show solution

(a) By symmetry \(\bar x = \bar y\). With \(A = \pi r^2/4\), top \(f = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\), bottom \(g = 0\):

\[ \bar y = \frac{4}{\pi r^2}\int_0^r \tfrac12(r^2 - x^2)\,dx = \frac{4}{\pi r^2}\cdot\tfrac12\left(r^3 - \tfrac{r^3}{3}\right) = \frac{4}{\pi r^2}\cdot\frac{r^3}{3} = \frac{4r}{3\pi}. \]

(b) The disk's centroid is its center, at distance \(R\): \(V = 2\pi R\cdot\pi a^2 = 2\pi^2 Ra^2\).

8.4 Hydrostatic force

Pressure in a fluid at rest at depth \(d\) is \(P = \rho g d\) (ignoring atmospheric pressure), and it acts equally in all directions. On a horizontal plate, the force is just \(PA\). On a vertical wall the depth changes, so slice the wall into horizontal strips, each at a single depth.

Key formula

Measure depth \(y\) downward from the surface. If the wall's width at depth \(y\) is \(w(y)\),

\[ F = \int_{y_1}^{y_2} \rho g\,y\,w(y)\,dy. \]

Shortcut: \(F = \rho g\,\bar y\,A\), where \(\bar y\) is the depth of the plate's centroid. (The integral is \(\rho g\) times the moment of the plate about the surface line.)

Example 8.8

A vertical plate is an isosceles triangle with its 4 m base along the water surface and its vertex 3 m below. Find the hydrostatic force on one side (\(\rho g = 9800\ \text{N/m}^3\)).

Show solution

By similar triangles, the width at depth \(y\) is \(w(y) = 4\left(1 - \frac{y}{3}\right)\).

\[ F = \int_0^3 9800\,y\left(4 - \tfrac{4}{3}y\right)dy = 9800\left[2y^2 - \tfrac49 y^3\right]_0^3 = 9800(18 - 12) = 58{,}800\ \text{N}. \]

Check: \(A = 6\ \text{m}^2\); the centroid of a triangle is one-third of the height from the base, so \(\bar y = 1\) m; \(F = 9800\cdot 1\cdot 6 = 58{,}800\) N. \(\checkmark\)

Practice problems

  1. Find the arc length of \(y = \cosh x\) on \([-1, 1]\).
    Answer

    \(1 + \sinh^2 x = \cosh^2 x\), so \(L = \int_{-1}^1\cosh x\,dx = 2\sinh 1 = e - e^{-1} \approx 2.350\).

  2. Find the arc length of \(y = \ln(\cos x)\) for \(0 \le x \le \pi/4\).
    Answer

    \(y' = -\tan x\), \(\sqrt{1 + \tan^2 x} = \sec x\). \(L = \big[\ln|\sec x + \tan x|\big]_0^{\pi/4} = \ln(\sqrt2 + 1) \approx 0.881\).

  3. Find the area of the surface formed by revolving \(y = \sqrt x\), \(0 \le x \le 2\), about the \(x\)-axis.
    Answer

    \(y\sqrt{1 + (y')^2} = \sqrt x\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{4x}} = \sqrt{x + \tfrac14}\). \(S = 2\pi\int_0^2\sqrt{x + \tfrac14}\,dx = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\left[\left(\tfrac94\right)^{3/2} - \left(\tfrac14\right)^{3/2}\right] = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\cdot\tfrac{26}{8} = \tfrac{13\pi}{3}\).

  4. Find the centroid of the region between \(y = 4 - x^2\) and the \(x\)-axis.
    Answer

    \(\bar x = 0\) by symmetry. \(A = \tfrac{32}{3}\). \(\int_{-2}^2\tfrac12(4 - x^2)^2dx = \tfrac12\left(64 - \tfrac{128}{3} + \tfrac{64}{5}\right) = \tfrac{256}{15}\). \(\bar y = \tfrac{256/15}{32/3} = \tfrac85\).

  5. Use Pappus to find the volume generated by revolving the disk \((x - 3)^2 + y^2 \le 1\) about the \(y\)-axis.
    Answer

  6. A vertical rectangular gate 2 m wide and 1.5 m tall has its top edge 3 m below the water surface. Find the force on it.
    Answer

9. Differential Equations

Stewart Ch. 9 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 4

Physical laws usually say how a quantity changes, not what it is: the rate of cooling is proportional to the temperature difference, the voltage across an inductor is proportional to the rate of change of current, a population grows at a rate proportional to its size. Such laws are differential equations. Solving one means finding the function whose rate of change obeys the law. This chapter gives three complementary views: geometric (direction fields show solution shapes without solving anything), numerical (Euler's method steps along the field), and analytic (separable and linear equations solved exactly).

9.1 Modeling and terminology

Definitions

  • A differential equation (DE) relates an unknown function to its derivatives. Its order is the highest derivative that appears.
  • A solution is a function that makes the equation true on some interval. The general solution contains arbitrary constants (for a first-order equation, one constant).
  • An initial-value problem (IVP) pairs a DE with a condition such as \(y(t_0) = y_0\), which picks out one particular solution.
  • An equilibrium solution is a constant solution: for \(y' = f(y)\), any \(y = c\) with \(f(c) = 0\).

Standard first-order models

  • Natural growth/decay: \(\dfrac{dP}{dt} = kP\).
  • Logistic growth with carrying capacity \(M\): \(\dfrac{dP}{dt} = kP\left(1 - \dfrac{P}{M}\right)\).
  • Newton's law of cooling: \(\dfrac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - T_s)\), with \(T_s\) the surrounding temperature.
  • RL circuit: \(L\dfrac{dI}{dt} + RI = E(t)\); RC circuit: \(R\dfrac{dq}{dt} + \dfrac{q}{C} = E(t)\).
  • Mixing: \(\dfrac{dS}{dt} = (\text{rate in}) - (\text{rate out})\), where rate out = (concentration in tank) \(\times\) (outflow rate).

Example 9.1 (verifying a solution)

Show that \(y = \dfrac{1}{x + C}\) solves \(y' = -y^2\), and find the solution with \(y(0) = \tfrac12\).

Show solution

\(y' = -\frac{1}{(x + C)^2} = -y^2\). \(\checkmark\) Then \(y(0) = \frac{1}{C} = \frac12\) gives \(C = 2\), so \(y = \frac{1}{x + 2}\) (valid on \(x \gt -2\), the interval containing \(x = 0\)). Note that \(y = 0\) is also a solution (the equilibrium), which the formula misses for every finite \(C\).

9.2 Direction fields

The equation \(y' = F(x, y)\) tells you the slope of the solution curve through any point \((x, y)\), without solving anything. Draw a short segment of that slope at each point of a grid: this is the direction field (slope field). Solution curves are the curves that are tangent to the segments everywhere, like streamlines in a fluid.

Definitions

An isocline is a curve \(F(x, y) = c\) along which all the slopes equal \(c\). For an autonomous equation \(y' = f(y)\) (no explicit \(x\)), the slope depends only on height, so the field is the same along every horizontal line; an equilibrium \(y = c\) is stable if nearby solutions approach it and unstable if they move away.

Study tip: phase line

For \(y' = f(y)\), mark the zeros of \(f\) on a vertical line and the sign of \(f\) between them (up arrows where \(f \gt 0\), down where \(f \lt 0\)). An equilibrium with arrows pointing toward it from both sides is stable. This takes a minute and gives the long-run behaviour of every solution.

Example 9.2

Describe the direction field of \(y' = x - y\) and find a straight-line solution.

Show solution

Isoclines are lines \(x - y = c\), i.e. \(y = x - c\). On the line \(y = x\) the slopes are 0; on \(y = x - 1\) they are 1. A line of slope 1 on which the field also has slope 1 must be a solution: try \(y = x - 1\). Then \(y' = 1\) and \(x - y = 1\). \(\checkmark\)

Above that line \(x - y \lt 1\), so solutions are less steep than the line and fall toward it; below, they are steeper and rise toward it. All solutions approach \(y = x - 1\) as \(x\) increases. (The general solution, found in 9.6, is \(y = x - 1 + Ce^{-x}\).)

9.3 Euler's method

Follow the direction field in small straight steps: from the current point, move a step \(h\) to the right along the tangent line, then recompute the slope.

Key formula: Euler's method for \(y' = F(x, y)\), \(y(x_0) = y_0\)

\[ x_{n+1} = x_n + h, \qquad y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,F(x_n, y_n). \]

The error at a fixed endpoint is roughly proportional to \(h\) (Euler is a first-order method): halving the step size roughly halves the error.

Common mistake

Evaluating the slope at the new point instead of the current one, or forgetting to update \(x_n\) inside \(F(x_n, y_n)\).

Example 9.3

Use Euler's method with \(h = 0.1\) to estimate \(y(0.3)\) for \(y' = x + y\), \(y(0) = 1\). Compare with the exact solution \(y = 2e^x - x - 1\).

Show solution \[ \begin{aligned} y_1 &= 1 + 0.1(0 + 1) = 1.1 \\ y_2 &= 1.1 + 0.1(0.1 + 1.1) = 1.22 \\ y_3 &= 1.22 + 0.1(0.2 + 1.22) = 1.362 \end{aligned} \]

Exact: \(y(0.3) = 2e^{0.3} - 1.3 \approx 1.3997\). (Check: \(y' = 2e^x - 1 = (2e^x - x - 1) + x = y + x\), \(y(0) = 1\).) The error is about \(0.038\). Euler underestimates here because the solution is concave up, so each tangent step lands below the curve.

9.4 Separable equations

Definition and method

A first-order DE is separable if it can be written \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = g(x)\,h(y)\). For \(h(y) \ne 0\):

\[ \int\frac{dy}{h(y)} = \int g(x)\,dx + C. \]

Why it works: if \(y(x)\) is a solution, then \(\frac{1}{h(y(x))}y'(x) = g(x)\). Integrate both sides in \(x\); by the substitution rule, the left side is \(\int\frac{dy}{h(y)}\). "Multiplying by \(dx\)" is just shorthand for this.

Common mistake

Dividing by \(h(y)\) discards any constant solutions \(y = c\) with \(h(c) = 0\). Check for them separately. Also, add the constant \(C\) as soon as you integrate, before solving for \(y\): \(\ln|y| = x^2 + C\) gives \(y = Ae^{x^2}\), not \(e^{x^2} + C\).

Example 9.4

Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = xy^2\), \(y(0) = 1\). On what interval is the solution valid?

Show solution \[ \int y^{-2}\,dy = \int x\,dx \;\Longrightarrow\; -\frac1y = \frac{x^2}{2} + C. \]

\(y(0) = 1\): \(-1 = C\). So \(\frac1y = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2}\), giving \(y = \dfrac{2}{2 - x^2}\).

The solution blows up at \(x = \pm\sqrt2\), so it is valid on \(-\sqrt2 \lt x \lt \sqrt2\). Nonlinear equations can have solutions that escape to infinity in finite time even though the equation itself is perfectly smooth.

Example 9.5 (mixing)

A tank holds 200 L of pure water. Brine containing 0.5 kg of salt per litre flows in at 4 L/min; the well-stirred mixture drains at 4 L/min. How much salt is in the tank after one hour, and in the long run?

Show solution

Let \(S(t)\) be the salt (kg). Rate in \(= 0.5 \times 4 = 2\) kg/min. The volume stays 200 L, so rate out \(= \frac{S}{200}\times 4 = \frac{S}{50}\).

\[ \frac{dS}{dt} = 2 - \frac{S}{50} = \frac{100 - S}{50}, \qquad S(0) = 0. \]

Separate: \(\int\frac{dS}{100 - S} = \int\frac{dt}{50}\), so \(-\ln|100 - S| = \frac{t}{50} + C\), i.e. \(100 - S = Ae^{-t/50}\). At \(t = 0\), \(A = 100\):

\[ S(t) = 100\left(1 - e^{-t/50}\right), \qquad S(60) = 100(1 - e^{-1.2}) \approx 69.9\ \text{kg}. \]

As \(t\to\infty\), \(S\to 100\) kg, the amount when the whole tank has the inflow concentration (\(0.5 \times 200\)).

9.5 Exponential and logistic growth

Theorem (natural growth)

The only solutions of \(\dfrac{dy}{dt} = ky\) are \(y(t) = y(0)e^{kt}\). For \(k \gt 0\) the doubling time is \(\frac{\ln 2}{k}\); for \(k \lt 0\) the half-life is \(\frac{\ln 2}{|k|}\).

Example 9.6 (radiocarbon dating)

Carbon-14 has half-life about 5730 years. A sample has 30% of its original carbon-14. Estimate its age.

Show solution

\(k = -\frac{\ln 2}{5730}\). Solve \(0.3 = e^{kt}\):

\[ t = \frac{\ln 0.3}{k} = \frac{5730\,\ln(1/0.3)}{\ln 2} = 5730\times\frac{1.20397}{0.69315} \approx 9950\ \text{years}. \]

Pure exponential growth cannot continue forever. The logistic model makes the relative growth rate \(\frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dt}\) decrease linearly from \(k\) (small population) to 0 (at the carrying capacity \(M\)).

Theorem (logistic equation)

The solution of \(\dfrac{dP}{dt} = kP\left(1 - \dfrac{P}{M}\right)\), \(P(0) = P_0 \gt 0\), is

\[ P(t) = \frac{M}{1 + Ae^{-kt}}, \qquad A = \frac{M - P_0}{P_0}. \]

Equilibria: \(P = 0\) (unstable) and \(P = M\) (stable). Every positive solution tends to \(M\). The growth rate \(P'\) is largest when \(P = M/2\), where the graph has its inflection point.

Derivation. Separate and use partial fractions: \(\frac{M}{P(M - P)} = \frac1P + \frac{1}{M - P}\). So \(\int\left(\frac1P + \frac{1}{M - P}\right)dP = \int k\,dt\) gives \(\ln\left|\frac{P}{M - P}\right| = kt + C\), hence \(\frac{M - P}{P} = Ae^{-kt}\). Solve for \(P\). The maximum of \(P' = kP - \frac{k}{M}P^2\), a downward parabola in \(P\), is at \(P = M/2\).

Example 9.7

A population satisfies \(P' = 0.5P(1 - P/1000)\) with \(P(0) = 100\). Find \(P(t)\) and the time when the population is growing fastest.

Show solution

\(A = \frac{1000 - 100}{100} = 9\), so \(P(t) = \dfrac{1000}{1 + 9e^{-0.5t}}\).

Fastest growth when \(P = 500\): \(1 + 9e^{-0.5t} = 2 \Rightarrow e^{-0.5t} = \tfrac19 \Rightarrow t = 2\ln 9 = 4\ln 3 \approx 4.39\).

9.6 First-order linear equations

Theorem (integrating factor)

A first-order linear equation has the standard form

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)\,y = Q(x), \]

with \(P, Q\) continuous on an interval. Multiply by the integrating factor \(I(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,dx}\). Because \(I' = PI\), the left side becomes an exact derivative:

\[ \big(I(x)\,y\big)' = I(x)\,Q(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y = \frac{1}{I(x)}\left[\int I(x)Q(x)\,dx + C\right]. \]

Proof. \((Iy)' = Iy' + I'y = Iy' + PIy = I(y' + Py) = IQ\). Integrate both sides and divide by \(I \gt 0\).

Common mistake

Computing \(P(x)\) before putting the equation in standard form. For \(xy' + 2y = 4x^2\), divide by \(x\) first: \(P = 2/x\), not \(2\). And the constant \(C\) must be added before dividing by \(I(x)\), so it becomes \(C/I(x)\), not \(C\).

Example 9.8

Solve \(xy' + 2y = 4x^2\), \(y(1) = 2\), for \(x \gt 0\).

Show solution

Standard form: \(y' + \frac{2}{x}y = 4x\). \(I = e^{\int 2/x\,dx} = e^{2\ln x} = x^2\).

\[ (x^2y)' = 4x^3 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2y = x^4 + C \;\Longrightarrow\; y = x^2 + \frac{C}{x^2}. \]

\(y(1) = 1 + C = 2 \Rightarrow C = 1\). So \(y = x^2 + x^{-2}\).

Example 9.9 (RL circuit)

A circuit has inductance \(L = 2\) H, resistance \(R = 10\ \Omega\) and a constant 40 V source switched on at \(t = 0\) with \(I(0) = 0\). Find \(I(t)\).

Show solution

\(2I' + 10I = 40\), so \(I' + 5I = 20\). The integrating factor is \(e^{\int 5\,dt} = e^{5t}\).

\[ (e^{5t}I)' = 20e^{5t} \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{5t}I = 4e^{5t} + C \;\Longrightarrow\; I = 4 + Ce^{-5t}. \]

\(I(0) = 0 \Rightarrow C = -4\): \(I(t) = 4\left(1 - e^{-5t}\right)\) A. The steady state is \(E/R = 4\) A and the time constant is \(L/R = 0.2\) s.

Study tip: structure of the solution

The general solution of a linear equation is always (one particular solution) + \(C\times\)(solution of the homogeneous equation \(y' + Py = 0\)). In circuits these are the steady-state and transient responses. This structure carries over unchanged to the higher-order linear ODEs you meet later.

Practice problems

  1. Solve \(y' = y\cos x\), \(y(0) = 3\).
    Answer

    Separable: \(\ln|y| = \sin x + C\), so \(y = Ae^{\sin x}\); \(A = 3\). \(y = 3e^{\sin x}\).

  2. Use Euler's method with \(h = 0.5\) to estimate \(y(1)\) for \(y' = y\), \(y(0) = 1\). Compare with the exact value.
    Answer

    \(y_1 = 1 + 0.5(1) = 1.5\), \(y_2 = 1.5 + 0.5(1.5) = 2.25\). Exact \(e \approx 2.718\); error \(\approx 0.47\). (Euler gives \((1 + h)^{1/h}\), which tends to \(e\) as \(h\to 0\).)

  3. Find the equilibria of \(y' = y(y - 2)(y - 4)\) and classify each as stable or unstable.
    Answer

    Signs of \(f\): negative for \(y \lt 0\), positive on \((0,2)\), negative on \((2,4)\), positive for \(y \gt 4\). So \(y = 0\) unstable, \(y = 2\) stable, \(y = 4\) unstable.

  4. Coffee at 90°C sits in a 20°C room and cools to 70°C in 5 minutes. When does it reach 50°C?
    Answer

    \(T - 20 = 70e^{-kt}\). \(50 = 70e^{-5k} \Rightarrow k = \tfrac15\ln\tfrac75 \approx 0.06729\). \(30 = 70e^{-kt} \Rightarrow t = \ln(7/3)/k \approx 12.6\) min.

  5. Solve \(y' - y = e^{2x}\).
    Answer

    \(I = e^{-x}\): \((e^{-x}y)' = e^{x}\), so \(e^{-x}y = e^x + C\), \(y = e^{2x} + Ce^{x}\).

  6. For \(P' = 0.2P(1 - P/500)\), \(P(0) = 50\): find \(P(t)\), \(\lim_{t\to\infty}P\), and when growth is fastest.
    Answer

    \(A = 450/50 = 9\): \(P = \frac{500}{1 + 9e^{-0.2t}}\). Limit 500. Fastest at \(P = 250\): \(t = 5\ln 9 \approx 11.0\).

10. Parametric Equations and Polar Coordinates

Stewart Ch. 10 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 7

Not every curve is the graph of a function \(y = f(x)\). A circle fails the vertical line test; the path of a particle may loop back on itself. Parametric equations describe a curve by giving both coordinates as functions of a third variable, usually time: this records not just the shape but how it is traced. Polar coordinates describe a point by its distance from the origin and its direction, which is natural for anything with rotational symmetry: orbits, antenna radiation patterns, spirals. The calculus is the same as before (slopes, areas, lengths), rewritten for the new description.

10.1 Parametric curves

Definition

A parametric curve is the set of points \((x, y) = \big(f(t), g(t)\big)\) as the parameter \(t\) ranges over an interval \(I\). The direction of increasing \(t\) gives the curve's orientation. Different parametrizations can trace the same set of points at different speeds or in different directions.

Study tip: eliminating the parameter

Solve one equation for \(t\) and substitute, or use an identity. For \(x = a\cos t\), \(y = b\sin t\): \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1\). Always note the restrictions the parametrization imposes: \(x = t^2\) forces \(x \ge 0\).

Example 10.1

Describe the curves (a) \(x = 2\cos t\), \(y = 3\sin t\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\); (b) the cycloid \(x = r(\theta - \sin\theta)\), \(y = r(1 - \cos\theta)\).

Show solution

(a) \(\left(\frac x2\right)^2 + \left(\frac y3\right)^2 = 1\): an ellipse with semi-axes 2 (horizontal) and 3 (vertical), traced once counterclockwise starting at \((2, 0)\).

(b) The cycloid is the path of a point on the rim of a wheel of radius \(r\) rolling along the \(x\)-axis. After rolling through angle \(\theta\), the centre is at \((r\theta, r)\), and the point sits at offset \((-r\sin\theta, -r\cos\theta)\) from the centre. It touches the axis (with a cusp) at \(\theta = 2\pi n\) and reaches height \(2r\) at \(\theta = \pi\).

10.2 Calculus with parametric curves

Key formulas (\(x = f(t)\), \(y = g(t)\))

Slope (chain rule, \(\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx}\frac{dx}{dt}\)), valid where \(dx/dt \ne 0\):

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}, \qquad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt}. \]

Horizontal tangent: \(dy/dt = 0\), \(dx/dt \ne 0\). Vertical tangent: \(dx/dt = 0\), \(dy/dt \ne 0\).

Area under the curve (traced once, left to right as \(t\) goes from \(\alpha\) to \(\beta\)):

\[ A = \int y\,dx = \int_\alpha^\beta g(t)\,f'(t)\,dt. \]

Arc length (curve traced exactly once, \(f', g'\) continuous):

\[ L = \int_\alpha^\beta\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt. \]

Surface area about the \(x\)-axis (\(y \ge 0\)): \(S = \int_\alpha^\beta 2\pi y\,\sqrt{(dx/dt)^2 + (dy/dt)^2}\,dt\).

Common mistake

The second derivative is not \(\frac{d^2y/dt^2}{d^2x/dt^2}\). Differentiate the expression for \(dy/dx\) with respect to \(t\), then divide by \(dx/dt\) once more.

Example 10.2

For \(x = t^2 - 1\), \(y = t^3 - 3t\): find the points with horizontal and vertical tangents, the tangent line at \(t = 2\), and the concavity there.

Show solution

\(\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t\), \(\frac{dy}{dt} = 3t^2 - 3\), so \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3(t^2 - 1)}{2t}\).

Horizontal: \(t = \pm 1\), at points \((0, -2)\) (\(t = 1\)) and \((0, 2)\) (\(t = -1\)). Vertical: \(t = 0\), at \((-1, 0)\).

At \(t = 2\): point \((3, 2)\), slope \(\frac{3\cdot 3}{4} = \frac94\). Tangent: \(y - 2 = \frac94(x - 3)\).

Concavity: \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac32 t - \frac32 t^{-1}\), so \(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac32 + \frac{3}{2t^2}\). Divide by \(2t\):

\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{3(t^2 + 1)}{4t^3}; \quad\text{at } t = 2: \ \frac{15}{32} \gt 0, \text{ concave up.} \]

(The curve crosses itself at \((2, 0)\), reached at both \(t = \sqrt3\) and \(t = -\sqrt3\), with two different tangent lines there.)

Example 10.3 (one arch of a cycloid)

Find the length of one arch of the cycloid \(x = r(\theta - \sin\theta)\), \(y = r(1 - \cos\theta)\), and the area under it.

Show solution

\(x' = r(1 - \cos\theta)\), \(y' = r\sin\theta\). Then

\[ (x')^2 + (y')^2 = r^2(1 - 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = 2r^2(1 - \cos\theta) = 4r^2\sin^2\tfrac{\theta}{2}. \]

On \([0, 2\pi]\), \(\sin(\theta/2) \ge 0\), so

\[ L = \int_0^{2\pi}2r\sin\tfrac\theta2\,d\theta = 2r\Big[-2\cos\tfrac\theta2\Big]_0^{2\pi} = 2r(2 + 2) = 8r. \]

Area: \(A = \int_0^{2\pi} y\,x'\,d\theta = r^2\int_0^{2\pi}(1 - \cos\theta)^2\,d\theta = r^2\int_0^{2\pi}(1 - 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta)\,d\theta = r^2(2\pi - 0 + \pi) = 3\pi r^2\), three times the area of the rolling wheel.

Physics connection

For a particle at \((x(t), y(t))\), the integrand \(\sqrt{x'^2 + y'^2}\) is its speed, so the arc length formula says distance = \(\int\) speed \(dt\). A projectile launched at speed \(v_0\) and angle \(\alpha\) follows \(x = v_0 t\cos\alpha\), \(y = v_0 t\sin\alpha - \tfrac12 gt^2\); eliminating \(t\) shows the path is a parabola.

10.3 Polar coordinates and polar curves

Definition

The point with polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\) is at distance \(|r|\) from the origin (the pole), in direction \(\theta\) measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis. If \(r \lt 0\), the point is in the opposite direction: \((r, \theta)\) and \((-r, \theta + \pi)\) are the same point. Every point has infinitely many polar representations.

\[ x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta; \qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2,\quad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}. \]

Common mistake

\(\theta = \arctan(y/x)\) only gives angles in \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\). For a point in quadrant II or III, add \(\pi\). Example: \((-1, \sqrt3)\) has \(r = 2\) and \(\theta = 2\pi/3\), not \(\arctan(-\sqrt3) = -\pi/3\).

Common polar curves

  • \(r = a\): circle of radius \(a\) centred at the pole. \(\theta = \alpha\): line through the pole.
  • \(r = 2a\cos\theta\) (or \(2a\sin\theta\)): circle of radius \(a\) through the pole, centred at \((a, 0)\) (or \((0, a)\)).
  • \(r = a(1 \pm\cos\theta)\): cardioid. \(r = a + b\cos\theta\): limaçon (inner loop if \(|b| \gt |a|\)).
  • \(r = a\cos n\theta\) or \(a\sin n\theta\): rose with \(n\) petals if \(n\) is odd, \(2n\) petals if \(n\) is even.
  • \(r = a\theta\): Archimedean spiral.

Key formula: tangents to polar curves

Treat \(r = f(\theta)\) as the parametric curve \(x = f(\theta)\cos\theta\), \(y = f(\theta)\sin\theta\):

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/d\theta}{dx/d\theta} = \frac{\frac{dr}{d\theta}\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{\frac{dr}{d\theta}\cos\theta - r\sin\theta}. \]

At the pole (\(r = 0\)) with \(dr/d\theta \ne 0\), this reduces to \(\tan\theta\): the curve leaves the pole in the direction \(\theta\).

Example 10.4

(a) Convert \(r = 4\sin\theta\) to Cartesian form. (b) For the cardioid \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\), find the slope at \(\theta = \pi/2\) and the points with horizontal tangents.

Show solution

(a) Multiply by \(r\): \(r^2 = 4r\sin\theta \Rightarrow x^2 + y^2 = 4y \Rightarrow x^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 4\). A circle of radius 2 centred at \((0, 2)\).

(b) \(r' = -\sin\theta\). At \(\theta = \pi/2\): \(r = 1\), \(r' = -1\), so

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{(-1)(1) + (1)(0)}{(-1)(0) - (1)(1)} = \frac{-1}{-1} = 1. \]

Horizontal tangents: \(\frac{dy}{d\theta} = r'\sin\theta + r\cos\theta = -\sin^2\theta + \cos\theta + \cos^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta + \cos\theta - 1 = (2\cos\theta - 1)(\cos\theta + 1)\).

\(\cos\theta = \tfrac12\): \(\theta = \pm\pi/3\), \(r = \tfrac32\), points \(\left(\tfrac34, \pm\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{4}\right)\). At these, \(\frac{dx}{d\theta} = -\sin\theta(1 + 2\cos\theta) \ne 0\), so they are genuine horizontal tangents. \(\cos\theta = -1\) (\(\theta = \pi\)) gives the pole, where \(dx/d\theta = 0\) too: that is the cusp.

10.4 Areas and lengths in polar coordinates

The natural slice in polar coordinates is a thin sector, not a rectangle. A sector of radius \(r\) and angle \(d\theta\) has area \(\tfrac12 r^2\,d\theta\) (a fraction \(d\theta/2\pi\) of \(\pi r^2\)).

Key formulas

\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12 r^2\,d\theta, \qquad A_{\text{between}} = \int_a^b\tfrac12\big(r_{\text{out}}^2 - r_{\text{in}}^2\big)\,d\theta, \] \[ L = \int_a^b\sqrt{r^2 + \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\,d\theta. \]

The length formula comes from the parametric one: with \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\), one finds \((dx/d\theta)^2 + (dy/d\theta)^2 = r^2 + (dr/d\theta)^2\).

Common mistake

Choosing \(\theta\)-limits that trace part of the region twice (e.g. integrating \(r = \cos 3\theta\) over \([0, 2\pi]\) traces the rose twice). Also, setting \(r_1(\theta) = r_2(\theta)\) can miss intersection points: two curves may pass through the pole at different \(\theta\) values, or meet at a point with different representations. Sketch the curves.

Example 10.5

Find (a) the area inside the cardioid \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\) and (b) its length.

Show solution

(a)

\[ A = \tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}(1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta)\,d\theta = \tfrac12\left(2\pi + 0 + \pi\right) = \frac{3\pi}{2}. \]

(b) \(r^2 + r'^2 = 1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 2 + 2\cos\theta = 4\cos^2\tfrac\theta2\). Using symmetry (\(\cos\tfrac\theta2 \ge 0\) on \([0,\pi]\)):

\[ L = 2\int_0^\pi 2\cos\tfrac\theta2\,d\theta = 4\Big[2\sin\tfrac\theta2\Big]_0^\pi = 8. \]

Example 10.6

Find the area of one petal of \(r = \cos 3\theta\).

Show solution

A petal runs between consecutive zeros of \(r\): \(\cos 3\theta = 0\) at \(\theta = \pm\pi/6\).

\[ A = \tfrac12\int_{-\pi/6}^{\pi/6}\cos^2 3\theta\,d\theta = \tfrac14\int_{-\pi/6}^{\pi/6}(1 + \cos 6\theta)\,d\theta = \tfrac14\cdot\tfrac\pi3 = \frac{\pi}{12}. \]

The full three-petal rose has area \(\pi/4\).

Example 10.7 (area between polar curves)

Find the area inside \(r = 3\cos\theta\) and outside \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\).

Show solution

Intersections: \(3\cos\theta = 1 + \cos\theta \Rightarrow \cos\theta = \tfrac12 \Rightarrow \theta = \pm\pi/3\). Between these the circle is outside. Use symmetry about the \(x\)-axis:

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= 2\cdot\tfrac12\int_0^{\pi/3}\big[9\cos^2\theta - (1 + \cos\theta)^2\big]\,d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/3}(8\cos^2\theta - 2\cos\theta - 1)\,d\theta \\ &= \int_0^{\pi/3}(3 + 4\cos 2\theta - 2\cos\theta)\,d\theta = \Big[3\theta + 2\sin 2\theta - 2\sin\theta\Big]_0^{\pi/3} \\ &= \pi + \sqrt3 - \sqrt3 = \pi. \end{aligned} \]

Theorem (focus-directrix form)

Fix a point \(F\) (focus), a line \(\ell\) (directrix) and \(e \gt 0\) (eccentricity). The set of points \(P\) with \(\frac{|PF|}{\text{dist}(P, \ell)} = e\) is an ellipse if \(e \lt 1\), a parabola if \(e = 1\), a hyperbola if \(e \gt 1\). With the focus at the pole and directrix \(x = d\) (or \(x = -d\)), its polar equation is

\[ r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\cos\theta} \quad\left(\text{or } r = \frac{ed}{1 - e\cos\theta}\right), \]

with \(\sin\theta\) in place of \(\cos\theta\) for a horizontal directrix \(y = \pm d\).

Physics connection

Under an inverse-square force, orbits are conics with the attracting body at a focus. Planetary orbits are ellipses with small \(e\) (Earth's is about 0.017); the polar form above, with the Sun at the pole, is exactly how orbital mechanics writes them. Perihelion is \(r_{\min} = \frac{ed}{1 + e}\) and aphelion \(r_{\max} = \frac{ed}{1 - e}\).

Example 10.8

Identify the conic \(r = \dfrac{10}{3 + 2\cos\theta}\), find its vertices, and find \(a\), \(b\), \(c\).

Show solution

Divide by 3: \(r = \dfrac{10/3}{1 + \frac23\cos\theta}\). So \(e = \tfrac23 \lt 1\): an ellipse, with \(ed = \tfrac{10}{3}\), \(d = 5\).

Vertices: \(\theta = 0\): \(r = 10/5 = 2\), point \((2, 0)\). \(\theta = \pi\): \(r = 10/1 = 10\), point \((-10, 0)\).

Major axis length \(2a = 12\), so \(a = 6\); centre \((-4, 0)\); the focus at the origin is \(c = 4\) from the centre. Check: \(e = c/a = 4/6 = 2/3\). \(\checkmark\) Then \(b^2 = a^2 - c^2 = 20\), \(b = 2\sqrt5\).

Practice problems

  1. Eliminate the parameter: \(x = e^t\), \(y = e^{2t} + 1\).
    Answer

    \(y = x^2 + 1\) with \(x \gt 0\) (the right half of the parabola), traced left to right.

  2. Find the length of \(x = t^2\), \(y = \tfrac23 t^3\), \(0 \le t \le \sqrt3\).
    Answer

    \(\sqrt{4t^2 + 4t^4} = 2t\sqrt{1 + t^2}\). \(L = \tfrac23\big[(1 + t^2)^{3/2}\big]_0^{\sqrt3} = \tfrac23(8 - 1) = \tfrac{14}{3}\).

  3. Write \(x^2 + y^2 = 6x\) in polar form.
    Answer

    \(r^2 = 6r\cos\theta \Rightarrow r = 6\cos\theta\): a circle of radius 3 centred at \((3, 0)\).

  4. Find the total area enclosed by the four-petal rose \(r = 2\sin 2\theta\).
    Answer

    \(\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}4\sin^2 2\theta\,d\theta = 2\cdot\pi = 2\pi\). (Over \([0, 2\pi]\) each of the four petals is traced exactly once.)

  5. Find the slope of the tangent to the spiral \(r = \theta\) at \(\theta = \pi/2\).
    Answer

11. Sequences, Series and Power Series

Stewart Ch. 11 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 5–6

Can you add infinitely many numbers and get a finite answer? Sometimes: \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \cdots = 1\), because the partial sums creep up to 1. Sometimes not: \(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \cdots\) grows without bound, even though its terms shrink to zero. The first half of this chapter builds tests that decide which case you are in without computing the sum. The second half uses the payoff: a power series is an "infinite polynomial", and many functions (\(e^x\), \(\sin x\), \(\ln(1 + x)\)) are power series. Truncating gives a Taylor polynomial, which is how calculators compute these functions and how physicists justify approximations like \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\).

11.1 Sequences

Definition (limit of a sequence)

A sequence \(\{a_n\}\) converges to \(L\), written \(\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n = L\), if for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) there is an integer \(N\) such that \(|a_n - L| \lt \varepsilon\) whenever \(n \gt N\). Otherwise the sequence diverges. (If \(a_n\) grows without bound we write \(a_n\to\infty\), but the sequence still diverges.)

Theorems for computing limits

  • Function link: if \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x) = L\) and \(a_n = f(n)\), then \(a_n\to L\). (So L'Hôpital's rule can be used on \(f\).)
  • Limit laws (sum, product, quotient) and the Squeeze Theorem hold as for functions. In particular, if \(|a_n|\to 0\) then \(a_n\to 0\).
  • Continuity: if \(a_n\to L\) and \(f\) is continuous at \(L\), then \(f(a_n)\to f(L)\).
  • Geometric: \(r^n\) converges if \(-1 \lt r \le 1\) (to 0 if \(|r| \lt 1\), to 1 if \(r = 1\)) and diverges otherwise.
  • Monotone Sequence Theorem: every bounded, monotonic sequence converges. (This is a form of the completeness of the real numbers.)

Useful limits (as \(n\to\infty\))

\[ \frac{\ln n}{n}\to 0,\qquad \sqrt[n]{n}\to 1,\qquad \left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)^n\to e^x,\qquad \frac{x^n}{n!}\to 0\ \text{(any } x),\qquad \frac{n!}{n^n}\to 0. \]

Growth order for large \(n\): \(\ln n \ll n^p \ll b^n \ll n! \ll n^n\) (for \(p \gt 0\), \(b \gt 1\)).

Example 11.1

Find the limits: (a) \(\dfrac{3n^2 - n}{5n^2 + 2}\); (b) \(\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n}\); (c) \(n\sin\dfrac1n\).

Show solution

(a) Divide top and bottom by \(n^2\): \(\frac{3 - 1/n}{5 + 2/n^2}\to\frac35\).

(b) \(\left|\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\right| = \frac1n\to 0\), so the sequence \(\to 0\) by the squeeze theorem.

(c) Let \(x = 1/n \to 0^+\): \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\to 1\).

Example 11.2 (recursive sequence)

Let \(a_1 = 1\) and \(a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n}\). Show the sequence converges and find its limit.

Show solution

Bounded above by 2 (induction): \(a_1 = 1 \lt 2\); if \(a_n \lt 2\) then \(a_{n+1} \lt \sqrt{4} = 2\).

Increasing: \(a_{n+1} \gt a_n \iff 2 + a_n \gt a_n^2 \iff (a_n - 2)(a_n + 1) \lt 0\), which holds since \(0 \lt a_n \lt 2\).

By the Monotone Sequence Theorem, \(a_n\to L\). Take limits in the recursion: \(L = \sqrt{2 + L}\), so \(L^2 - L - 2 = 0\), \(L = 2\) or \(L = -1\). Since all terms are positive, \(L = 2\).

Common mistake

Solving \(L = g(L)\) before proving the limit exists. The algebra only tells you what the limit must be if there is one. For example \(a_{n+1} = -a_n\) with \(a_1 = 1\) gives "\(L = -L\), so \(L = 0\)", but the sequence \(1, -1, 1, \ldots\) diverges.

11.2 Series

Definition

Given \(\{a_n\}\), the partial sums are \(s_n = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n\). The series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\) converges with sum \(s\) if \(s_n\to s\); otherwise it diverges. A series is a sequence of partial sums in disguise.

Theorem (Geometric series)

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty ar^{n-1} = a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \frac{a}{1 - r}\quad\text{if } |r| \lt 1, \]

and the series diverges if \(|r| \ge 1\) (with \(a \ne 0\)). In words: first term over (1 − ratio).

Proof. \(s_n - rs_n = a - ar^n\), so \(s_n = \frac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}\) for \(r \ne 1\). This converges iff \(r^n\) does, i.e. iff \(|r| \lt 1\) (the case \(r = -1\) oscillates; \(r = 1\) gives \(s_n = na\)).

Theorem (Test for Divergence)

If \(\sum a_n\) converges, then \(a_n\to 0\). Equivalently: if \(\lim a_n \ne 0\) or does not exist, then \(\sum a_n\) diverges.

Proof. \(a_n = s_n - s_{n-1}\to s - s = 0\).

Common mistake

The converse is false: \(a_n\to 0\) does not imply convergence. The harmonic series \(\sum\frac1n\) diverges although \(\frac1n\to 0\). Proof by grouping: \(1 + \tfrac12 + \left(\tfrac13 + \tfrac14\right) + \left(\tfrac15 + \cdots + \tfrac18\right) + \cdots\); each bracket is at least \(\tfrac12\), so \(s_{2^k} \ge 1 + \tfrac k2\to\infty\).

Convergent series can be added and multiplied by constants term by term: \(\sum(ca_n + b_n) = c\sum a_n + \sum b_n\). Changing or removing finitely many terms never affects convergence (only the sum). A telescoping series is one whose partial sums collapse after cancellation.

Example 11.3

Find the sums: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty 3\left(-\tfrac25\right)^n\); (b) \(0.\overline{27} = 0.272727\ldots\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n(n+1)}\).

Show solution

(a) Geometric with \(a = 3\), \(r = -\tfrac25\): sum \(= \frac{3}{1 + 2/5} = \frac{15}{7}\).

(b) \(\frac{27}{100} + \frac{27}{100^2} + \cdots = \frac{27/100}{1 - 1/100} = \frac{27}{99} = \frac{3}{11}\).

(c) Partial fractions: \(\frac{1}{n(n+1)} = \frac1n - \frac{1}{n+1}\). Then \(s_n = \left(1 - \tfrac12\right) + \left(\tfrac12 - \tfrac13\right) + \cdots + \left(\tfrac1n - \tfrac{1}{n+1}\right) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{n+1}\to 1\).

Example 11.4 (bouncing ball)

A ball dropped from 10 m rebounds each time to \(\tfrac34\) of its previous height. Find the total vertical distance travelled.

Show solution

Down 10, then each rebound goes up and down: \(2(7.5) + 2(7.5)(0.75) + \cdots\).

\[ D = 10 + 2\cdot\frac{7.5}{1 - 0.75} = 10 + 2(30) = 70\ \text{m}. \]

11.3 The integral test

Theorem (Integral Test)

Suppose \(f\) is continuous, positive and decreasing on \([1,\infty)\) and \(a_n = f(n)\). Then \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\) converges if and only if \(\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx\) converges. (It is enough for \(f\) to be decreasing for \(x \ge N\), starting the comparison there.)

Proof idea. Draw rectangles of width 1. Right-endpoint rectangles fit under the curve and left-endpoint rectangles cover it:

\[ a_2 + a_3 + \cdots + a_n \le \int_1^n f(x)\,dx \le a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_{n-1}. \]

If the integral is finite, the partial sums are bounded and increasing, so they converge (Monotone Sequence Theorem). If the integral is infinite, the partial sums exceed it and diverge.

Corollary (\(p\)-series)

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^p}\ \text{converges if } p \gt 1 \text{ and diverges if } p \le 1. \]

Remainder estimate

Under the integral-test hypotheses, the remainder \(R_n = s - s_n\) satisfies

\[ \int_{n+1}^\infty f(x)\,dx \le R_n \le \int_n^\infty f(x)\,dx, \qquad\text{so}\qquad s_n + \int_{n+1}^\infty f \le s \le s_n + \int_n^\infty f. \]

Common mistake

The integral's value is not the series' sum. \(\int_1^\infty x^{-2}dx = 1\), but \(\sum n^{-2} = \pi^2/6 \approx 1.645\). The test only says whether both are finite.

Example 11.5

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^\infty\frac{1}{n\ln n}\) converge?

Show solution

\(f(x) = \frac{1}{x\ln x}\) is continuous, positive and decreasing for \(x \ge 2\) (its denominator increases). With \(u = \ln x\):

\[ \int_2^t\frac{dx}{x\ln x} = \Big[\ln(\ln x)\Big]_2^t = \ln(\ln t) - \ln(\ln 2)\to\infty. \]

The series diverges, though extremely slowly: the partial sums grow like \(\ln(\ln n)\), and the first billion terms add up to less than 5.

Example 11.6 (estimating a sum)

Given \(s_{10} = \sum_{n=1}^{10}\frac{1}{n^3} \approx 1.197532\), bound the error and give an interval containing \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^3}\).

Show solution

\(\int_n^\infty x^{-3}\,dx = \frac{1}{2n^2}\). So \(R_{10} \le \frac{1}{200} = 0.005\), and

\[ 1.197532 + \frac{1}{2(11)^2} \le s \le 1.197532 + \frac{1}{2(10)^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; 1.20166 \le s \le 1.20253. \]

(The true value is \(1.20206\ldots\).) Using the midpoint of the interval as an estimate is far better than \(s_{10}\) itself.

11.4 The comparison tests

Theorem (Direct Comparison Test)

Suppose \(0 \le a_n \le b_n\) for all \(n\) (or all \(n \ge N\)).

  • If \(\sum b_n\) converges, so does \(\sum a_n\).
  • If \(\sum a_n\) diverges, so does \(\sum b_n\).

Why: the partial sums of \(\sum a_n\) are increasing and bounded by \(\sum b_n\).

Theorem (Limit Comparison Test)

Suppose \(a_n \gt 0\), \(b_n \gt 0\) and

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n} = c, \quad 0 \lt c \lt \infty. \]

Then \(\sum a_n\) and \(\sum b_n\) both converge or both diverge.

Why: for large \(n\), \(\tfrac c2 b_n \lt a_n \lt 2c\,b_n\), and direct comparison applies in each direction.

Study tip: choosing \(b_n\)

Keep only the dominant terms of numerator and denominator. \(\frac{2n^2 + 3n}{\sqrt{5 + n^7}}\) behaves like \(\frac{2n^2}{n^{7/2}} = \frac{2}{n^{3/2}}\), so compare with \(\sum n^{-3/2}\) (a convergent \(p\)-series). Limit comparison is usually easier than direct comparison because you do not need an inequality.

Example 11.7

Test for convergence: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum\frac{1}{2^n + 1}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\ln n}{n}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum\frac{2n^2 + 3n}{\sqrt{5 + n^7}}\); (d) \(\displaystyle\sum\sin\frac1n\).

Show solution

(a) \(\frac{1}{2^n + 1} \lt \frac{1}{2^n}\), a convergent geometric series. Converges.

(b) For \(n \ge 3\), \(\ln n \gt 1\), so \(\frac{\ln n}{n} \gt \frac1n\). The harmonic series diverges, so this diverges.

(c) With \(b_n = n^{-3/2}\):

\[ \frac{a_n}{b_n} = \frac{(2n^2 + 3n)\,n^{3/2}}{\sqrt{5 + n^7}} = \frac{2n^{7/2} + 3n^{5/2}}{n^{7/2}\sqrt{5n^{-7} + 1}} = \frac{2 + 3/n}{\sqrt{5n^{-7} + 1}}\to 2. \]

Since \(0 \lt 2 \lt \infty\) and \(\sum n^{-3/2}\) converges, the series converges.

(d) \(\frac{\sin(1/n)}{1/n}\to 1\), and \(\sum\frac1n\) diverges, so the series diverges.

11.5 Alternating series

Theorem (Alternating Series Test)

If \(b_n \gt 0\), the sequence \(b_n\) is decreasing (\(b_{n+1} \le b_n\), at least eventually), and \(\lim b_n = 0\), then

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty(-1)^{n-1}b_n = b_1 - b_2 + b_3 - b_4 + \cdots \]

converges.

Proof sketch. The even partial sums \(s_2, s_4, s_6, \ldots\) increase, since \(s_{2n+2} = s_{2n} + (b_{2n+1} - b_{2n+2})\) with a non-negative bracket. They are bounded above by \(b_1\), since \(s_{2n} = b_1 - (b_2 - b_3) - \cdots - b_{2n}\). So they converge to some \(s\). The odd sums satisfy \(s_{2n+1} = s_{2n} + b_{2n+1}\to s + 0\). Both subsequences have limit \(s\).

Theorem (Alternating Series Estimation)

Under the same hypotheses, the error after \(n\) terms is at most the first omitted term:

\[ |R_n| = |s - s_n| \le b_{n+1}, \]

and the true sum lies between any two consecutive partial sums.

Example 11.8

(a) Show \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n n}{n^2 + 1}\) converges. (b) Estimate \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}\) to within 0.0005.

Show solution

(a) \(b_n = \frac{n}{n^2 + 1}\to 0\). Decreasing: \(f(x) = \frac{x}{x^2 + 1}\) has \(f'(x) = \frac{1 - x^2}{(x^2 + 1)^2} \lt 0\) for \(x \gt 1\). So the series converges by the AST.

(b) \(b_n = \frac{1}{n!}\) is decreasing to 0. We need \(b_{n+1} \lt 0.0005\): \(\frac{1}{6!} \approx 0.00139\) is too big, but \(\frac{1}{7!} \approx 0.000198\) works. So sum the terms through \(n = 6\):

\[ 1 - 1 + \tfrac12 - \tfrac16 + \tfrac1{24} - \tfrac1{120} + \tfrac1{720} \approx 0.36806. \]

(The exact sum is \(e^{-1} \approx 0.36788\); the error 0.00018 is within the bound.)

11.6 Absolute and conditional convergence

Definition

\(\sum a_n\) is absolutely convergent if \(\sum|a_n|\) converges, and conditionally convergent if \(\sum a_n\) converges but \(\sum|a_n|\) diverges.

Theorem

Absolute convergence implies convergence.

Proof. \(0 \le a_n + |a_n| \le 2|a_n|\), so \(\sum(a_n + |a_n|)\) converges by comparison. Then \(\sum a_n = \sum(a_n + |a_n|) - \sum|a_n|\) is a difference of convergent series.

Why the distinction matters

An absolutely convergent series can be rearranged in any order with the same sum. A conditionally convergent one cannot: by Riemann's rearrangement theorem, its terms can be reordered to sum to any real number, or to diverge. The alternating harmonic series \(1 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 - \cdots = \ln 2\) is the standard conditionally convergent example.

Example 11.9

Classify (a) \(\displaystyle\sum\frac{\cos n}{n^2}\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n}\).

Show solution

(a) Not alternating in a regular pattern, but \(\left|\frac{\cos n}{n^2}\right| \le \frac{1}{n^2}\), so \(\sum|a_n|\) converges. Absolutely convergent (hence convergent).

(b) \(\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\) decreases to 0, so the AST gives convergence. But \(\sum\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\) is a \(p\)-series with \(p = \tfrac12\), which diverges. Conditionally convergent.

11.7 The ratio and root tests

Theorem (Ratio Test)

Let \(L = \displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|\).

  • If \(L \lt 1\), \(\sum a_n\) converges absolutely.
  • If \(L \gt 1\) (including \(L = \infty\)), \(\sum a_n\) diverges.
  • If \(L = 1\), the test is inconclusive.

Why it works. If \(L \lt 1\), pick \(r\) with \(L \lt r \lt 1\). For \(n \ge N\), \(|a_{n+1}| \le r|a_n|\), so \(|a_{N+k}| \le |a_N|\,r^k\). The tail is dominated by a convergent geometric series. If \(L \gt 1\), then eventually \(|a_{n+1}| \gt |a_n|\), so the terms do not go to zero. A series passes the ratio test exactly when it is "eventually at least as good as geometric".

Theorem (Root Test)

Let \(L = \displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}\). The conclusions are the same as for the ratio test: \(L \lt 1\) absolute convergence, \(L \gt 1\) divergence, \(L = 1\) inconclusive. (Same proof: \(|a_n| \le r^n\) eventually.)

Common mistake

Using the ratio test on \(p\)-series or other rational functions of \(n\). For \(\sum\frac{1}{n}\) and \(\sum\frac{1}{n^2}\) alike, \(L = 1\): no information. The ratio test is for factorials and exponentials; the root test is for expressions raised to the \(n\)th power.

Example 11.10

Test: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum\frac{n^3}{3^n}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum\frac{n!}{n^n}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum\left(\frac{2n + 3}{3n + 2}\right)^n\).

Show solution

(a) \(\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \frac{(n+1)^3}{3^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{3^n}{n^3} = \frac13\left(1 + \frac1n\right)^3\to\frac13 \lt 1\). Converges absolutely.

(b) \(\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{n^n}{n!} = \frac{(n+1)\,n^n}{(n+1)^{n+1}} = \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n = \frac{1}{(1 + 1/n)^n}\to\frac1e \lt 1\). Converges.

(c) \(\sqrt[n]{a_n} = \frac{2n + 3}{3n + 2}\to\frac23 \lt 1\). Converges by the root test.

11.8 Strategy for testing series

Decision order

  1. Divergence test: does \(a_n\not\to 0\)? Then it diverges. (Check this first; it is quick.)
  2. Recognise a family: geometric \(\sum ar^n\), \(p\)-series \(\sum 1/n^p\), telescoping.
  3. Rational or algebraic in \(n\) (powers and roots of \(n\)): limit comparison with a \(p\)-series.
  4. Factorials or \(c^n\): ratio test.
  5. \((\ldots)^n\): root test.
  6. \((-1)^n\) factor: check absolute convergence first; if that fails, use the alternating series test (conditional).
  7. \(f(n)\) easy to integrate (e.g. \(\frac{1}{n\ln n}\), \(ne^{-n^2}\)): integral test.
  8. Bounded oscillating factors like \(\sin n\), \(\cos n\): bound them by 1 and compare absolute values.

Practice problems: convergence

  1. \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n}{2n + 1}\)
    Answer

    Diverges: \(a_n\to\tfrac12 \ne 0\) (divergence test).

  2. Find the sum of \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{2^{n+1}}{3^n}\).
    Answer

    \(2\sum_{n=1}^\infty\left(\tfrac23\right)^n = 2\cdot\frac{2/3}{1 - 2/3} = 4\).

  3. \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^2 + 4}\)
    Answer

    Converges: \(\frac{1}{n^2 + 4} \lt \frac{1}{n^2}\), a convergent \(p\)-series.

  4. \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n\ln n}{n}\): absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent, or divergent?
    Answer

    \(\frac{\ln n}{n}\) decreases for \(n \ge 3\) (derivative of \(\frac{\ln x}{x}\) is \(\frac{1 - \ln x}{x^2} \lt 0\) for \(x \gt e\)) and \(\to 0\), so the AST gives convergence. \(\sum\frac{\ln n}{n}\) diverges (Example 11.7b). Conditionally convergent.

  5. \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{5^n}{n!}\)
    Answer

    Ratio: \(\frac{5}{n + 1}\to 0 \lt 1\). Converges absolutely (its sum is \(e^5\)).

  6. \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty ne^{-n^2}\)
    Answer

    Integral test: \(xe^{-x^2}\) is positive and decreasing for \(x \ge 1\), and \(\int_1^\infty xe^{-x^2}dx = \left[-\tfrac12 e^{-x^2}\right]_1^\infty = \tfrac{1}{2e}\). Converges.

  7. \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\left(\frac{n}{n + 1}\right)^{n^2}\)
    Answer

    Root: \(\sqrt[n]{a_n} = \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n = \frac{1}{(1 + 1/n)^n}\to\frac1e \lt 1\). Converges.

11.9 Power series

Definition

A power series centred at \(a\) is

\[ \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n(x - a)^n = c_0 + c_1(x - a) + c_2(x - a)^2 + \cdots \]

For each \(x\) it is an ordinary series that may or may not converge. Its sum is a function of \(x\) whose domain is the set of \(x\) where the series converges. (Convention: \((x - a)^0 = 1\) even at \(x = a\).)

Theorem (Radius of convergence)

For a power series \(\sum c_n(x - a)^n\), exactly one of the following holds:

  1. It converges only at \(x = a\) (\(R = 0\)).
  2. It converges for all \(x\) (\(R = \infty\)).
  3. There is \(R \gt 0\) such that it converges (absolutely) for \(|x - a| \lt R\) and diverges for \(|x - a| \gt R\).

\(R\) is the radius of convergence. At the two endpoints \(x = a \pm R\) anything can happen; they must be checked individually. The interval of convergence is the set of all \(x\) where it converges.

Study tip: the standard procedure

(1) Apply the ratio test (or root test) to \(|c_n(x - a)^n|\), treating \(x\) as fixed. (2) Solve "limit \(\lt 1\)" for \(|x - a| \lt R\). (3) Plug each endpoint into the series and test the resulting numerical series with a different test (the ratio test always gives \(L = 1\) there). (4) State the interval with the correct brackets.

Example 11.11

Find the radius and interval of convergence: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(x - 3)^n}{n\,2^n}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(2x - 1)^n}{n^2}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty n!\,x^n\).

Show solution

(a) \(\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = \frac{|x - 3|^{n+1}}{(n+1)2^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{n\,2^n}{|x - 3|^n} = \frac{|x - 3|}{2}\cdot\frac{n}{n+1}\to\frac{|x - 3|}{2}\). Converges for \(|x - 3| \lt 2\): \(R = 2\).

Endpoints: \(x = 5\) gives \(\sum\frac{2^n}{n2^n} = \sum\frac1n\), divergent. \(x = 1\) gives \(\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\), convergent (AST). Interval: \([1, 5)\).

(b) Ratio: \(|2x - 1|\frac{n^2}{(n+1)^2}\to|2x - 1|\). Need \(|2x - 1| \lt 1\), i.e. \(\left|x - \tfrac12\right| \lt \tfrac12\): \(R = \tfrac12\), centre \(\tfrac12\).

Endpoints: \(x = 1\): \(\sum\frac{1}{n^2}\) converges. \(x = 0\): \(\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\) converges absolutely. Interval: \([0, 1]\).

(c) Ratio: \((n + 1)|x|\to\infty\) for any \(x \ne 0\). Converges only at \(x = 0\): \(R = 0\).

11.10 Representing functions as power series

Everything starts from the geometric series, read as a function:

\[ \frac{1}{1 - x} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots, \qquad |x| \lt 1. \]

Substitute for \(x\), multiply by powers of \(x\), and differentiate or integrate to get many more.

Theorem (Term-by-term differentiation and integration)

If \(f(x) = \sum c_n(x - a)^n\) has radius of convergence \(R \gt 0\), then \(f\) is differentiable (hence continuous) on \((a - R, a + R)\) and

\[ f'(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty nc_n(x - a)^{n-1}, \qquad \int f(x)\,dx = C + \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n\frac{(x - a)^{n+1}}{n + 1}. \]

Both new series have the same radius \(R\). The behaviour at the endpoints may change, so recheck them.

Example 11.12 (substitution)

Find a power series for \(\dfrac{x}{4 + x}\) and its radius of convergence.

Show solution

Factor to match \(\frac{1}{1 - u}\): \(\frac{x}{4 + x} = \frac{x}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - (-x/4)}\).

\[ \frac{x}{4 + x} = \frac x4\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(-\frac x4\right)^n = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n x^{n+1}}{4^{n+1}}, \qquad \left|\frac{x}{4}\right| \lt 1 \iff |x| \lt 4. \]

\(R = 4\).

Example 11.13 (integration: \(\arctan\) and \(\ln\))

Find Maclaurin series for \(\arctan x\) and \(\ln(1 + x)\).

Show solution

Replace \(x\) by \(-x^2\) in the geometric series: \(\frac{1}{1 + x^2} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty(-1)^n x^{2n}\) for \(|x| \lt 1\). Integrate, using \(\arctan 0 = 0\) to get \(C = 0\):

\[ \arctan x = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{2n + 1} = x - \frac{x^3}{3} + \frac{x^5}{5} - \cdots,\qquad R = 1. \]

Similarly \(\frac{1}{1 + x} = \sum(-1)^n x^n\); integrate with \(\ln 1 = 0\):

\[ \ln(1 + x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n-1}x^n}{n} = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} - \cdots,\qquad R = 1. \]

Both series also converge at \(x = 1\) (alternating series), and their sums there are the function values: \(1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \cdots = \frac\pi4\) and \(1 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 - \cdots = \ln 2\).

Example 11.14 (differentiation: summing a series)

Find the exact value of \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n}{2^n}\).

Show solution

Differentiate the geometric series: \(\frac{1}{(1 - x)^2} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty nx^{n-1}\) for \(|x| \lt 1\). Multiply by \(x\): \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty nx^n = \frac{x}{(1 - x)^2}\). At \(x = \tfrac12\):

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n}{2^n} = \frac{1/2}{(1/2)^2} = 2. \]

11.11 Taylor and Maclaurin series

Theorem (coefficients are forced)

If \(f\) has a power series representation \(f(x) = \sum c_n(x - a)^n\) for \(|x - a| \lt R\), then

\[ c_n = \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}, \qquad f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n. \]

This is the Taylor series of \(f\) at \(a\); when \(a = 0\) it is the Maclaurin series.

Proof. Setting \(x = a\) gives \(f(a) = c_0\). Differentiate term by term: \(f'(x) = c_1 + 2c_2(x - a) + 3c_3(x - a)^2 + \cdots\), so \(f'(a) = c_1\). Again: \(f''(a) = 2c_2\), then \(f'''(a) = 3\cdot 2\,c_3\), and in general \(f^{(n)}(a) = n!\,c_n\).

The theorem says: if \(f\) has a power series, it must be the Taylor series. It does not say every smooth function equals its Taylor series. To prove that it does, show the remainder goes to zero.

Definition (Taylor polynomial and remainder)

The \(n\)th-degree Taylor polynomial of \(f\) at \(a\) is

\[ T_n(x) = \sum_{i=0}^n\frac{f^{(i)}(a)}{i!}(x - a)^i = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x - a)^2 + \cdots + \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n, \]

and the remainder is \(R_n(x) = f(x) - T_n(x)\). \(T_1\) is the tangent-line (linear) approximation. If \(R_n(x)\to 0\) as \(n\to\infty\) for \(|x - a| \lt R\), then \(f\) equals its Taylor series on that interval.

Theorem (Taylor's Inequality)

If \(|f^{(n+1)}(x)| \le M\) for \(|x - a| \le d\), then for \(|x - a| \le d\)

\[ |R_n(x)| \le \frac{M}{(n + 1)!}|x - a|^{n+1}. \]

Equivalently (Lagrange form), \(R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(z)}{(n + 1)!}(x - a)^{n+1}\) for some \(z\) between \(a\) and \(x\).

Proof idea (\(n = 1\), \(x \gt a\)). From \(f''(t) \le M\), integrate from \(a\) to \(x\): \(f'(x) - f'(a) \le M(x - a)\). Integrate again: \(f(x) - f(a) - f'(a)(x - a) \le \frac{M}{2}(x - a)^2\), i.e. \(R_1(x) \le \frac M2(x - a)^2\). The same argument starting from \(f'' \ge -M\) gives \(R_1(x) \ge -\frac M2(x - a)^2\). For general \(n\), integrate \(n + 1\) times. The \(n = 0\) case is just the Mean Value Theorem, and the Lagrange form is its generalisation.

Common mistake

Assuming a function with derivatives of all orders equals its Taylor series. The function \(f(x) = e^{-1/x^2}\) (with \(f(0) = 0\)) has \(f^{(n)}(0) = 0\) for every \(n\), so its Maclaurin series is identically 0, yet \(f(x) \gt 0\) for \(x \ne 0\). Here \(R_n(x)\not\to 0\).

Example 11.15 (\(e^x\) equals its series)

Derive the Maclaurin series for \(e^x\) and prove it converges to \(e^x\) for every \(x\).

Show solution

Every derivative of \(e^x\) is \(e^x\), and equals 1 at 0, so \(c_n = \frac{1}{n!}\) and the series is \(\sum\frac{x^n}{n!}\). The ratio test gives \(\frac{|x|}{n + 1}\to 0\), so \(R = \infty\).

Now fix \(x\) and let \(d = |x|\). On \([-d, d]\), \(|f^{(n+1)}| = e^t \le e^d = M\). Taylor's inequality:

\[ |R_n(x)| \le \frac{e^d}{(n + 1)!}|x|^{n+1}\to 0, \]

because \(\frac{d^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\to 0\) for any fixed \(d\). Therefore \(e^x = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x^n}{n!}\) for all \(x\). At \(x = 1\): \(e = 1 + 1 + \frac12 + \frac16 + \cdots\).

The same argument works for \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\), even more easily: all their derivatives are bounded by \(M = 1\).

Example 11.16 (a Taylor series not at 0)

Find the Taylor series of \(\ln x\) at \(a = 1\).

Show solution

\(f' = x^{-1}\), \(f'' = -x^{-2}\), \(f''' = 2x^{-3}\), \(f^{(4)} = -3!\,x^{-4}\); in general \(f^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!\,x^{-n}\) for \(n \ge 1\). At \(x = 1\): \(\frac{f^{(n)}(1)}{n!} = \frac{(-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!}{n!} = \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\), and \(f(1) = 0\).

\[ \ln x = \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}(x - 1)^n = (x - 1) - \frac{(x - 1)^2}{2} + \frac{(x - 1)^3}{3} - \cdots \]

This is the \(\ln(1 + u)\) series with \(u = x - 1\), valid for \(0 \lt x \le 2\).

Example 11.17 (using known series)

(a) Find the Maclaurin series of \(x^2e^{-x}\) and use it to find \(f^{(5)}(0)\). (b) Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x - x}{x^3}\). (c) Approximate \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,dx\) with error less than 0.001.

Show solution

(a) \(e^{-x} = \sum\frac{(-1)^n x^n}{n!}\), so \(x^2e^{-x} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n x^{n+2}}{n!}\). The \(x^5\) term has \(n = 3\): coefficient \(-\frac{1}{3!} = -\frac16\). Since this coefficient is \(\frac{f^{(5)}(0)}{5!}\), \(f^{(5)}(0) = -\frac{120}{6} = -20\). (No need to differentiate five times.)

(b) \(\sin x - x = -\frac{x^3}{6} + \frac{x^5}{120} - \cdots\), so \(\frac{\sin x - x}{x^3} = -\frac16 + \frac{x^2}{120} - \cdots\to -\frac16\).

(c) \(e^{-x^2} = \sum\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{n!}\). Integrate term by term:

\[ \int_0^1 e^{-x^2}dx = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{n!\,(2n + 1)} = 1 - \frac13 + \frac{1}{10} - \frac{1}{42} + \frac{1}{216} - \frac{1}{1320} + \cdots \]

This is alternating with decreasing terms. The term \(\frac{1}{1320} \approx 0.00076 \lt 0.001\), so stop before it: \(1 - 0.33333 + 0.1 - 0.02381 + 0.00463 \approx 0.7475\). (True value \(0.74682\ldots\).) This integral has no elementary antiderivative, yet we computed it to three decimals by hand.

11.12 The binomial series

Theorem (Binomial series)

For any real \(k\) and \(|x| \lt 1\),

\[ (1 + x)^k = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\binom{k}{n}x^n, \qquad \binom{k}{n} = \frac{k(k - 1)(k - 2)\cdots(k - n + 1)}{n!},\quad \binom{k}{0} = 1. \]

If \(k\) is a non-negative integer, the coefficients vanish for \(n \gt k\) and this is the ordinary binomial theorem (a finite sum valid for all \(x\)). Otherwise \(R = 1\) (ratio test: \(\left|\frac{k - n}{n + 1}\right||x|\to|x|\)).

Common mistake

The formula needs the form \((1 + \text{small})^k\). For \(\sqrt{4 + x}\), factor first: \(\sqrt{4 + x} = 2\left(1 + \frac x4\right)^{1/2}\), valid for \(|x| \lt 4\).

Example 11.18

(a) Find the first four terms of the series for \(\sqrt{1 + x}\) and estimate \(\sqrt{1.1}\). (b) Find the Maclaurin series of \(\arcsin x\) up to \(x^7\).

Show solution

(a) \(k = \tfrac12\): \(\binom{1/2}{2} = \frac{\frac12\left(-\frac12\right)}{2} = -\frac18\), \(\binom{1/2}{3} = \frac{\frac12\left(-\frac12\right)\left(-\frac32\right)}{6} = \frac{1}{16}\).

\[ \sqrt{1 + x} = 1 + \frac x2 - \frac{x^2}{8} + \frac{x^3}{16} - \cdots \]

At \(x = 0.1\): \(1 + 0.05 - 0.00125 + 0.0000625 = 1.0488125\). (True value \(1.0488088\ldots\); error about \(4\times 10^{-6}\).)

(b) \(k = -\tfrac12\): \(\binom{-1/2}{1} = -\frac12\), \(\binom{-1/2}{2} = \frac{\left(-\frac12\right)\left(-\frac32\right)}{2} = \frac38\), \(\binom{-1/2}{3} = \frac{\left(-\frac12\right)\left(-\frac32\right)\left(-\frac52\right)}{6} = -\frac{5}{16}\). With \(x\to -x^2\):

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = 1 + \frac12x^2 + \frac38x^4 + \frac{5}{16}x^6 + \cdots \]

Integrate (with \(\arcsin 0 = 0\)):

\[ \arcsin x = x + \frac{x^3}{6} + \frac{3x^5}{40} + \frac{5x^7}{112} + \cdots,\qquad |x| \lt 1. \]

Physics connection: relativistic kinetic energy

With \(\gamma = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}\), the binomial series with \(k = -\tfrac12\) and \(x = -v^2/c^2\) gives \(\gamma = 1 + \frac{v^2}{2c^2} + \frac{3v^4}{8c^4} + \cdots\). So the kinetic energy is

\[ K = (\gamma - 1)mc^2 = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \frac{3}{8}\frac{mv^4}{c^2} + \cdots \]

Newtonian kinetic energy is the first term; the second is the leading relativistic correction, negligible when \(v \ll c\).

11.13 Applications of Taylor polynomials

In practice we use a finite Taylor polynomial \(T_n\) and need to know how large the error is. Two tools bound it:

  • Taylor's inequality (always applicable): \(|R_n| \le \frac{M}{(n+1)!}|x - a|^{n+1}\).
  • Alternating series estimate (when the series at that \(x\) is alternating with decreasing terms): \(|R_n| \le\) first omitted term.

Example 11.19

(a) Find \(T_2\) for \(f(x) = \sqrt x\) at \(a = 4\) and use it to estimate \(\sqrt{4.2}\). (b) Bound the error of \(T_2\) on \(4 \le x \le 4.5\).

Show solution

(a) \(f(4) = 2\); \(f'(x) = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2}\), \(f'(4) = \tfrac14\); \(f''(x) = -\tfrac14 x^{-3/2}\), \(f''(4) = -\tfrac{1}{32}\).

\[ T_2(x) = 2 + \frac{x - 4}{4} - \frac{(x - 4)^2}{64}, \qquad T_2(4.2) = 2 + 0.05 - 0.000625 = 2.049375. \]

(b) \(f'''(x) = \tfrac38 x^{-5/2}\), which is decreasing, so on \([4, 4.5]\) its maximum is at \(x = 4\): \(M = \frac{3}{8\cdot 32} = \frac{3}{256}\).

\[ |R_2(x)| \le \frac{3/256}{3!}(0.5)^3 = \frac{3}{256}\cdot\frac{0.125}{6} \approx 0.000244. \]

(At \(x = 4.2\) specifically, the bound is \(\frac{3/256}{6}(0.2)^3 \approx 0.0000156\); the true error is \(\sqrt{4.2} - 2.049375 \approx 0.0000152\).)

Example 11.20 (small-angle approximation)

For what angles is \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) accurate to within 0.001?

Show solution

\(\sin\theta = \theta - \frac{\theta^3}{6} + \frac{\theta^5}{120} - \cdots\). For \(|\theta| \lt 1\) this alternates with decreasing terms, so the error of \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\) is at most \(\frac{|\theta|^3}{6}\). (Taylor's inequality with \(n = 2\), since \(T_2 = T_1 = \theta\), and \(M = 1\), gives the same bound.)

\[ \frac{|\theta|^3}{6} \lt 0.001 \iff |\theta| \lt \sqrt[3]{0.006} \approx 0.182\ \text{rad} \approx 10.4^\circ. \]

This is why the simple-pendulum equation \(\ddot\theta + \frac gL\sin\theta = 0\) is replaced by the linear \(\ddot\theta + \frac gL\theta = 0\) for swings up to about 10°. The next correction to the period is \(T \approx 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16}\right)\).

Physics connection: far-field approximations

Charges \(+q\) and \(-q\) sit at \(x = \pm d/2\) on the \(x\)-axis. At a far point \(x = r \gg d\), the field is \(E = kq\left[\frac{1}{(r - d/2)^2} - \frac{1}{(r + d/2)^2}\right] = \frac{kq}{r^2}\left[(1 - u)^{-2} - (1 + u)^{-2}\right]\) with \(u = \frac{d}{2r}\). Binomial: \((1 \mp u)^{-2} \approx 1 \pm 2u\), so \(E \approx \frac{kq}{r^2}\cdot 4u = \frac{2kqd}{r^3}\). The dipole field falls off as \(1/r^3\), one power faster than a single charge. Expanding in a small ratio and keeping the first non-vanishing term is the most common use of Taylor series in physics.

Practice problems: power and Taylor series

  1. Find the interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n\,x^n}{3^n}\).
    Answer

    Ratio: \(\frac{n+1}{n}\cdot\frac{|x|}{3}\to\frac{|x|}{3}\), so \(R = 3\). At \(x = \pm 3\) the terms are \(n(\pm1)^n\), which do not go to 0. Interval \((-3, 3)\).

  2. Find a power series for \(\dfrac{x}{1 - x^2}\) and its radius.
    Answer

    \(x\sum(x^2)^n = \sum_{n=0}^\infty x^{2n+1} = x + x^3 + x^5 + \cdots\), \(R = 1\).

  3. Find the Maclaurin series of \(x\cos 2x\).
    Answer

    \(\cos 2x = \sum\frac{(-1)^n(2x)^{2n}}{(2n)!}\), so \(x\cos 2x = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n 4^n x^{2n+1}}{(2n)!} = x - 2x^3 + \tfrac23 x^5 - \cdots\), all \(x\).

  4. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1 - \cos x - x^2/2}{x^4}\).
    Answer

    \(1 - \cos x = \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{24} + \cdots\), so the numerator is \(-\frac{x^4}{24} + \cdots\). Limit \(-\frac{1}{24}\).

  5. Find the sum \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n\pi^{2n}}{4^n(2n)!}\).
    Answer

    \(\frac{\pi^{2n}}{4^n} = \left(\frac\pi2\right)^{2n}\), so this is the cosine series at \(x = \pi/2\): sum \(= \cos\frac\pi2 = 0\).

  6. How many terms of \(\ln(1.2) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n-1}(0.2)^n}{n}\) guarantee an error below \(10^{-4}\)? Compute the estimate.
    Answer

    Need the first omitted term \(\frac{0.2^{n+1}}{n+1} \lt 10^{-4}\): \(n = 3\) gives \(0.0004\) (too big); \(n = 4\) gives \(0.000064\). Four terms: \(0.2 - 0.02 + 0.0026667 - 0.0004 = 0.1822667\). (True: \(0.1823216\).)
