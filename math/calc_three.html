1. Vectors & the geometry of space

Stewart Ch. 12 · OpenStax Vol. 3 Ch. 2

Single-variable calculus lives on a line; almost everything physical lives in space. This chapter builds the toolkit for describing space: points, arrows (vectors) and the two products that turn geometry into algebra. The dot product measures how much two directions agree (giving angles, projections and work); the cross product builds a new direction perpendicular to two others (giving normals, areas and torque). With those two operations, lines and planes become one-line equations, and every later chapter is built on them.

1.1 Three-dimensional coordinates & vectors

A point in space is an ordered triple \((x, y, z)\) in a right-handed coordinate system: if the fingers of your right hand curl from the positive \(x\)-axis toward the positive \(y\)-axis, your thumb points along the positive \(z\)-axis. The three coordinate planes (\(xy\), \(yz\), \(xz\)) divide space into eight octants.

Distance and spheres

\[ |P_1P_2| = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 + (z_2-z_1)^2} \] \[ (x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 + (z-l)^2 = r^2 \quad \text{(sphere, centre } (h,k,l),\ \text{radius } r) \]

Definition (Vector)

A vector is a quantity with magnitude and direction, written in components as \(\mathbf{a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle = a_1\mathbf{i} + a_2\mathbf{j} + a_3\mathbf{k}\), where \(\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}\) are the unit vectors along the axes. The vector from \(A(x_1,y_1,z_1)\) to \(B(x_2,y_2,z_2)\) is \(\overrightarrow{AB} = \langle x_2-x_1,\ y_2-y_1,\ z_2-z_1\rangle\). Its magnitude (length) is \(|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}\), and the unit vector in its direction is \(\hat{\mathbf{a}} = \mathbf{a}/|\mathbf{a}|\) (for \(\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{0}\)).

A vector has no fixed location: \(\langle 1,2,3\rangle\) drawn from the origin and drawn from \((5,5,5)\) is the same vector. Addition and scalar multiplication are componentwise, and they obey the familiar rules: \(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{a}\), \((\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})+\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a}+(\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c})\), \(c(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}) = c\mathbf{a}+c\mathbf{b}\), and so on. Geometrically, \(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}\) is the diagonal of the parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) (tip-to-tail rule), and \(c\mathbf{a}\) stretches \(\mathbf{a}\) by \(|c|\), reversing it if \(c \lt 0\).

Study tip

To find the centre and radius of a sphere given as \(x^2+y^2+z^2 + Dx + Ey + Fz + G = 0\), complete the square in each variable separately. If the right-hand side comes out negative the equation has no points; if zero, it is a single point.

1.2 The dot product & projections

Definition (Dot product)

\(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3\). The result is a scalar. It is commutative, distributes over addition, and \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} = |\mathbf{a}|^2\).

Theorem (Geometric form)

If \(\theta \in [0,\pi]\) is the angle between nonzero vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), then

\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = |\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta . \]

In particular \(\mathbf{a}\perp\mathbf{b} \iff \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0\).

Proof sketch. The triangle with sides \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{b}\) and \(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\) satisfies the law of cosines: \(|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}|^2 = |\mathbf{a}|^2 + |\mathbf{b}|^2 - 2|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta\). Expanding the left side algebraically, \(|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}|^2 = (\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b})\cdot(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}) = |\mathbf{a}|^2 - 2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} + |\mathbf{b}|^2\). Comparing the two expressions gives the result. \(\square\)

Projections

\[ \operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b} = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|} \qquad \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b} = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|^2}\,\mathbf{a} \]

The scalar component is a signed length; the projection is the vector "shadow" of \(\mathbf{b}\) on the line of \(\mathbf{a}\). The remainder \(\mathbf{b} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}\) is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{a}\).

Direction cosines. The angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) that \(\mathbf{a}\) makes with the axes satisfy \(\cos\alpha = a_1/|\mathbf{a}|\), etc., so \(\hat{\mathbf{a}} = \langle\cos\alpha,\cos\beta,\cos\gamma\rangle\) and \(\cos^2\alpha+\cos^2\beta+\cos^2\gamma = 1\).

Physics link. The work done by a constant force \(\mathbf{F}\) over a straight displacement \(\mathbf{D}\) is \(W = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{D}\): only the component of force along the motion does work. This becomes the line integral \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) in Chapter 5.

Common mistake

\(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}\) divides by \(|\mathbf{a}|^2\), not \(|\mathbf{a}|\), because it multiplies the scalar component by the unit vector \(\mathbf{a}/|\mathbf{a}|\). Also, \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b} \neq \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{a}\) in general.

Example 1.1

Let \(\mathbf{u} = \langle 2,-1,2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 1,3,-1\rangle\). Find the angle between them, \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v}\), and \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v}\).

Show solution

Dot product and lengths:

\[ \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 2(1) + (-1)(3) + 2(-1) = -3, \qquad |\mathbf{u}| = \sqrt{4+1+4} = 3, \qquad |\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{1+9+1} = \sqrt{11}. \]

Angle:

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{-3}{3\sqrt{11}} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{11}} \;\Rightarrow\; \theta = \arccos\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{11}}\right) \approx 107.5^\circ . \]

The angle is obtuse, as the negative dot product predicts. Scalar component and projection:

\[ \operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v} = \frac{-3}{3} = -1, \qquad \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v} = \frac{-3}{9}\langle 2,-1,2\rangle = \left\langle -\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3}, -\tfrac{2}{3}\right\rangle . \]

Check: the projection has length 1 (matching \(|\operatorname{comp}| = 1\)) and points opposite to \(\mathbf{u}\).

1.3 The cross product & triple products

Definition (Cross product)

For \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^3\),

\[ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} = \langle a_2b_3 - a_3b_2,\ a_3b_1 - a_1b_3,\ a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 \rangle . \]

The result is a vector, and the cross product is defined only in three dimensions.

Theorem (Geometry of the cross product)

For nonzero \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\) with angle \(\theta\in[0,\pi]\):

  1. \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\) is orthogonal to both \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), with direction given by the right-hand rule.
  2. \(|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}| = |\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta\), the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\).
  3. \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}\) iff \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) are parallel.

Why orthogonal? \(\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\) is the determinant with rows \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\); two equal rows make it zero. Why that length? Expanding directly gives Lagrange's identity \(|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|^2 = |\mathbf{a}|^2|\mathbf{b}|^2 - (\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})^2 = |\mathbf{a}|^2|\mathbf{b}|^2(1-\cos^2\theta)\).

Algebraic rules. \(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a} = -\,\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\) (anticommutative); \(\mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{c}\); \(\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}\), \(\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{i} = \mathbf{j}\). The cross product is not associative: \((\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{i})\times\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{0}\) but \(\mathbf{i}\times(\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j}) = \mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{k} = -\mathbf{j}\).

Triple products

\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}, \qquad V_{\text{parallelepiped}} = |\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})| \] \[ \mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{b}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}) - \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}) \quad (\text{"BAC} - \text{CAB"}) \]

Three vectors are coplanar iff their scalar triple product is zero.

Physics link. Torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\), angular momentum \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}\), and the magnetic force \(\mathbf{F} = q\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) are all cross products; the BAC–CAB rule is used constantly in electrodynamics.

Common mistake

The middle (\(\mathbf{j}\)) component of the determinant carries a minus sign: it is \(-(a_1b_3 - a_3b_1)\). Always check your answer by dotting it with \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\); both should give zero.

Example 1.2

Find the area of the triangle with vertices \(P(1,0,2)\), \(Q(3,1,0)\), \(R(0,2,1)\), and an equation of the plane through them.

Show solution

Edge vectors: \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle 2,1,-2\rangle\), \(\overrightarrow{PR} = \langle -1,2,-1\rangle\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR} &= \big\langle (1)(-1) - (-2)(2),\ (-2)(-1) - (2)(-1),\ (2)(2) - (1)(-1) \big\rangle \\ &= \langle -1+4,\ 2+2,\ 4+1\rangle = \langle 3, 4, 5\rangle . \end{aligned} \]

Check: \(\langle 3,4,5\rangle\cdot\langle 2,1,-2\rangle = 6+4-10 = 0\) and \(\langle 3,4,5\rangle\cdot\langle -1,2,-1\rangle = -3+8-5 = 0\). Good.

The triangle is half the parallelogram:

\[ A = \tfrac12\sqrt{9+16+25} = \tfrac12\sqrt{50} = \tfrac{5\sqrt2}{2} \approx 3.54 . \]

The cross product is a normal vector, so the plane through \(P\) is

\[ 3(x-1) + 4(y-0) + 5(z-2) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 3x + 4y + 5z = 13 . \]

Check \(Q\): \(9+4+0 = 13\). Check \(R\): \(0+8+5 = 13\).

Example 1.3

Find the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by \(\mathbf{a} = \langle 1,0,2\rangle\), \(\mathbf{b} = \langle 0,3,1\rangle\), \(\mathbf{c} = \langle 2,1,0\rangle\).

Show solution

Expand the determinant along the first row:

\[ \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \end{vmatrix} = 1(3\cdot 0 - 1\cdot 1) - 0(\cdots) + 2(0\cdot 1 - 3\cdot 2) = -1 - 12 = -13 . \]

Volume \(= |-13| = 13\). The negative sign only says that \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\) form a left-handed triple.

1.4 Lines & planes

Definition (Line)

The line through \(P_0\) (position vector \(\mathbf{r}_0\)) with direction \(\mathbf{v} = \langle a,b,c\rangle\) is

\[ \mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{v} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x = x_0 + at,\ y = y_0 + bt,\ z = z_0 + ct . \]

If \(a,b,c\) are all nonzero, eliminating \(t\) gives the symmetric equations \(\dfrac{x-x_0}{a} = \dfrac{y-y_0}{b} = \dfrac{z-z_0}{c}\). The segment from \(\mathbf{r}_0\) to \(\mathbf{r}_1\) is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = (1-t)\mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{r}_1\), \(0\le t\le 1\).

Definition (Plane)

The plane through \(P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)\) with normal vector \(\mathbf{n} = \langle a,b,c\rangle\) is the set of points \(P\) with \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\overrightarrow{P_0P} = 0\):

\[ a(x-x_0) + b(y-y_0) + c(z-z_0) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad ax + by + cz = d . \]

The coefficients of \(x,y,z\) in a linear equation are a normal vector.

Two lines in space can be parallel, intersecting, or skew (neither parallel nor meeting). Two planes are parallel if their normals are parallel; otherwise they meet in a line whose direction is \(\mathbf{n}_1\times\mathbf{n}_2\), and the angle between the planes is the angle between their normals.

Distances

\[ \text{point } (x_1,y_1,z_1) \text{ to plane } ax+by+cz=d: \quad D = \frac{|ax_1+by_1+cz_1-d|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}} \] \[ \text{point } Q \text{ to line through } P \text{ with direction } \mathbf{v}: \quad D = \frac{|\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\mathbf{v}|}{|\mathbf{v}|} \] \[ \text{skew lines } \mathbf{r}_1 + t\mathbf{v}_1,\ \mathbf{r}_2 + s\mathbf{v}_2: \quad D = \frac{|(\mathbf{r}_2-\mathbf{r}_1)\cdot(\mathbf{v}_1\times\mathbf{v}_2)|}{|\mathbf{v}_1\times\mathbf{v}_2|} \]

Why the point–plane formula works. Pick any point \(P_0\) on the plane. The distance is the length of the projection of \(\overrightarrow{P_0Q}\) onto the normal: \(D = |\overrightarrow{P_0Q}\cdot\mathbf{n}|/|\mathbf{n}|\). Expanding the dot product and using \(ax_0+by_0+cz_0 = d\) gives the formula. The skew-line formula is the same idea, with normal \(\mathbf{v}_1\times\mathbf{v}_2\) to both lines.

Example 1.4

Find parametric equations for the line of intersection of the planes \(x+y+z = 3\) and \(2x - y + z = 1\), and the angle between the planes.

Show solution

Normals: \(\mathbf{n}_1 = \langle 1,1,1\rangle\), \(\mathbf{n}_2 = \langle 2,-1,1\rangle\). The line lies in both planes, so it is perpendicular to both normals:

\[ \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{n}_1\times\mathbf{n}_2 = \langle (1)(1)-(1)(-1),\ (1)(2)-(1)(1),\ (1)(-1)-(1)(2)\rangle = \langle 2, 1, -3\rangle . \]

A point on the line: set \(z=0\). Then \(x+y=3\) and \(2x-y=1\); adding gives \(3x = 4\), so \(x = \tfrac43\), \(y = \tfrac53\). Hence

\[ x = \tfrac43 + 2t, \qquad y = \tfrac53 + t, \qquad z = -3t . \]

Check in plane 2: \(2(\tfrac43+2t) - (\tfrac53+t) + (-3t) = \tfrac83 - \tfrac53 + 4t - t - 3t = 1\). Good.

Angle:

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2|}{|\mathbf{n}_1||\mathbf{n}_2|} = \frac{|2-1+1|}{\sqrt3\sqrt6} = \frac{2}{3\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt2}{3} \;\Rightarrow\; \theta \approx 61.9^\circ . \]

Example 1.5

Find the distance from \(Q(1,-2,4)\) to the plane \(2x - y + 2z = 1\).

Show solution \[ D = \frac{|2(1) - (-2) + 2(4) - 1|}{\sqrt{4+1+4}} = \frac{|2+2+8-1|}{3} = \frac{11}{3} . \]

1.5 Cylinders & quadric surfaces

A cylinder is a surface built from all lines parallel to a fixed line through a given plane curve. In practice: if an equation in \(x,y,z\) is missing a variable, the surface is a cylinder parallel to that variable's axis. For example \(y = x^2\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is a parabolic "trough" extending forever in the \(z\)-direction, and \(x^2 + z^2 = 4\) is a circular cylinder of radius 2 around the \(y\)-axis.

A quadric surface is the graph of a second-degree equation in \(x,y,z\). After translating and rotating, every nondegenerate quadric is one of six standard types. The key tool for recognising and sketching them is traces: intersect with the planes \(x=k\), \(y=k\), \(z=k\) and see which conics appear.

SurfaceStandard equation (axis \(z\))Traces
Ellipsoid\(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1\)All ellipses
Elliptic paraboloid\(\dfrac{z}{c} = \dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}\)Horizontal: ellipses; vertical: parabolas
Hyperbolic paraboloid (saddle)\(\dfrac{z}{c} = \dfrac{x^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}\)Horizontal: hyperbolas; vertical: parabolas
Cone\(\dfrac{z^2}{c^2} = \dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}\)Horizontal: ellipses; vertical through axis: line pairs

Hyperboloid of two sheets\(-\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1\)Horizontal: ellipses for \(|z|\gt c\), empty for \(|z|\lt c\); vertical: hyperbolas

Study tip

Count minus signs once the equation is in "\(=1\)" form: none is an ellipsoid, one is a hyperboloid of one sheet, two is a hyperboloid of two sheets. The variable with the odd sign out is the axis. If one variable appears only to the first power, it is a paraboloid with that variable as its axis.

Example 1.6

Identify the surface \(x^2 + 4y^2 - z^2 - 2x + 8y + 1 = 0\).

Show solution

Complete the square in \(x\) and in \(y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} (x^2 - 2x + 1) - 1 + 4(y^2 + 2y + 1) - 4 - z^2 + 1 &= 0 \\ (x-1)^2 + 4(y+1)^2 - z^2 &= 4 \\ \frac{(x-1)^2}{4} + \frac{(y+1)^2}{1} - \frac{z^2}{4} &= 1 . \end{aligned} \]

One minus sign, on \(z\): a hyperboloid of one sheet with axis parallel to the \(z\)-axis, centred at \((1,-1,0)\). Its narrowest horizontal trace (at \(z=0\)) is the ellipse with semi-axes 2 and 1.

Practice problems

  1. Find the unit vector in the direction of \(\langle 3,-6,2\rangle\).
    Answer

    \(|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{9+36+4} = 7\), so \(\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \langle \tfrac37, -\tfrac67, \tfrac27\rangle\).

  2. A constant force \(\mathbf{F} = \langle 4,1,-2\rangle\) N moves an object in a straight line from the origin to \((3,2,5)\) m. Find the work done.
    Answer

    \(W = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{D} = \langle 4,1,-2\rangle\cdot\langle 3,2,5\rangle = 12 + 2 - 10 = 4\) J.

  3. Find the plane through \((2,1,-1)\) parallel to \(3x - y + 4z = 7\).
    Answer

    Same normal \(\langle 3,-1,4\rangle\): \(3(x-2) - (y-1) + 4(z+1) = 0\), i.e. \(3x - y + 4z = 1\).

  4. Show that the lines \(\mathbf{r}_1 = \langle 1,2,0\rangle + t\langle 1,-1,2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{r}_2 = \langle 3,0,5\rangle + s\langle 2,1,1\rangle\) are skew, and find the distance between them.
    Answer

    Directions are not parallel. Equating components: \(1+t = 3+2s\), \(2-t = s\), \(2t = 5+s\). The first two give \(t = 2\), \(s = 0\); then the third reads \(4 = 5\), false. So the lines are skew. Normal to both: \(\langle 1,-1,2\rangle\times\langle 2,1,1\rangle = \langle -3,3,3\rangle\). With \(\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1 = \langle 2,-2,5\rangle\): \(D = |{-6}-6+15|/\sqrt{27} = 3/(3\sqrt3) = 1/\sqrt3\).

  5. Use the triple product to decide whether \(\langle 1,2,3\rangle\), \(\langle 2,0,1\rangle\), \(\langle 0,4,5\rangle\) are coplanar.
    Answer

    \(\det = 1(0\cdot5 - 1\cdot4) - 2(2\cdot5 - 1\cdot0) + 3(2\cdot4 - 0\cdot0) = -4 - 20 + 24 = 0\). Coplanar (indeed the third is \(2\times\)first \(-\) second).

  6. Identify and describe \(z = y^2 - x^2\) and \(4x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 16\).
    Answer

    \(z = y^2 - x^2\): hyperbolic paraboloid (saddle) through the origin, opening up along \(y\), down along \(x\). \(x^2/4 + y^2/16 + z^2/16 = 1\): ellipsoid with semi-axes 2, 4, 4 (a sphere squashed along \(x\)).

2. Vector functions

Stewart Ch. 13 · OpenStax Vol. 3 Ch. 3

A vector function \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) is a moving arrow: feed it a time and it returns a position. Its tip traces a curve in space, its derivative is the velocity (tangent to the curve), and its second derivative is acceleration. Because every operation works component by component, the calculus here is just single-variable calculus done three times at once. The new geometric ideas are arc length (how far along the curve you are) and curvature (how sharply it bends), which let us split acceleration into a part that changes speed and a part that changes direction.

2.1 Vector functions & space curves

Definition (Vector function)

A vector function is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle f(t), g(t), h(t)\rangle\). Its domain is the set of \(t\) where all three components are defined. Limits are taken componentwise: \(\lim_{t\to a}\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \lim f, \lim g, \lim h\rangle\) provided each limit exists, and \(\mathbf{r}\) is continuous at \(a\) iff each component is.

The set of points traced out, \(C = \{\mathbf{r}(t)\}\), is a space curve, and \(\mathbf{r}\) is a parametrisation of it. One curve has infinitely many parametrisations (e.g. \(\mathbf{r}(2t)\) traces the same path twice as fast). The standard example is the circular helix \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle a\cos t, a\sin t, bt\rangle\), which winds around the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=a^2\), rising \(2\pi b\) per turn.

Study tip

To parametrise the intersection of two surfaces, parametrise the "simpler" one first and substitute into the other. For a cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 = a^2\) use \(x = a\cos t\), \(y = a\sin t\), then solve the other equation for \(z\).

2.2 Derivatives & integrals of vector functions

Definition (Derivative)

\[ \mathbf{r}'(t) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{r}(t+h)-\mathbf{r}(t)}{h} = \langle f'(t), g'(t), h'(t)\rangle . \]

If \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\neq\mathbf{0}\), it is a tangent vector to the curve at \(\mathbf{r}(t)\), pointing in the direction of increasing \(t\), and \(\mathbf{T}(t) = \mathbf{r}'(t)/|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\) is the unit tangent. A parametrisation with \(\mathbf{r}'\) continuous and never zero is called smooth.

Differentiation rules

\[ \frac{d}{dt}[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}] = \mathbf{u}'\cdot\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}' \qquad \frac{d}{dt}[\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}] = \mathbf{u}'\times\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}' \] \[ \frac{d}{dt}[f(t)\mathbf{u}(t)] = f'\mathbf{u} + f\mathbf{u}' \qquad \frac{d}{dt}[\mathbf{u}(f(t))] = f'(t)\,\mathbf{u}'(f(t)) \]

In the cross-product rule, keep the order of the factors.

Theorem (Constant length)

If \(|\mathbf{r}(t)|\) is constant, then \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\perp\mathbf{r}(t)\) for all \(t\).

Proof. \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r} = c^2\). Differentiating, \(2\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}' = 0\). \(\square\)

This is why, for a particle on a sphere, the velocity is tangent to the sphere, and why \(\mathbf{T}'\perp\mathbf{T}\) (since \(|\mathbf{T}| = 1\)).

Integrals are also componentwise: \(\int_a^b \mathbf{r}(t)\,dt = \left\langle \int_a^b f, \int_a^b g, \int_a^b h\right\rangle\), and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus holds: \(\int_a^b \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt = \mathbf{r}(b) - \mathbf{r}(a)\). An indefinite integral has a vector constant of integration \(\mathbf{C}\).

2.3 Arc length, curvature & the TNB frame

Arc length

\[ L = \int_a^b |\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt = \int_a^b \sqrt{f'(t)^2 + g'(t)^2 + h'(t)^2}\,dt, \qquad s(t) = \int_a^t |\mathbf{r}'(u)|\,du, \qquad \frac{ds}{dt} = |\mathbf{r}'(t)| . \]

The formula holds for a smooth curve traversed exactly once as \(t\) runs from \(a\) to \(b\).

Speed is \(ds/dt\): the rate at which arc length accumulates. Arc length is intrinsic to the curve (independent of parametrisation), so it is the natural parameter for describing shape. Reparametrising by arc length means solving \(s = s(t)\) for \(t\) and substituting; the resulting \(\mathbf{r}(s)\) has unit speed.

Definition (Curvature)

The curvature of a smooth curve is the rate at which the unit tangent turns per unit length travelled:

\[ \kappa = \left|\frac{d\mathbf{T}}{ds}\right| = \frac{|\mathbf{T}'(t)|}{|\mathbf{r}'(t)|} = \frac{|\mathbf{r}'(t)\times\mathbf{r}''(t)|}{|\mathbf{r}'(t)|^3} . \]

For a plane graph \(y = f(x)\): \(\kappa(x) = \dfrac{|f''(x)|}{\left[1 + f'(x)^2\right]^{3/2}}\). A line has \(\kappa = 0\); a circle of radius \(R\) has \(\kappa = 1/R\).

Where the cross-product form comes from. Write \(\mathbf{r}' = v\mathbf{T}\) with \(v = |\mathbf{r}'|\). Then \(\mathbf{r}'' = v'\mathbf{T} + v\mathbf{T}'\), so \(\mathbf{r}'\times\mathbf{r}'' = v^2\,\mathbf{T}\times\mathbf{T}'\) (since \(\mathbf{T}\times\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{0}\)). Because \(\mathbf{T}\perp\mathbf{T}'\) and \(|\mathbf{T}| = 1\), \(|\mathbf{T}\times\mathbf{T}'| = |\mathbf{T}'|\). So \(|\mathbf{r}'\times\mathbf{r}''| = v^2|\mathbf{T}'| = v^2\cdot\kappa v = \kappa v^3\).

Definition (TNB frame)

Where \(\mathbf{T}'\neq\mathbf{0}\): the principal unit normal \(\mathbf{N} = \mathbf{T}'/|\mathbf{T}'|\) points toward the inside of the bend, and the binormal \(\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{T}\times\mathbf{N}\) completes a right-handed orthonormal frame that travels with the particle. \(\mathbf{T}\) and \(\mathbf{N}\) span the osculating plane (the plane the curve momentarily lies in), and the osculating circle has radius \(1/\kappa\), centred along \(\mathbf{N}\). The torsion \(\tau\), defined by \(d\mathbf{B}/ds = -\tau\mathbf{N}\), measures how fast the curve twists out of its osculating plane.

Example 2.1

For the helix \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 3\cos t, 3\sin t, 4t\rangle\): find the length of one turn, the arc-length parametrisation, \(\kappa\), \(\mathbf{T}\), \(\mathbf{N}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\).

Show solution \[ \mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -3\sin t, 3\cos t, 4\rangle, \qquad |\mathbf{r}'(t)| = \sqrt{9\sin^2 t + 9\cos^2 t + 16} = 5 . \]

Length of one turn: \(L = \int_0^{2\pi} 5\,dt = 10\pi\).

Arc length from \(t=0\): \(s = 5t\), so \(t = s/5\) and \(\mathbf{r}(s) = \langle 3\cos(s/5), 3\sin(s/5), 4s/5\rangle\), which has unit speed.

Unit tangent and its derivative:

\[ \mathbf{T} = \left\langle -\tfrac35\sin t, \tfrac35\cos t, \tfrac45\right\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{T}' = \left\langle -\tfrac35\cos t, -\tfrac35\sin t, 0\right\rangle, \qquad |\mathbf{T}'| = \tfrac35 . \] \[ \kappa = \frac{|\mathbf{T}'|}{|\mathbf{r}'|} = \frac{3/5}{5} = \frac{3}{25}, \qquad \mathbf{N} = \langle -\cos t, -\sin t, 0\rangle . \]

\(\mathbf{N}\) points horizontally toward the helix axis, as expected. Binormal:

\[ \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{T}\times\mathbf{N} = \left\langle 0 - \tfrac45(-\sin t),\ \tfrac45(-\cos t) - 0,\ \tfrac35\sin^2 t + \tfrac35\cos^2 t \right\rangle = \left\langle \tfrac45\sin t, -\tfrac45\cos t, \tfrac35\right\rangle . \]

Check with the general helix formula \(\kappa = a/(a^2+b^2) = 3/25\). The constant curvature makes sense: every point of a helix looks the same.

Example 2.2

Find the curvature of \(y = x^2\) at \((0,0)\) and at \((1,1)\), and the radius of the osculating circle at the origin.

Show solution

\(f'(x) = 2x\), \(f''(x) = 2\), so \(\kappa(x) = \dfrac{2}{(1+4x^2)^{3/2}}\).

\[ \kappa(0) = 2, \qquad \kappa(1) = \frac{2}{5^{3/2}} = \frac{2}{5\sqrt5} \approx 0.179 . \]

The osculating circle at the origin has radius \(1/\kappa = \tfrac12\) and centre \((0, \tfrac12)\). The parabola is most sharply bent at its vertex and straightens out as \(|x|\) grows.

2.4 Velocity, acceleration & their components

Motion in space

\[ \mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{r}'(t), \qquad v = |\mathbf{v}(t)| = \frac{ds}{dt}, \qquad \mathbf{a}(t) = \mathbf{v}'(t) = \mathbf{r}''(t) \] \[ \mathbf{a} = a_T\,\mathbf{T} + a_N\,\mathbf{N}, \qquad a_T = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{\mathbf{r}'\cdot\mathbf{r}''}{|\mathbf{r}'|}, \qquad a_N = \kappa v^2 = \frac{|\mathbf{r}'\times\mathbf{r}''|}{|\mathbf{r}'|} \]

Derivation. From \(\mathbf{v} = v\mathbf{T}\): \(\mathbf{a} = v'\mathbf{T} + v\mathbf{T}'\). And \(\mathbf{T}' = |\mathbf{T}'|\mathbf{N} = \kappa v\,\mathbf{N}\). So \(\mathbf{a} = v'\mathbf{T} + \kappa v^2\mathbf{N}\). Acceleration always lies in the osculating plane (no \(\mathbf{B}\) component). The tangential part changes speed; the normal part, \(v^2/R\) with \(R = 1/\kappa\), is the familiar centripetal acceleration. Since \(\mathbf{T}\perp\mathbf{N}\), \(|\mathbf{a}|^2 = a_T^2 + a_N^2\), a handy check.

Physics link. Newton's second law \(\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}\) is a vector ODE: integrate \(\mathbf{a}\) once (with initial velocity) to get \(\mathbf{v}\), again (with initial position) to get \(\mathbf{r}\). A central force \(\mathbf{F}\parallel\mathbf{r}\) gives \(\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\), so angular momentum is conserved and the motion is planar — the first step in deriving Kepler's laws.

Common mistake

\(a_T\) is the derivative of the speed \(|\mathbf{v}|\), not the magnitude of the acceleration. In uniform circular motion speed is constant so \(a_T = 0\), yet \(|\mathbf{a}| = v^2/R \neq 0\).

Example 2.3

A particle moves along \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2, t^3\rangle\). At \(t=1\), find \(a_T\), \(a_N\) and \(\kappa\).

Show solution

\(\mathbf{r}' = \langle 1, 2t, 3t^2\rangle\), \(\mathbf{r}'' = \langle 0, 2, 6t\rangle\). At \(t=1\): \(\mathbf{r}' = \langle 1,2,3\rangle\), \(\mathbf{r}'' = \langle 0,2,6\rangle\), \(|\mathbf{r}'| = \sqrt{14}\).

\[ a_T = \frac{\mathbf{r}'\cdot\mathbf{r}''}{|\mathbf{r}'|} = \frac{0+4+18}{\sqrt{14}} = \frac{22}{\sqrt{14}} \approx 5.88 . \] \[ \mathbf{r}'\times\mathbf{r}'' = \langle 2\cdot6 - 3\cdot2,\ 3\cdot0 - 1\cdot6,\ 1\cdot2 - 2\cdot0\rangle = \langle 6,-6,2\rangle, \qquad |\mathbf{r}'\times\mathbf{r}''| = \sqrt{76} = 2\sqrt{19} . \] \[ a_N = \frac{2\sqrt{19}}{\sqrt{14}} \approx 2.33, \qquad \kappa = \frac{2\sqrt{19}}{(\sqrt{14})^3} = \frac{2\sqrt{19}}{14\sqrt{14}} \approx 0.166 . \]

Check: \(a_T^2 + a_N^2 = \tfrac{484}{14} + \tfrac{76}{14} = \tfrac{560}{14} = 40 = |\langle 0,2,6\rangle|^2\). Good.

Example 2.4

A ball is launched from ground level at 40 m/s, 30° above horizontal. Ignoring air resistance (\(g = 9.8\) m/s\(^2\)), find its position function, maximum height and range.

Show solution

Take \(\mathbf{a} = \langle 0, -9.8\rangle\). Initial velocity \(\mathbf{v}_0 = 40\langle\cos30^\circ, \sin 30^\circ\rangle = \langle 20\sqrt3, 20\rangle\), initial position \(\mathbf{0}\). Integrate twice:

\[ \mathbf{v}(t) = \langle 20\sqrt3,\ 20 - 9.8t\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 20\sqrt3\,t,\ 20t - 4.9t^2\rangle . \]

Max height when \(v_y = 0\): \(t = 20/9.8\) s. Then

\[ y_{\max} = 20\cdot\frac{20}{9.8} - 4.9\left(\frac{20}{9.8}\right)^2 = \frac{400}{9.8} - \frac{200}{9.8} = \frac{200}{9.8} \approx 20.4 \text{ m}. \]

Landing when \(y = 0\), \(t \neq 0\): \(t = 20/4.9 \approx 4.08\) s. Range \(= 20\sqrt3\cdot\dfrac{20}{4.9} = \dfrac{400\sqrt3}{4.9} \approx 141.4\) m.

    Answer

    \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2\cos t, 2\sin t, 2\cos t + 2\sin t\rangle\), \(0\le t\le 2\pi\) (an ellipse).

  2. Find \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) if \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle 2t, e^t, \cos t\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{r}(0) = \langle 1,0,2\rangle\).

    Integrate: \(\langle t^2, e^t, \sin t\rangle + \mathbf{C}\). At 0: \(\langle 0,1,0\rangle + \mathbf{C} = \langle 1,0,2\rangle\), so \(\mathbf{C} = \langle 1,-1,2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t^2+1,\ e^t - 1,\ \sin t + 2\rangle\).

  3. Find the tangent line to \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle e^t, te^t, t^2\rangle\) at \(t = 0\).
    Answer

    \(\mathbf{r}(0) = \langle 1,0,0\rangle\); \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle e^t, (1+t)e^t, 2t\rangle\), so \(\mathbf{r}'(0) = \langle 1,1,0\rangle\). Line: \(x = 1+s,\ y = s,\ z = 0\).

  4. Find the length of \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t^2, 2t, \ln t\rangle\) for \(1\le t\le 2\).
    Answer

    \(|\mathbf{r}'|^2 = 4t^2 + 4 + 1/t^2 = (2t + 1/t)^2\). \(L = \int_1^2 (2t + 1/t)\,dt = [t^2 + \ln t]_1^2 = 3 + \ln 2\).

  5. For uniform circular motion \(\mathbf{r}(t) = R\langle\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t\rangle\), show \(\mathbf{a} = -\omega^2\mathbf{r}\), \(a_T = 0\), and \(a_N = v^2/R\).

    \(\mathbf{v} = R\omega\langle -\sin\omega t, \cos\omega t\rangle\), speed \(v = R\omega\) (constant, so \(a_T = 0\)). \(\mathbf{a} = -R\omega^2\langle\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t\rangle = -\omega^2\mathbf{r}\), magnitude \(R\omega^2 = v^2/R = a_N\).

  6. Find the curvature of \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2, t^3\rangle\) at \(t = 0\).

    \(\mathbf{r}'(0) = \langle 1,0,0\rangle\), \(\mathbf{r}''(0) = \langle 0,2,0\rangle\), cross product \(\langle 0,0,2\rangle\). \(\kappa = 2/1^3 = 2\).

3. Partial derivatives

Stewart Ch. 14 · OpenStax Vol. 3 Ch. 4

Now the input is a point and the output is a number: temperature in a room, potential in space, profit as a function of two prices. You can no longer ask for "the" derivative, because there are infinitely many directions to move. The fix is to vary one coordinate at a time (partial derivatives), then package all of them into a single vector, the gradient, which points uphill and whose dot product with any direction gives the slope in that direction. Near a point, a differentiable function looks like a plane; maxima and minima happen where that plane is flat (or, under a constraint, where the gradient lines up with the constraint's gradient).

3.1 Functions of several variables, level sets, limits

Definition (Function of two or three variables)

A function \(f: D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}\) assigns a number \(f(x,y)\) to each point of its domain \(D\). Its graph is the surface \(z = f(x,y)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). A level curve is \(\{(x,y) : f(x,y) = k\}\); a collection of them is a contour map. For \(f(x,y,z)\), the sets \(f = k\) are level surfaces.

Contour maps are how you "see" a function of two variables: closely spaced contours mean steep slopes. In physics, the level surfaces of the electric potential \(V\) are the equipotentials, and we will prove below that the field \(\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V\) crosses them at right angles.

Definition (Limit and continuity)

\(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y) = L\) means: for every \(\varepsilon\gt 0\) there is \(\delta\gt 0\) such that \(|f(x,y) - L|\lt\varepsilon\) whenever \((x,y)\in D\) and \(0\lt\sqrt{(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2}\lt\delta\). The function is continuous at \((a,b)\) if the limit equals \(f(a,b)\). Polynomials, rational functions (where the denominator is nonzero) and compositions of continuous functions are continuous.

The crucial difference from one variable: \((x,y)\) can approach \((a,b)\) along infinitely many paths, and the limit must be the same along all of them.

Study tip

To show a limit does not exist: find two paths (lines \(y = mx\), parabolas \(y = mx^2\), the axes) giving different values. To show a limit exists: path tests can never prove it; use the squeeze theorem (bound \(|f - L|\) by something that goes to 0) or switch to polar coordinates, where \(r\to 0\) captures every path at once.

Example 3.1

Decide whether each limit exists: (a) \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\), (b) \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}\).

Show solution

(a) Along \(y = mx\) (with \(x\neq 0\)):

\[ \frac{x(mx)}{x^2 + m^2x^2} = \frac{m}{1+m^2}. \]

This depends on \(m\) (0 along the \(x\)-axis, \(\tfrac12\) along \(y = x\)), so the limit does not exist.

(b) Since \(x^2 \le x^2 + y^2\), we have \(\dfrac{x^2}{x^2+y^2}\le 1\), so

\[ 0 \le \left|\frac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}\right| \le |y| \to 0 . \]

By the squeeze theorem the limit is \(0\). (In polar form: \(r\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\), bounded by \(r\).)

3.2 Partial derivatives

Definition (Partial derivative)

\[ f_x(a,b) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b) - f(a,b)}{h}, \qquad f_y(a,b) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a,b+h)-f(a,b)}{h} . \]

To compute \(f_x\), treat every other variable as a constant and differentiate with respect to \(x\). Geometrically, \(f_x(a,b)\) is the slope of the curve cut from the graph by the plane \(y = b\).

Higher derivatives. \(f_{xx} = \partial^2 f/\partial x^2\), \(f_{xy} = (f_x)_y = \partial^2 f/\partial y\,\partial x\) (note the order reverses between the two notations), and so on.

Theorem (Clairaut / equality of mixed partials)

If \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are both continuous on an open disk containing \((a,b)\), then \(f_{xy}(a,b) = f_{yx}(a,b)\).

Partial differential equations are statements about partial derivatives. The three you will meet constantly in physics are Laplace's equation \(u_{xx} + u_{yy} + u_{zz} = 0\) (electrostatic potential in charge-free space), the heat/diffusion equation \(u_t = \alpha^2 u_{xx}\), and the wave equation \(u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}\).

Example 3.2

For \(f(x,y) = x^2y^3 + e^{xy}\), find \(f_x\), \(f_y\) and verify \(f_{xy} = f_{yx}\).

Show solution \[ f_x = 2xy^3 + ye^{xy}, \qquad f_y = 3x^2y^2 + xe^{xy} . \]

Differentiate \(f_x\) with respect to \(y\) (product rule on \(ye^{xy}\)):

\[ f_{xy} = 6xy^2 + e^{xy} + xye^{xy} . \]

Differentiate \(f_y\) with respect to \(x\) (product rule on \(xe^{xy}\)):

\[ f_{yx} = 6xy^2 + e^{xy} + xye^{xy} . \]

They agree, as Clairaut guarantees (everything here is continuous).

3.3 Tangent planes, linear approximation & differentiability

Tangent plane and linearisation

\[ z - z_0 = f_x(a,b)(x-a) + f_y(a,b)(y-b), \qquad z_0 = f(a,b) \] \[ L(x,y) = f(a,b) + f_x(a,b)(x-a) + f_y(a,b)(y-b) \approx f(x,y) \text{ near } (a,b) \] \[ dz = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy \quad \text{(total differential)} \]

Definition (Differentiable)

\(f\) is differentiable at \((a,b)\) if \(f(a+\Delta x, b+\Delta y) - f(a,b) = f_x\Delta x + f_y\Delta y + \varepsilon_1\Delta x + \varepsilon_2\Delta y\) with \(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2\to 0\) as \((\Delta x,\Delta y)\to(0,0)\). In words: the tangent plane approximates \(f\) with error that is small compared with the step size.

Theorem (Sufficient condition)

If \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) exist near \((a,b)\) and are continuous at \((a,b)\), then \(f\) is differentiable at \((a,b)\).

Common mistake

Existence of partial derivatives does not imply differentiability, or even continuity. The function \(f = xy/(x^2+y^2)\), \(f(0,0) = 0\), from Example 3.1(a) has \(f_x(0,0) = f_y(0,0) = 0\) (it is zero on both axes) yet is not continuous at the origin.

Example 3.3

Find the tangent plane to \(z = \sqrt{20 - x^2 - 7y^2}\) at \((2,1,3)\), and use it to estimate \(f(1.95, 1.08)\).

Show solution

Check: \(f(2,1) = \sqrt{20-4-7} = 3\). Partials:

\[ f_x = \frac{-x}{\sqrt{20-x^2-7y^2}}, \quad f_y = \frac{-7y}{\sqrt{20-x^2-7y^2}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad f_x(2,1) = -\tfrac23, \ f_y(2,1) = -\tfrac73 . \] \[ z = 3 - \tfrac23(x-2) - \tfrac73(y-1) . \]

With \(\Delta x = -0.05\), \(\Delta y = 0.08\):

\[ f(1.95,1.08)\approx 3 - \tfrac23(-0.05) - \tfrac73(0.08) = 3 + 0.0333 - 0.1867 \approx 2.847 . \]

The exact value is \(\sqrt{20 - 3.8025 - 8.1648} = \sqrt{8.0327}\approx 2.834\): a good estimate.

3.4 The chain rule

Theorem (Chain rule)

Case 1. If \(z = f(x,y)\) is differentiable and \(x = x(t)\), \(y = y(t)\) are differentiable, then

\[ \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t). \]

Case 2. If \(x = x(s,t)\), \(y = y(s,t)\) have partial derivatives, then

\[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial s} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial s}, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} . \]

General rule: sum over every path from the dependent variable down to the independent variable in a tree diagram, multiplying derivatives along each path.

Implicit differentiation

\[ F(x,y) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}, \qquad F(x,y,z) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{F_x}{F_z}, \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\frac{F_y}{F_z} \]

(valid where the denominator is nonzero; the Implicit Function Theorem guarantees \(z\) is then locally a differentiable function of \(x,y\)). Proof: differentiate \(F(x,y,z(x,y)) = 0\) with respect to \(x\) by the chain rule: \(F_x + F_z\,z_x = 0\).

Example 3.4

Let \(z = x^2y + y^3\) with \(x = st\), \(y = s + t^2\). Find \(\partial z/\partial s\) and \(\partial z/\partial t\) at \((s,t) = (2,1)\).

Show solution

At \((s,t) = (2,1)\): \(x = 2\), \(y = 3\).

\[ z_x = 2xy = 12, \qquad z_y = x^2 + 3y^2 = 4 + 27 = 31 . \] \[ x_s = t = 1,\quad y_s = 1,\quad x_t = s = 2,\quad y_t = 2t = 2 . \] \[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = 12(1) + 31(1) = 43, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = 12(2) + 31(2) = 86 . \]

Check \(\partial z/\partial t\) by substituting first: \(z = s^2t^2(s+t^2) + (s+t^2)^3\), so \(z_t = s^2\big[2t(s+t^2) + 2t^3\big] + 6t(s+t^2)^2\). At \((2,1)\): \(4[6+2] + 6(9) = 32 + 54 = 86\). Good.

3.5 Directional derivatives & the gradient

Definition (Directional derivative and gradient)

For a unit vector \(\mathbf{u}\), the directional derivative is \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f(\mathbf{x}) = \lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(\mathbf{x}+h\mathbf{u}) - f(\mathbf{x})}{h}\). The gradient is the vector of partials:

\[ \nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y, f_z\rangle . \]

Theorem (Properties of the gradient)

If \(f\) is differentiable:

  1. \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u} = |\nabla f|\cos\theta\).
  2. The maximum of \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f\) is \(|\nabla f|\), attained when \(\mathbf{u}\) points along \(\nabla f\); the minimum is \(-|\nabla f|\), along \(-\nabla f\).
  3. \(\nabla f(P)\) is orthogonal to the level set of \(f\) through \(P\) (when \(\nabla f(P)\neq\mathbf{0}\)).

Proof of (1). Let \(g(h) = f(\mathbf{x}+h\mathbf{u})\). Then \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = g'(0)\), and by the chain rule \(g'(0) = f_x u_1 + f_y u_2 + f_z u_3 = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u}\). Part (2) follows because \(\cos\theta\) is largest at \(\theta = 0\).

Proof of (3). Let \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) be any smooth curve lying in the level surface \(f(x,y,z) = k\), with \(\mathbf{r}(t_0) = P\). Then \(f(\mathbf{r}(t)) = k\) for all \(t\). Differentiating with the chain rule,

\[ \nabla f(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t) = 0 . \]

At \(t_0\), \(\nabla f(P)\) is perpendicular to the tangent vector of every curve in the surface through \(P\), i.e. to the whole tangent plane. \(\square\)

Tangent plane to a level surface \(F(x,y,z) = k\)

\[ F_x(P)(x-x_0) + F_y(P)(y-y_0) + F_z(P)(z-z_0) = 0 \]

The normal line is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x_0,y_0,z_0\rangle + t\,\nabla F(P)\). (A graph \(z = f(x,y)\) is the level surface \(F = f(x,y) - z = 0\), recovering the earlier formula.)

Physics link. A conservative force is \(\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U\): it points in the direction of steepest decrease of potential energy and is perpendicular to equipotential surfaces. Heat flows along \(-\nabla T\) (Fourier's law).

Common mistake

\(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u}\) needs a unit vector. If a direction is given as \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 2,-1,2\rangle\) or as "toward the point Q", normalise first.

Example 3.5

Let \(f(x,y,z) = x^2y + yz^3\) and \(P = (1,2,1)\). (a) Find the rate of change of \(f\) at \(P\) in the direction of \(\langle 2,-1,2\rangle\). (b) In which direction does \(f\) increase fastest, and at what rate? (c) Find the tangent plane to the level surface of \(f\) through \(P\).

Show solution \[ \nabla f = \langle 2xy,\ x^2 + z^3,\ 3yz^2\rangle, \qquad \nabla f(1,2,1) = \langle 4, 2, 6\rangle . \]

(a) \(\mathbf{u} = \tfrac13\langle 2,-1,2\rangle\), so \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \tfrac13(8 - 2 + 12) = 6\).

(b) Fastest increase along \(\langle 4,2,6\rangle\) (or the unit vector \(\langle 2,1,3\rangle/\sqrt{14}\)), at rate \(|\nabla f| = \sqrt{16+4+36} = \sqrt{56} = 2\sqrt{14}\approx 7.48\).

(c) \(f(P) = 2 + 2 = 4\), so the level surface is \(x^2y + yz^3 = 4\), with normal \(\langle 4,2,6\rangle\parallel\langle 2,1,3\rangle\):

\[ 2(x-1) + (y-2) + 3(z-1) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x + y + 3z = 7 . \]

3.6 Maximum & minimum values

Definition (Critical point)

\((a,b)\) is a critical point of \(f\) if \(\nabla f(a,b) = \mathbf{0}\) or a partial derivative fails to exist there. If \(f\) has a local extremum at an interior point where its first partials exist, then \(\nabla f = \mathbf{0}\) there (Fermat's theorem in several variables: each one-variable slice must have a horizontal tangent).

Theorem (Second derivative test)

Suppose the second partials of \(f\) are continuous on a disk centred at \((a,b)\), and \(\nabla f(a,b) = \mathbf{0}\). Let

\[ D = f_{xx}(a,b)\,f_{yy}(a,b) - \big[f_{xy}(a,b)\big]^2 . \]
  • \(D\gt 0\) and \(f_{xx}\gt 0\): local minimum.
  • \(D\gt 0\) and \(f_{xx}\lt 0\): local maximum.
  • \(D\lt 0\): saddle point.
  • \(D = 0\): the test is inconclusive.

Why it works (sketch). Near a critical point, the second-order Taylor expansion is \(f \approx f(a,b) + \tfrac12\left(f_{xx}h^2 + 2f_{xy}hk + f_{yy}k^2\right)\). Completing the square, the quadratic form equals \(\tfrac{1}{2f_{xx}}\left[(f_{xx}h + f_{xy}k)^2 + D\,k^2\right]\). If \(D\gt0\), the bracket is positive for every nonzero \((h,k)\), so the sign is that of \(f_{xx}\). If \(D \lt 0\), the form takes both signs. \(D\) is the determinant of the Hessian matrix, whose eigenvalues are the principal curvatures; this generalises to \(n\) variables.

Theorem (Extreme value theorem) and the closed-region method

A continuous function on a closed and bounded set in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) attains an absolute maximum and minimum. To find them: (1) evaluate \(f\) at critical points in the interior; (2) find the extreme values of \(f\) on the boundary (parametrise it, or use Lagrange multipliers); (3) the largest and smallest of all these values win.

Example 3.6

Find and classify the critical points of \(f(x,y) = x^3 - 6xy + 3y^2\).

Show solution \[ f_x = 3x^2 - 6y = 0, \qquad f_y = -6x + 6y = 0 . \]

The second equation gives \(y = x\); substituting, \(3x^2 - 6x = 3x(x-2) = 0\), so the critical points are \((0,0)\) and \((2,2)\).

\[ f_{xx} = 6x, \quad f_{yy} = 6, \quad f_{xy} = -6, \qquad D = 36x - 36 . \]
  • \((0,0)\): \(D = -36\lt 0\), a saddle point.
  • \((2,2)\): \(D = 36\gt0\), \(f_{xx} = 12\gt 0\), a local minimum with value \(f(2,2) = 8 - 24 + 12 = -4\).

Example 3.7

Find the absolute extrema of \(f(x,y) = x^2 + 2y^2 - 2x\) on the closed disk \(x^2 + y^2\le 4\).

Show solution

Interior. \(f_x = 2x - 2 = 0\), \(f_y = 4y = 0\) gives \((1,0)\), inside the disk, with \(f(1,0) = -1\).

Boundary. On \(x^2+y^2 = 4\), substitute \(y^2 = 4 - x^2\) for \(-2\le x\le 2\):

\[ g(x) = x^2 + 2(4 - x^2) - 2x = -x^2 - 2x + 8 . \]

\(g'(x) = -2x - 2 = 0\) at \(x = -1\): \(g(-1) = 9\), at the points \((-1,\pm\sqrt3)\). Endpoints: \(g(2) = 0\) at \((2,0)\); \(g(-2) = 8\) at \((-2,0)\).

Compare \(\{-1, 9, 0, 8\}\): absolute maximum \(9\) at \((-1,\pm\sqrt3)\); absolute minimum \(-1\) at \((1,0)\).

3.7 Lagrange multipliers

Theorem (Method of Lagrange multipliers)

Let \(f\) and \(g\) have continuous first partial derivatives. If \(f\) restricted to the constraint surface \(g(x,y,z) = k\) has a local extremum at \(P\), and \(\nabla g(P)\neq\mathbf{0}\), then there is a number \(\lambda\) with

\[ \nabla f(P) = \lambda\,\nabla g(P) . \]

With two constraints \(g = k\), \(h = c\): \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g + \mu\nabla h\).

Why. Let \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) be any curve in the constraint surface through \(P\). Then \(f(\mathbf{r}(t))\) has an extremum at \(t_0\), so \(\nabla f(P)\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t_0) = 0\). Thus \(\nabla f(P)\) is perpendicular to the constraint surface, just as \(\nabla g(P)\) is (Section 3.5). Two vectors normal to the same surface are parallel. In 2D: at an extremum, the level curve of \(f\) is tangent to the constraint curve.

Study tip

You get \(n + 1\) equations (the components of \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\), plus \(g = k\)) in \(n + 1\) unknowns. A reliable strategy is to solve each component equation for \(\lambda\) and set them equal, or divide equations to eliminate \(\lambda\) — but watch for cases where you divide by zero. The method finds candidates; evaluate \(f\) at all of them and compare. (In mechanics, \(\lambda\) is the constraint force; in economics, a shadow price: \(\lambda = df_{\text{opt}}/dk\).)

Example 3.8

Find the extreme values of \(f(x,y) = 3x + 4y\) on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\).

Show solution \[ \langle 3, 4\rangle = \lambda\langle 2x, 2y\rangle \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac{3}{2\lambda},\ y = \frac{4}{2\lambda} \quad (\lambda\neq 0 \text{ since } \nabla f\neq\mathbf{0}). \]

Substitute into the constraint: \(\dfrac{9 + 16}{4\lambda^2} = 25\), so \(\lambda^2 = \tfrac14\), \(\lambda = \pm\tfrac12\). Points: \((3,4)\) and \((-3,-4)\).

\(f(3,4) = 25\) (maximum), \(f(-3,-4) = -25\) (minimum). Geometrically, \(3x+4y\) is \(5\times\) the component of the position vector along \(\langle 3,4\rangle/5\), maximised when the point lies in that direction.

Example 3.9

A box with edges \(x,y,z\gt 0\) must satisfy \(x + 2y + 3z = 18\). Maximise its volume.

Show solution

\(f = xyz\), \(g = x + 2y + 3z\). \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\):

\[ yz = \lambda, \qquad xz = 2\lambda, \qquad xy = 3\lambda . \]

Dividing the second by the first (all variables positive): \(x/y = 2\), so \(x = 2y\). Dividing the third by the first: \(x/z = 3\), so \(x = 3z\). Then \(x + 2y + 3z = x + x + x = 18\), giving \(x = 6\), \(y = 3\), \(z = 2\), and \(V = 36\).

This is the maximum: on the boundary of the allowed region (some edge \(\to 0\)) the volume tends to 0, and the volume is positive inside, so the single interior candidate is the maximum.

Practice problems

  1. Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}\) does not exist.
    Answer

    Along the \(x\)-axis (\(y=0\)): value \(1\). Along the \(y\)-axis (\(x = 0\)): value \(-1\). Different, so no limit.

  2. Show that \(u(x,y) = \ln(x^2+y^2)\) satisfies Laplace's equation \(u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0\) away from the origin. (Up to a constant, this is the potential of an infinite line charge.)
    Answer

    \(u_x = \dfrac{2x}{x^2+y^2}\), \(u_{xx} = \dfrac{2(x^2+y^2) - 4x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \dfrac{2y^2 - 2x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\). By symmetry \(u_{yy} = \dfrac{2x^2-2y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\). The sum is 0.

  3. Find the directional derivative of \(f(x,y) = xe^y\) at \((2,0)\) in the direction toward \((5,4)\).
    Answer

    \(\nabla f = \langle e^y, xe^y\rangle = \langle 1, 2\rangle\) at \((2,0)\). Direction \(\langle 3,4\rangle\), unit \(\langle \tfrac35,\tfrac45\rangle\). \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \tfrac35 + \tfrac85 = \tfrac{11}{5}\).

  4. Find and classify the critical points of \(f(x,y) = 2x^3 + y^2 - 6xy\).
    Answer

    \(f_x = 6x^2 - 6y = 0\Rightarrow y = x^2\); \(f_y = 2y - 6x = 0\Rightarrow y = 3x\). So \(x^2 = 3x\): points \((0,0)\), \((3,9)\). \(D = (12x)(2) - 36 = 24x - 36\). At \((0,0)\): \(D = -36\), saddle. At \((3,9)\): \(D = 36\gt0\), \(f_{xx} = 36\gt 0\), local min, \(f = 54 + 81 - 162 = -27\).

  5. Use Lagrange multipliers to find the point on the plane \(x + 2y + 2z = 9\) closest to the origin.
    Answer

    Minimise \(x^2+y^2+z^2\): \(2x = \lambda\), \(2y = 2\lambda\), \(2z = 2\lambda\), so \(y = z = 2x\). Then \(x + 4x + 4x = 9\Rightarrow x = 1\). Point \((1,2,2)\), distance 3 (agrees with \(|{-9}|/\sqrt{1+4+4} = 3\)).

  6. Two resistors in parallel satisfy \(1/R = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2\). If \(R_1 = 100\pm1\ \Omega\) and \(R_2 = 400\pm 4\ \Omega\), use differentials to estimate the maximum error in \(R\).
    Answer

    \(R = 80\ \Omega\). Differentiating implicitly, \(-R^{-2}dR = -R_1^{-2}dR_1 - R_2^{-2}dR_2\), so \(dR = (R/R_1)^2dR_1 + (R/R_2)^2 dR_2 = 0.64\,dR_1 + 0.04\,dR_2\). Max error \(\approx 0.64(1) + 0.04(4) = 0.8\ \Omega\) (1%).

4. Multiple integrals

Stewart Ch. 15 · OpenStax Vol. 3 Ch. 5

A single integral adds up \(f(x)\,dx\) along a line. A double integral adds up \(f(x,y)\,dA\) over a region in the plane, and a triple integral adds up \(f\,dV\) over a solid. The interpretation depends on what \(f\) is: height (giving volume), density (giving mass or charge), or 1 (giving area or volume). In practice, every multiple integral is computed as a stack of ordinary integrals (iterated integrals), and the real skill is describing the region with the right limits — often in a coordinate system (polar, cylindrical, spherical, or a custom one) that matches the region's symmetry. When you change coordinates, the area or volume element picks up a scaling factor, the Jacobian.

4.1 Double integrals over rectangles

Definition (Double integral)

Partition the rectangle \(R = [a,b]\times[c,d]\) into small subrectangles \(R_{ij}\) of area \(\Delta A\), pick a sample point \((x_{ij}^*, y_{ij}^*)\) in each, and define

\[ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \lim_{m,n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A \]

when the limit exists. If \(f\ge 0\), this is the volume of the solid between the graph of \(f\) and \(R\).

Theorem (Fubini)

If \(f\) is continuous on \(R = [a,b]\times[c,d]\), then

\[ \iint_R f\,dA = \int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx = \int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy . \]

(It holds more generally for bounded \(f\) whose discontinuities lie on finitely many smooth curves.) For a product, \(\iint_R g(x)h(y)\,dA = \left(\int_a^b g\right)\left(\int_c^d h\right)\).

The average value of \(f\) over \(R\) is \(f_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{1}{A(R)}\iint_R f\,dA\).

Example 4.1

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_R y\sin(xy)\,dA\) where \(R = [1,2]\times[0,\pi/2]\).

Show solution

Integrating in \(y\) first would need integration by parts. Integrating in \(x\) first is immediate, since \(y\) is the derivative of \(xy\) with respect to \(x\):

\[ \int_1^2 y\sin(xy)\,dx = \Big[-\cos(xy)\Big]_{x=1}^{x=2} = \cos y - \cos 2y . \] \[ \int_0^{\pi/2}(\cos y - \cos 2y)\,dy = \Big[\sin y - \tfrac12\sin 2y\Big]_0^{\pi/2} = (1 - 0) - 0 = 1 . \]

4.2 Double integrals over general regions

Definition (Type I and Type II regions)

Type I (vertically simple): \(D = \{a\le x\le b,\ g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x)\}\), so \(\iint_D f\,dA = \int_a^b\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f\,dy\,dx\).

Type II (horizontally simple): \(D = \{c\le y\le d,\ h_1(y)\le x\le h_2(y)\}\), so \(\iint_D f\,dA = \int_c^d\int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)} f\,dx\,dy\).

Properties: linearity; \(\iint_D 1\,dA = A(D)\); additivity over regions that overlap only on boundaries; if \(f\le g\) then \(\iint f\le\iint g\).

Study tip

Always sketch the region. For a Type I description, draw a vertical arrow through the region: where it enters is the lower \(y\)-limit, where it leaves is the upper. The outer limits must be constants. If the inner integral has no elementary antiderivative (e.g. \(e^{y^2}\), \(\sin(x^2)\)), try reversing the order.

Common mistake

When reversing the order you cannot just swap the limits. Reconstruct the region from the original limits, sketch it, then describe it the other way. The outer limits of the new integral must be numbers, never variables.

Example 4.2

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_D xy\,dA\) where \(D\) is the region between \(y = x^2\) and \(y = 2x\).

Show solution

The curves meet where \(x^2 = 2x\), i.e. \(x = 0, 2\). For \(0\lt x\lt 2\), \(x^2\lt 2x\), so \(D = \{0\le x\le2,\ x^2\le y\le 2x\}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^2\!\!\int_{x^2}^{2x} xy\,dy\,dx &= \int_0^2 x\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_{x^2}^{2x}dx = \frac12\int_0^2 x\left(4x^2 - x^4\right)dx \\ &= \frac12\int_0^2\left(4x^3 - x^5\right)dx = \frac12\left[x^4 - \frac{x^6}{6}\right]_0^2 = \frac12\left(16 - \frac{32}{3}\right) = \frac83 . \end{aligned} \]

Example 4.3

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^4\!\!\int_{\sqrt x}^{2}\cos(y^3)\,dy\,dx\).

Show solution

\(\cos(y^3)\) has no elementary antiderivative, so reverse the order. The region is \(0\le x\le 4\), \(\sqrt x\le y\le 2\): bounded by the curve \(y = \sqrt x\) (i.e. \(x = y^2\)), the line \(y=2\) and the \(y\)-axis. Described horizontally: \(0\le y\le 2\), \(0\le x\le y^2\).

\[ \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^{y^2}\cos(y^3)\,dx\,dy = \int_0^2 y^2\cos(y^3)\,dy = \left[\tfrac13\sin(y^3)\right]_0^2 = \tfrac13\sin 8 \approx 0.330 . \]

4.3 Double integrals in polar coordinates

Polar coordinates

\[ x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta,\quad r^2 = x^2+y^2, \qquad dA = r\,dr\,d\theta \] \[ \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA = \int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_{h_1(\theta)}^{h_2(\theta)} f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta \]

Where the extra \(r\) comes from. A small "polar rectangle" between radii \(r\) and \(r + \Delta r\) and angles \(\theta\) and \(\theta+\Delta\theta\) is nearly a rectangle with sides \(\Delta r\) (radial) and \(r\,\Delta\theta\) (an arc). Its area is \(\approx r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\). Further from the origin, the same \(\Delta\theta\) sweeps a longer arc.

Common mistake

Forgetting the factor \(r\) is the single most common error in this chapter. A quick check: \(\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a r\,dr\,d\theta = \pi a^2\), the area of a disk. Without the \(r\) you would get \(2\pi a\), which has the wrong units.

Example 4.4

Find the volume of the solid under \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\) and above the \(xy\)-plane.

Show solution

The paraboloid meets \(z=0\) on the circle \(x^2+y^2 = 4\), so \(D\) is the disk \(r\le 2\). In polar, \(z = 4 - r^2\):

\[ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 (4 - r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\left[2r^2 - \frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 = 2\pi(8 - 4) = 8\pi . \]

Example 4.5 (the Gaussian integral)

Show that \(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt\pi\).

Show solution

\(e^{-x^2}\) has no elementary antiderivative, but squaring the integral turns it into a double integral over the whole plane, which is easy in polar coordinates. Let \(I = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}dx\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} I^2 &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}dx\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-y^2}dy = \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA \\ &= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\left[-\tfrac12 e^{-r^2}\right]_0^\infty = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac12 = \pi . \end{aligned} \]

Since \(I\gt 0\), \(I = \sqrt\pi\). (Here the \(r\) in \(dA\) is exactly what makes the integral doable. This result underlies the normal distribution and countless physics calculations.)

4.4 Applications: mass, moments, centre of mass, surface area

Lamina with density \(\delta(x,y)\)

\[ m = \iint_D \delta\,dA, \qquad M_y = \iint_D x\,\delta\,dA, \qquad M_x = \iint_D y\,\delta\,dA, \qquad (\bar x,\bar y) = \left(\frac{M_y}{m}, \frac{M_x}{m}\right) \] \[ I_x = \iint_D y^2\delta\,dA, \qquad I_y = \iint_D x^2\delta\,dA, \qquad I_0 = \iint_D (x^2+y^2)\,\delta\,dA = I_x + I_y \]

(Note the crossed labels: \(M_y\), the moment about the \(y\)-axis, measures \(x\)-distances.) The same formulas with \(\delta\) a charge density give total charge and dipole moments.

Surface area of a graph \(z = f(x,y)\) over \(D\)

\[ A(S) = \iint_D\sqrt{1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2}\;dA \]

Reason: a patch \(\Delta x\times\Delta y\) of \(D\) lifts to a parallelogram on the tangent plane spanned by \(\langle\Delta x, 0, f_x\Delta x\rangle\) and \(\langle 0,\Delta y, f_y\Delta y\rangle\); their cross product \(\langle -f_x, -f_y, 1\rangle\Delta x\Delta y\) has length \(\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}\,\Delta x\Delta y\). (This is a special case of Section 5.6.)

Example 4.6

A lamina occupies the quarter-disk \(x^2+y^2\le 4\), \(x,y\ge0\), with density \(\delta = x + y\). Find its mass and centre of mass.

Show solution

In polar, \(\delta = r(\cos\theta + \sin\theta)\), with \(0\le r\le 2\), \(0\le\theta\le\pi/2\).

\[ m = \int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^2 r(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \left(\int_0^2 r^2\,dr\right)\left(\int_0^{\pi/2}(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)\,d\theta\right) = \frac83\cdot 2 = \frac{16}{3} . \] \[ \begin{aligned} M_y &= \int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^2 (r\cos\theta)\,r(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \left(\int_0^2 r^3dr\right)\left(\int_0^{\pi/2}(\cos^2\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta)\,d\theta\right) \\ &= 4\left(\frac\pi4 + \frac12\right) = \pi + 2 . \end{aligned} \]

So \(\bar x = \dfrac{\pi+2}{16/3} = \dfrac{3(\pi+2)}{16}\approx 0.964\). The region and density are symmetric under \(x\leftrightarrow y\), so \(\bar y = \bar x\). (For uniform density the centroid would be at \(8/(3\pi)\approx 0.849\); the density grows away from the origin, pulling the centre of mass outward.)

Example 4.7

Find the area of the part of the paraboloid \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\) that lies above the \(xy\)-plane.

Show solution

\(f_x = -2x\), \(f_y = -2y\), so \(\sqrt{1 + 4x^2 + 4y^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4r^2}\). The region is \(r\le 2\).

\[ A = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2\sqrt{1+4r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\left[\frac{(1+4r^2)^{3/2}}{12}\right]_0^2 = \frac{\pi}{6}\left(17\sqrt{17} - 1\right)\approx 36.2 . \]

(Substitution \(u = 1 + 4r^2\), \(du = 8r\,dr\), so \(\int\sqrt u\,\tfrac{du}{8} = \tfrac{1}{12}u^{3/2}\).)

4.5 Triple integrals

Triple integrals are defined by Riemann sums over boxes, and Fubini's theorem again lets us evaluate them as iterated integrals in any of six orders. For a general solid, the most common description is "Type 1": \(E = \{(x,y)\in D,\ u_1(x,y)\le z\le u_2(x,y)\}\), giving

\[ \iiint_E f\,dV = \iint_D\left[\int_{u_1(x,y)}^{u_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\right]dA , \]

where \(D\) is the shadow (projection) of \(E\) on the \(xy\)-plane. Analogous descriptions project onto the \(yz\)- or \(xz\)-plane. Volume is \(V = \iiint_E 1\,dV\), mass \(m = \iiint_E\delta\,dV\), centre of mass \(\bar x = \frac1m\iiint x\,\delta\,dV\) etc., and moment of inertia about the \(z\)-axis \(I_z = \iiint (x^2+y^2)\,\delta\,dV\).

Study tip

Set up a triple integral in two stages: (1) find the bottom and top surfaces in \(z\) (inner limits); (2) find the shadow \(D\) and describe it as a 2D region (outer limits). The shadow's boundary is usually where the top and bottom surfaces meet, or where the top meets a coordinate plane.

Example 4.8

Use a triple integral to find the volume of the tetrahedron bounded by the coordinate planes and \(2x + 3y + z = 6\).

Show solution

Bottom \(z = 0\), top \(z = 6 - 2x - 3y\). The shadow is the triangle in the \(xy\)-plane cut off by \(2x + 3y = 6\): \(0\le x\le 3\), \(0\le y\le (6-2x)/3\).

\[ V = \int_0^3\!\!\int_0^{(6-2x)/3}\!\!\int_0^{6-2x-3y}dz\,dy\,dx = \int_0^3\!\!\int_0^{(6-2x)/3}(6-2x-3y)\,dy\,dx . \]

Inner integral, writing \(c = 6 - 2x\) and upper limit \(c/3\):

\[ \left[cy - \tfrac32 y^2\right]_0^{c/3} = \frac{c^2}{3} - \frac{c^2}{6} = \frac{c^2}{6} . \]

Outer: \(\displaystyle\int_0^3\frac{(6-2x)^2}{6}dx\). With \(u = 6-2x\), \(dx = -du/2\): \(\displaystyle\int_0^6\frac{u^2}{12}du = \frac{216}{36} = 6\).

Check: a tetrahedron with legs 3, 2, 6 along the axes has volume \(\tfrac16\cdot3\cdot2\cdot6 = 6\).

4.6 Cylindrical & spherical coordinates

Cylindrical \((r,\theta,z)\)

\[ x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta,\quad z = z, \qquad dV = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta \]

Use when the solid has an axis of symmetry: cylinders, paraboloids \(z = x^2+y^2\), cones \(z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) (i.e. \(z = r\)).

Spherical \((\rho,\theta,\phi)\)

\[ x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\quad y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\quad z = \rho\cos\phi, \qquad \rho^2 = x^2+y^2+z^2 \] \[ dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta, \qquad \rho\ge 0,\ 0\le\phi\le\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le 2\pi \]

\(\phi\) is the angle down from the positive \(z\)-axis. Use when the solid is bounded by spheres \(\rho = a\) and cones \(\phi = c\).

Where \(\rho^2\sin\phi\) comes from. A small spherical "box" has edges \(\Delta\rho\) (radial), \(\rho\,\Delta\phi\) (along a meridian, a circle of radius \(\rho\)) and \(\rho\sin\phi\,\Delta\theta\) (along a circle of latitude, whose radius is the distance to the \(z\)-axis, \(\rho\sin\phi\)). Their product is \(\rho^2\sin\phi\,\Delta\rho\,\Delta\phi\,\Delta\theta\).

Common mistake

Physics books usually swap the names: \(\theta\) for the polar angle from the \(z\)-axis and \(\phi\) for the azimuth, and write \(dV = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi\). The geometry is identical; always check which convention a source uses before copying a formula (especially for gradient, divergence and curl in spherical coordinates).

Example 4.9

A solid is bounded below by \(z = x^2 + y^2\) and above by \(z = 4\), with density \(\delta = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). Find its mass.

Show solution

In cylindrical coordinates: \(r^2\le z\le 4\); the shadow is \(r\le 2\); \(\delta = r\).

\[ m = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_{r^2}^4 r\cdot r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_0^2 r^2(4 - r^2)\,dr = 2\pi\left[\frac{4r^3}{3} - \frac{r^5}{5}\right]_0^2 = 2\pi\left(\frac{32}{3} - \frac{32}{5}\right) = \frac{128\pi}{15} . \]

Example 4.10

Find the volume of the "ice-cream cone" inside the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2 = 4\) and above the cone \(z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\).

Show solution

The sphere is \(\rho = 2\). The cone \(z = r\) makes a \(45^\circ\) angle with the \(z\)-axis: \(\phi = \pi/4\). So \(0\le\rho\le 2\), \(0\le\phi\le\pi/4\), \(0\le\theta\le 2\pi\).

\[ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/4}\!\!\int_0^2\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\Big[-\cos\phi\Big]_0^{\pi/4}\cdot\frac{8}{3} = 2\pi\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\frac83 = \frac{8\pi}{3}\left(2 - \sqrt2\right)\approx 4.91 . \]

Example 4.11

Show that a uniform solid ball of mass \(M\) and radius \(a\) has moment of inertia \(I_z = \tfrac25 Ma^2\) about a diameter.

Show solution

Let the constant density be \(\delta\). The distance squared to the \(z\)-axis is \(x^2+y^2 = \rho^2\sin^2\phi\).

\[ \begin{aligned} I_z &= \delta\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^a (\rho^2\sin^2\phi)\,\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta = \delta\cdot 2\pi\cdot\frac{a^5}{5}\int_0^\pi\sin^3\phi\,d\phi . \end{aligned} \]

With \(\sin^3\phi = (1-\cos^2\phi)\sin\phi\) and \(u = \cos\phi\): \(\int_0^\pi\sin^3\phi\,d\phi = \int_{-1}^1(1-u^2)\,du = \tfrac43\). So \(I_z = \dfrac{8\pi\delta a^5}{15}\).

Since \(M = \tfrac43\pi a^3\delta\): \(\dfrac{I_z}{M} = \dfrac{8\pi a^5/15}{4\pi a^3/3} = \dfrac{2}{5}a^2\). \(\square\)

4.7 Change of variables & Jacobians

Definition (Jacobian)

For a transformation \(T(u,v) = (x(u,v), y(u,v))\), the Jacobian is

\[ \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \begin{vmatrix} \partial x/\partial u & \partial x/\partial v \\ \partial y/\partial u & \partial y/\partial v \end{vmatrix} = x_u y_v - x_v y_u , \]

and similarly a \(3\times3\) determinant for \(\partial(x,y,z)/\partial(u,v,w)\).

Theorem (Change of variables)

Let \(T\) be a \(C^1\) transformation whose Jacobian is nonzero, mapping a region \(S\) in the \(uv\)-plane one-to-one onto \(R\) in the \(xy\)-plane (except perhaps on the boundary). If \(f\) is continuous on \(R\), then

\[ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \iint_S f\big(x(u,v), y(u,v)\big)\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|du\,dv . \]

Why the absolute Jacobian. A tiny rectangle \(\Delta u\times\Delta v\) maps to (approximately) the parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf{r}_u\Delta u\) and \(\mathbf{r}_v\Delta v\), where \(\mathbf{r}_u = \langle x_u, y_u\rangle\). Its area is \(|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v = |x_uy_v - x_vy_u|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v\). For polar coordinates, \(\partial(x,y)/\partial(r,\theta) = \cos\theta\cdot r\cos\theta - (-r\sin\theta)\sin\theta = r\), recovering \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\); the spherical Jacobian works out to \(\rho^2\sin\phi\) in the same way. A useful fact: \(\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)} = 1\Big/\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\), so you can compute whichever is easier.

Study tip

Choose \(u, v\) so the boundary curves become \(u = \) const and \(v = \) const (the region becomes a rectangle), or so the integrand simplifies. Pairs of parallel lines \(ax + by = c_1, c_2\) suggest \(u = ax + by\).

Example 4.12

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_R (x+y)\,e^{x-y}\,dA\), where \(R\) is the parallelogram bounded by \(x+y = 0\), \(x + y = 2\), \(x - y = 0\), \(x - y = 3\).

Show solution

Let \(u = x + y\), \(v = x - y\). Then \(R\) becomes the rectangle \(0\le u\le 2\), \(0\le v\le 3\). Solving: \(x = \tfrac{u+v}{2}\), \(y = \tfrac{u-v}{2}\).

\[ \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \begin{vmatrix}\tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 & -\tfrac12\end{vmatrix} = -\tfrac14 - \tfrac14 = -\tfrac12, \qquad \left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right| = \frac12 . \] \[ \iint_R (x+y)e^{x-y}\,dA = \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^3 u\,e^v\cdot\tfrac12\,dv\,du = \frac12\left(\int_0^2 u\,du\right)\left(\int_0^3 e^v dv\right) = \frac12\cdot 2\cdot(e^3 - 1) = e^3 - 1 . \]

Practice problems

  1. Evaluate \(\iint_D (x+y)\,dA\), where \(D\) is the triangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\), \((1,1)\).
    Answer

    \(D = \{0\le x\le1,\ 0\le y\le x\}\). \(\int_0^1\left[xy + \tfrac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^x dx = \int_0^1\tfrac32x^2\,dx = \tfrac12\).

  2. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_{x^2}^1 x^3\sin(y^3)\,dy\,dx\) by reversing the order.
    Answer

    Region: \(0\le y\le 1\), \(0\le x\le\sqrt y\). \(\int_0^1\sin(y^3)\left[\tfrac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^{\sqrt y}dy = \tfrac14\int_0^1 y^2\sin(y^3)\,dy = \tfrac{1}{12}(1 - \cos 1)\approx 0.0383\).

  3. Evaluate \(\iint_D \dfrac{1}{1+x^2+y^2}\,dA\) over the unit disk.
    Answer

    \(\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1\frac{r}{1+r^2}dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac12\ln(1+r^2)\Big|_0^1 = \pi\ln 2\).

  4. Find the volume of the solid inside the cylinder \(x^2+y^2 = 4\), above \(z = 0\) and below \(z = 5 + y\).
    Answer

    \(\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 (5 + r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\). The \(\sin\theta\) term integrates to 0 over a full turn, leaving \(5\cdot 2\pi\cdot 2 = 20\pi\). (The plane tilts symmetrically about the axis; note \(5 + y\gt 0\) on the disk.)

  5. A ball of radius \(R\) carries charge density \(\rho_c = \rho_0(1 - r/R)\), where \(r\) is the distance from the centre. Find the total charge.
    Answer

    \(Q = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^R\rho_0(1 - \rho/R)\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta = 4\pi\rho_0\left(\tfrac{R^3}{3} - \tfrac{R^3}{4}\right) = \tfrac{\pi\rho_0R^3}{3}\).

  6. Use \(x = au\), \(y = bv\) to show the ellipse \(x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2\le 1\) has area \(\pi ab\).
    Answer

    Jacobian \(\begin{vmatrix} a & 0\\ 0 & b\end{vmatrix} = ab\). The ellipse maps from the unit disk \(u^2+v^2\le1\), so area \(= \iint_{\text{disk}} ab\,du\,dv = ab\cdot\pi\).

5. Vector calculus

Stewart Ch. 16 · OpenStax Vol. 3 Ch. 6

This chapter is where the course pays off for a physicist. A vector field attaches an arrow to every point of space: a force, a velocity, an electric or magnetic field. Two kinds of question dominate. Along a curve: how much does the field push along the path (work, circulation)? That is a line integral. Through a surface: how much of the field passes through it (flux)? That is a surface integral. The local, derivative versions of these ideas are curl (circulation per unit area) and divergence (outflow per unit volume). The great theorems of Green, Stokes and Gauss say that adding up a derivative over a region equals adding up the field over the region's boundary — the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, promoted to higher dimensions. Maxwell's equations are exactly these theorems applied to \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\).

5.1 Vector fields

Definition (Vector field)

A vector field on \(D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^3\) is a function \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle P, Q, R\rangle\) assigning a vector to each point of \(D\). It is continuous (or \(C^1\)) if its component functions are. A field of the form \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla f\) is a gradient field, and \(f\) is a potential function for it; such an \(\mathbf{F}\) is called conservative.

Key examples:

  • Rotation: \(\mathbf{F} = \langle -y, x\rangle\) circles the origin counter-clockwise, with speed growing with distance (a rigidly rotating disc's velocity field).
  • Radial / inverse-square: \(\mathbf{F} = \dfrac{c\,\mathbf{r}}{|\mathbf{r}|^3}\) with \(\mathbf{r} = \langle x,y,z\rangle\), magnitude \(|c|/|\mathbf{r}|^2\). With \(c = q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)\) this is the Coulomb field of a point charge; with \(c = -GMm\) it is Newtonian gravity. It is conservative: \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla\big(-c/|\mathbf{r}|\big)\).
  • Gradient fields \(\nabla f\) are everywhere perpendicular to the level sets of \(f\) (Section 3.5).

A flow line (streamline, field line) is a curve \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) with \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\): it is everywhere tangent to the field.

5.2 Line integrals

Definition (Line integrals)

Let \(C\) be a smooth curve given by \(\mathbf{r}(t)\), \(a\le t\le b\).

Of a scalar function (with respect to arc length):

\[ \int_C f\,ds = \int_a^b f(\mathbf{r}(t))\,|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt . \]

Of a vector field (work, circulation):

\[ \int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt = \int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds = \int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz . \]

For a piecewise-smooth curve, add the integrals over the pieces. If \(C\) is closed, write \(\oint_C\).

Both integrals are independent of the parametrisation chosen, as long as it traces \(C\) once. The difference is orientation: \(\int_{-C} f\,ds = \int_C f\,ds\) (arc length is positive either way), but \(\int_{-C}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) (walking the other way, the field helps instead of hinders). Interpretations: \(\int_C f\,ds\) with \(f\) a linear density is the mass of a wire; \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) is the work done by force \(\mathbf{F}\) on a particle moving along \(C\); around a closed loop it is the circulation (in EM: \(\oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) is the EMF).

Study tip

Recipe: (1) parametrise \(C\) in the correct direction; (2) substitute \(x(t), y(t), z(t)\) into the field; (3) compute \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\); (4) dot and integrate. Standard parametrisations: segment \((1-t)\mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{r}_1\); circle \(\langle a\cos t, a\sin t\rangle\) (counter-clockwise); graph \(y = g(x)\) as \(\langle t, g(t)\rangle\).

Example 5.1

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C (x^2 + y^2 + z)\,ds\), where \(C\) is one turn of the helix \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle\cos t, \sin t, t\rangle\), \(0\le t\le2\pi\).

Show solution

\(\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -\sin t, \cos t, 1\rangle\), so \(|\mathbf{r}'(t)| = \sqrt{2}\). On the curve, \(x^2 + y^2 + z = 1 + t\).

\[ \int_0^{2\pi}(1 + t)\sqrt2\,dt = \sqrt2\left[t + \frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^{2\pi} = \sqrt2\,(2\pi + 2\pi^2) = 2\sqrt2\,\pi(1+\pi)\approx 36.8 . \]

Example 5.2

Let \(\mathbf{F} = \langle y^2, x\rangle\). Compute the work done moving from \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\) along (a) the line \(y = x\) and (b) the parabola \(y = x^2\).

Show solution

(a) \(\mathbf{r} = \langle t,t\rangle\), \(\mathbf{r}' = \langle1,1\rangle\), \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle t^2, t\rangle\):

\[ \int_0^1 (t^2 + t)\,dt = \frac13 + \frac12 = \frac56 . \]

(b) \(\mathbf{r} = \langle t, t^2\rangle\), \(\mathbf{r}' = \langle 1, 2t\rangle\), \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle t^4, t\rangle\):

\[ \int_0^1 (t^4 + 2t^2)\,dt = \frac15 + \frac23 = \frac{13}{15} . \]

Different answers for the same endpoints: the work is path-dependent, so \(\mathbf{F}\) is not conservative. (Consistent with the test in the next section: \(P_y = 2y\neq 1 = Q_x\).)

5.3 The fundamental theorem for line integrals & conservative fields

Theorem (Fundamental theorem for line integrals)

Let \(C\) be a smooth curve \(\mathbf{r}(t)\), \(a\le t\le b\), and let \(f\) be differentiable with \(\nabla f\) continuous on \(C\). Then

\[ \int_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{r}(b)) - f(\mathbf{r}(a)) . \]

Proof. By the chain rule, \(\dfrac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \nabla f(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\). So the line integral is \(\int_a^b\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf{r}(t))\,dt\), and the ordinary FTC finishes it. \(\square\)

Consequences. For a conservative field, the line integral depends only on the endpoints (path independence), and \(\oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0\) for every closed curve. Conversely, on an open connected region, a continuous field whose line integrals are path-independent is conservative (define \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \int_{\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) along any path).

Theorem (Test for a conservative field)

Necessary: if \(\mathbf{F} = \langle P,Q\rangle\) is conservative with \(C^1\) components, then \(\partial P/\partial y = \partial Q/\partial x\). In 3D: \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\).

Sufficient: if \(P, Q\) have continuous first partials on an open, simply connected region \(D\) (connected, with no holes: every closed loop can be shrunk to a point within \(D\)), and \(P_y = Q_x\) throughout \(D\), then \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative on \(D\). In 3D: \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\) on a simply connected domain (for example all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) implies conservative.

The necessary direction is just Clairaut: if \(P = f_x\) and \(Q = f_y\), then \(P_y = f_{xy} = f_{yx} = Q_x\). Example 5.6 below shows why simple connectivity cannot be dropped.

Study tip (finding a potential)

Integrate \(P\) with respect to \(x\), adding an unknown function of the remaining variables: \(f = \int P\,dx + g(y,z)\). Differentiate with respect to \(y\), compare with \(Q\) to find \(g_y\), integrate, and repeat with \(z\). Always check by differentiating your final \(f\).

Physics link (energy conservation). If \(\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U\), the work from \(A\) to \(B\) is \(U(A) - U(B)\). By the work–energy theorem it also equals \(K(B) - K(A)\). Hence \(K + U\) is the same at \(A\) and \(B\): that is why such forces are called "conservative". In electrostatics, \(\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V\) and \(\int_A^B\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = V(A) - V(B)\), the potential difference.

Example 5.3

Show \(\mathbf{F} = \langle 2xy,\ x^2 + z^2,\ 2yz + 1\rangle\) is conservative, find a potential, and compute \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for any path \(C\) from \((1,0,2)\) to \((2,1,1)\).

Show solution

Curl (\(P = 2xy\), \(Q = x^2+z^2\), \(R = 2yz + 1\)):

\[ \operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F} = \langle R_y - Q_z,\ P_z - R_x,\ Q_x - P_y\rangle = \langle 2z - 2z,\ 0 - 0,\ 2x - 2x\rangle = \mathbf{0} . \]

The domain is \(\mathbb{R}^3\) (simply connected), so \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative.

Potential. \(f = \int 2xy\,dx = x^2y + g(y,z)\). Then \(f_y = x^2 + g_y = x^2 + z^2\), so \(g_y = z^2\) and \(g = yz^2 + h(z)\). Then \(f_z = 2yz + h'(z) = 2yz + 1\), so \(h(z) = z\). Hence

\[ f(x,y,z) = x^2y + yz^2 + z . \]

Integral. \(f(2,1,1) = 4 + 1 + 1 = 6\) and \(f(1,0,2) = 0 + 0 + 2 = 2\), so \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 6 - 2 = 4\) for every path.

5.4 Green's theorem

Theorem (Green)

Let \(C\) be a positively oriented (counter-clockwise: region on your left), piecewise-smooth, simple closed curve in the plane, and \(D\) the region it bounds. If \(P\) and \(Q\) have continuous first partial derivatives on an open region containing \(D\), then

\[ \oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_D\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dA . \]

Proof for a rectangle \(D = [a,b]\times[c,d]\). It suffices to show \(\oint_C P\,dx = -\iint_D P_y\,dA\) (the \(Q\) part is identical with the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) swapped). By Fubini and the FTC,

\[ -\iint_D\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\,dA = -\int_a^b\big[P(x,d) - P(x,c)\big]\,dx . \]

Now go around the boundary counter-clockwise. On the bottom edge (\(y = c\), \(x\) from \(a\) to \(b\)) we get \(\int_a^b P(x,c)\,dx\). On the top edge (\(y = d\), \(x\) from \(b\) back to \(a\)) we get \(-\int_a^b P(x,d)\,dx\). On the vertical edges \(x\) is constant, so \(dx = 0\) and \(\int P\,dx = 0\). The total matches. \(\square\)

Any reasonable region can be cut into small rectangles (or Type I/II pieces). The line integrals over shared interior edges are traversed in opposite directions and cancel, leaving only the outer boundary: this cancellation of interior boundaries is the engine behind every theorem in this chapter.

Consequences of Green's theorem

\[ A(D) = \oint_C x\,dy = -\oint_C y\,dx = \frac12\oint_C x\,dy - y\,dx \] \[ \text{Circulation form: } \oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds = \iint_D(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{k}\,dA \qquad \text{Flux form: } \oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds = \iint_D\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}\,dA \]

(\(\mathbf{n}\) is the outward unit normal to \(C\).) For a region with holes, the boundary consists of the outer curve counter-clockwise and each inner curve clockwise, so the region always stays on your left.

Common mistake

Green's theorem needs \(P, Q\) to be \(C^1\) on the whole region \(D\), not just on \(C\). If the field blows up at a point inside \(C\) (like \(\langle -y, x\rangle/(x^2+y^2)\) at the origin), you cannot apply it directly; cut out the bad point with a small circle first.

Example 5.4

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\oint_C xy^2\,dx + (x^2y + 2x)\,dy\) around the unit circle, counter-clockwise, using Green's theorem; then check directly.

Show solution

\(P = xy^2\), \(Q = x^2y + 2x\): \(Q_x - P_y = (2xy + 2) - 2xy = 2\). So

\[ \oint_C = \iint_D 2\,dA = 2\cdot\pi(1)^2 = 2\pi . \]

Direct check. \(x = \cos t\), \(y = \sin t\), \(dx = -\sin t\,dt\), \(dy = \cos t\,dt\):

\[ \int_0^{2\pi}\Big[-\cos t\sin^3 t + \cos^3 t\sin t + 2\cos^2 t\Big]dt . \]

The first two terms integrate to zero over a full period (they are the derivatives of the periodic functions \(-\frac14\sin^4 t\) and \(-\frac14\cos^4 t\)), and \(\int_0^{2\pi}2\cos^2 t\,dt = 2\pi\). Both methods agree.

Example 5.5

Use Green's theorem to find the area of the ellipse \(x = a\cos t\), \(y = b\sin t\).

Show solution \[ A = \frac12\oint_C x\,dy - y\,dx = \frac12\int_0^{2\pi}\big[(a\cos t)(b\cos t) - (b\sin t)(-a\sin t)\big]dt = \frac12\int_0^{2\pi}ab\,dt = \pi ab . \]

(This is the principle of the planimeter: tracing the boundary measures the enclosed area.)

Example 5.6 (why "simply connected" matters)

Let \(\mathbf{F} = \dfrac{\langle -y, x\rangle}{x^2+y^2}\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\). Show \(Q_x = P_y\) everywhere on the domain, yet \(\oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 2\pi\) around the unit circle.

Show solution

\(P = -y/(x^2+y^2)\), \(Q = x/(x^2+y^2)\). By the quotient rule:

\[ Q_x = \frac{(x^2+y^2) - 2x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}, \qquad P_y = \frac{-(x^2+y^2) + 2y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} . \]

Equal. But on the unit circle, \(\mathbf{F} = \langle -\sin t, \cos t\rangle = \mathbf{r}'(t)\), so

\[ \oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi}(\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t)\,dt = 2\pi\neq 0 . \]

So \(\mathbf{F}\) is not conservative on the punctured plane. There is no contradiction: the domain has a hole, so the sufficient test does not apply, and Green's theorem cannot be used on the disk because \(\mathbf{F}\) is undefined at the origin. Locally \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla\theta\) (the polar angle), but \(\theta\) cannot be defined continuously all the way around. In fact, by Green's theorem on the region between \(C\) and any other loop around the origin, every counter-clockwise loop around the origin gives \(2\pi\).

Physics: this is (up to the factor \(\mu_0 I/2\pi\)) the magnetic field of a long straight wire along the \(z\)-axis. It is curl-free outside the wire, yet \(\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \mu_0 I\) around any loop enclosing it — Ampère's law.

5.5 Curl & divergence

Definition (Curl and divergence)

With the operator \(\nabla = \left\langle\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}, \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}, \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\right\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{F} = \langle P,Q,R\rangle\):

\[ \operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F} = \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k}\\ \partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z \\ P & Q & R\end{vmatrix} = \left\langle R_y - Q_z,\ P_z - R_x,\ Q_x - P_y\right\rangle \] \[ \operatorname{div}\mathbf{F} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = P_x + Q_y + R_z, \qquad \nabla^2 f = \nabla\cdot\nabla f = f_{xx} + f_{yy} + f_{zz} \text{ (Laplacian)} \]

Physical meaning. Think of \(\mathbf{F}\) as a fluid velocity.

  • Divergence is the net outflow per unit volume at a point: \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}(P) = \lim\limits_{V\to0}\frac{1}{V}\iint_{S}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\) over small closed surfaces around \(P\). Positive means a source, negative a sink, zero means incompressible.
  • Curl measures local rotation: \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}(P) = \lim\limits_{a\to0}\frac{1}{\pi a^2}\oint_{C_a}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for small circles \(C_a\) around \(P\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf{n}\). A tiny paddle wheel spins fastest when its axle is along \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\), with angular velocity \(\tfrac12|\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}|\). For rigid rotation \(\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf{r}\), \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{v} = 2\boldsymbol\omega\).

Theorem (Two vanishing identities)

If \(f\) has continuous second partials and \(\mathbf{F}\) has continuous second partials, then

\[ \nabla\times(\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) = 0 . \]

Proof. With \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y, f_z\rangle\), the curl formula gives

\[ \nabla\times\nabla f = \langle f_{zy} - f_{yz},\ f_{xz} - f_{zx},\ f_{yx} - f_{xy}\rangle = \mathbf{0} \]

by Clairaut's theorem. Similarly

\[ \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) = (R_y - Q_z)_x + (P_z - R_x)_y + (Q_x - P_y)_z = R_{yx} - Q_{zx} + P_{zy} - R_{xy} + Q_{xz} - P_{yz} = 0 , \]

since the terms cancel in pairs. \(\square\)

So a gradient field is curl-free (irrotational), and a curl field is divergence-free (solenoidal). On simply connected (respectively, suitably simple) domains the converses hold: curl-free fields have scalar potentials, and divergence-free fields have vector potentials \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}\). In EM: \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\) is what lets us write \(\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}\), and in electrostatics \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}\) is what lets us write \(\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V\).

Product rules (all follow from the ordinary product rule)

\[ \nabla\cdot(f\mathbf{F}) = f\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} + \mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla f \qquad \nabla\times(f\mathbf{F}) = f\,\nabla\times\mathbf{F} + \nabla f\times\mathbf{F} \] \[ \nabla\cdot(\mathbf{F}\times\mathbf{G}) = \mathbf{G}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) - \mathbf{F}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{G}) \qquad \nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}) - \nabla^2\mathbf{F} \]

Common mistake

Curl is only defined for 3D fields (for a 2D field \(\langle P, Q\rangle\), treat it as \(\langle P,Q,0\rangle\); the curl is then \((Q_x - P_y)\mathbf{k}\)). Divergence produces a scalar, curl a vector; "div of a scalar" or "grad of a vector field" (in this course) are meaningless, which is a useful sanity check on any identity.

Example 5.7

(a) For \(\mathbf{F} = \langle x^2y,\ yz^2,\ xz\rangle\), find \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}\) and \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\), and verify \(\operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}) = 0\). (b) Show the inverse-square field \(\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r}/|\mathbf{r}|^3\) has zero divergence away from the origin.

Show solution

(a) \(P = x^2y\), \(Q = yz^2\), \(R = xz\).

\[ \operatorname{div}\mathbf{F} = 2xy + z^2 + x . \] \[ \operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F} = \langle R_y - Q_z,\ P_z - R_x,\ Q_x - P_y\rangle = \langle 0 - 2yz,\ 0 - z,\ 0 - x^2\rangle = \langle -2yz, -z, -x^2\rangle . \] \[ \operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}) = \partial_x(-2yz) + \partial_y(-z) + \partial_z(-x^2) = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0 . \]

(b) Let \(\rho = |\mathbf{r}| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\), so \(\partial\rho/\partial x = x/\rho\). Then

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{x}{\rho^3}\right) = \frac{1}{\rho^3} - \frac{3x}{\rho^4}\cdot\frac{x}{\rho} = \frac{1}{\rho^3} - \frac{3x^2}{\rho^5} . \]

Adding the analogous \(y\) and \(z\) terms:

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = \frac{3}{\rho^3} - \frac{3(x^2+y^2+z^2)}{\rho^5} = \frac{3}{\rho^3} - \frac{3}{\rho^3} = 0 \quad (\rho\neq 0). \]

The Coulomb field has no sources except at the charge itself — which will make Gauss's law work (Section 5.9).

5.6 Parametric surfaces & their areas

Definition (Parametric surface)

A parametric surface is the image of a vector function of two variables, \(\mathbf{r}(u,v) = \langle x(u,v), y(u,v), z(u,v)\rangle\), \((u,v)\in D\). The partial derivatives \(\mathbf{r}_u\), \(\mathbf{r}_v\) are tangent to the surface, so

\[ \mathbf{N} = \mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v \]

is a normal vector. The surface is smooth if \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\neq\mathbf{0}\) (on the interior of \(D\)).

Example 5.8

Using the spherical parametrisation, compute \(\mathbf{r}_\phi\times\mathbf{r}_\theta\) for the sphere of radius \(a\), and show its area is \(4\pi a^2\).

Show solution \[ \mathbf{r}_\phi = \langle a\cos\phi\cos\theta,\ a\cos\phi\sin\theta,\ -a\sin\phi\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{r}_\theta = \langle -a\sin\phi\sin\theta,\ a\sin\phi\cos\theta,\ 0\rangle . \]

Components of the cross product:

\[ \begin{aligned} x&:\ (a\cos\phi\sin\theta)(0) - (-a\sin\phi)(a\sin\phi\cos\theta) = a^2\sin^2\phi\cos\theta \\ y&:\ (-a\sin\phi)(-a\sin\phi\sin\theta) - (a\cos\phi\cos\theta)(0) = a^2\sin^2\phi\sin\theta \\ z&:\ (a\cos\phi\cos\theta)(a\sin\phi\cos\theta) - (a\cos\phi\sin\theta)(-a\sin\phi\sin\theta) = a^2\sin\phi\cos\phi \end{aligned} \] \[ \mathbf{r}_\phi\times\mathbf{r}_\theta = a^2\sin\phi\,\langle\sin\phi\cos\theta,\ \sin\phi\sin\theta,\ \cos\phi\rangle = a\sin\phi\;\mathbf{r}(\phi,\theta) . \]

It points radially outward (since \(\sin\phi\ge0\)) with length \(a^2\sin\phi\). Area:

\[ A = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi a^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta = a^2\cdot 2\cdot 2\pi = 4\pi a^2 . \]

5.7 Surface integrals & flux

Definition (Surface integral of a scalar function)

\[ \iint_S f\,dS = \iint_D f(\mathbf{r}(u,v))\,|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\,dA . \]

For a graph \(z = g(x,y)\): \(\iint_S f\,dS = \iint_D f(x,y,g(x,y))\sqrt{1 + g_x^2 + g_y^2}\,dA\). Uses: mass of a shell, total charge on a surface, average value over a surface.

Definition (Orientation and flux)

A surface is orientable if a continuous choice of unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) exists over the whole surface (a Möbius strip is not orientable). Closed surfaces are oriented outward by convention (many texts mark an integral over a closed surface with a circled double-integral sign; these notes just write \(\iint_S\) and say "closed"). The flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across the oriented surface \(S\) is

\[ \iint_S\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iint_S\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iint_D\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(u,v))\cdot(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v)\,dA , \]

where the last form requires \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\) to point in the chosen direction (otherwise negate it). For a graph \(z = g(x,y)\) oriented upward, \(\mathbf{r}_x\times\mathbf{r}_y = \langle -g_x, -g_y, 1\rangle\), so

\[ \iint_S\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iint_D\left(-P\,g_x - Q\,g_y + R\right)dA . \]

If \(\mathbf{F}\) is a fluid velocity, flux is the volume of fluid crossing \(S\) per unit time. In EM, \(\iint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\) is the electric flux and \(\iint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\) the magnetic flux whose rate of change drives Faraday's law. Note the neat cancellation: \(|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\) in \(dS\) cancels the normalisation of \(\mathbf{n}\), so you never need to compute a square root for a flux integral.

Common mistake

Orientation errors flip the sign of a flux. After computing \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\), test it at one convenient point: does it point the way the problem asks (up, outward, away from the axis)? If not, swap the order of the cross product.

Example 5.9

Evaluate \(\iint_S z\,dS\) over the upper hemisphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2 = a^2\), \(z\ge 0\).

Show solution

Spherical parametrisation with \(0\le\phi\le\pi/2\): \(z = a\cos\phi\), \(dS = a^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta\).

\[ \iint_S z\,dS = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}a\cos\phi\cdot a^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta = 2\pi a^3\left[\tfrac12\sin^2\phi\right]_0^{\pi/2} = \pi a^3 . \]

Dividing by the area \(2\pi a^2\): the average height on the hemisphere is \(a/2\).

Example 5.10 (flux of a point charge)

Compute the outward flux of \(\mathbf{E} = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{\mathbf{r}}{|\mathbf{r}|^3}\) through the sphere \(|\mathbf{r}| = a\).

Show solution

On the sphere the outward unit normal is \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{r}/a\), and \(|\mathbf{r}| = a\), so

\[ \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{n} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}}{a^3\cdot a} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2}, \]

a constant. Hence the flux is this constant times the area:

\[ \iint_S\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^2}\cdot 4\pi a^2 = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0} . \]

The radius cancels: the \(1/a^2\) decay of the field exactly balances the \(a^2\) growth of the area. This is Gauss's law for a sphere; Section 5.9 extends it to any closed surface.

Example 5.11

Find the upward flux of \(\mathbf{F} = \langle y, x, z\rangle\) through the paraboloid \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\), \(z\ge0\).

Show solution

\(g = 4 - x^2 - y^2\), \(g_x = -2x\), \(g_y = -2y\). Upward normal \(\langle -g_x, -g_y, 1\rangle = \langle 2x, 2y, 1\rangle\).

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\langle 2x,2y,1\rangle = 2xy + 2xy + z = 4xy + 4 - x^2 - y^2 . \]

Over the disk \(r\le 2\) in polar coordinates, \(4xy = 4r^2\cos\theta\sin\theta\) integrates to zero over \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\). What remains:

\[ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2(4 - r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 8\pi \]

(the same integral as Example 4.4). We will confirm this with the Divergence Theorem in Example 5.14.

5.8 Stokes' theorem

Theorem (Stokes)

Let \(S\) be an oriented, piecewise-smooth surface bounded by a simple, closed, piecewise-smooth curve \(C\) with positive orientation (walking along \(C\) with your head in the direction of \(\mathbf{n}\), the surface is on your left). If \(\mathbf{F}\) has continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing \(S\), then

\[ \oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot d\mathbf{S} . \]

Meaning. Circulation around the edge equals the total "microscopic circulation" (curl) through the surface. As with Green's theorem, chop \(S\) into tiny patches; the circulation around each is \(\approx(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\Delta S\), and the line integrals over shared edges cancel, leaving only the outer boundary. Green's theorem is the special case where \(S\) is a flat region in the \(xy\)-plane with \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{k}\).

Surface independence. The right side depends only on the boundary curve: any two surfaces with the same oriented boundary give the same flux of a curl. So you may replace a complicated surface by a simple one (often a flat disk) with the same edge. And if \(S\) is closed (no boundary), \(\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0\).

Physics link. Faraday's law \(\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\frac{d}{dt}\iint_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\) becomes, by Stokes, \(\iint_S\left(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} + \partial_t\mathbf{B}\right)\cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0\) for every surface, hence \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t\). Ampère's law \(\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} = \mu_0\iint\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\) becomes \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J}\) the same way.

Example 5.12

Verify Stokes' theorem for \(\mathbf{F} = \langle -y, x, xz\rangle\) on the paraboloid \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\), \(z\ge0\), oriented upward.

Show solution

Line integral. The boundary is the circle \(x^2+y^2 = 4\), \(z = 0\); upward orientation means counter-clockwise viewed from above: \(\mathbf{r} = \langle 2\cos t, 2\sin t, 0\rangle\). Then \(\mathbf{F} = \langle -2\sin t, 2\cos t, 0\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{r}' = \langle -2\sin t, 2\cos t, 0\rangle\):

\[ \oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi}(4\sin^2 t + 4\cos^2 t)\,dt = 8\pi . \]

Surface integral.

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \langle R_y - Q_z,\ P_z - R_x,\ Q_x - P_y\rangle = \langle 0 - 0,\ 0 - z,\ 1 - (-1)\rangle = \langle 0, -z, 2\rangle . \]

With the upward normal \(\langle 2x, 2y, 1\rangle\) from Example 5.11: \((\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\langle 2x,2y,1\rangle = -2yz + 2 = -2y(4 - x^2 - y^2) + 2\). The first term is odd in \(y\) over the symmetric disk, so it integrates to 0, leaving

\[ \iint_D 2\,dA = 2\cdot\pi(2)^2 = 8\pi . \]

Both sides equal \(8\pi\).

Example 5.13

Let \(C\) be the curve where the plane \(y + z = 3\) cuts the cylinder \(x^2+y^2 = 1\), oriented counter-clockwise when viewed from above. Use Stokes' theorem to evaluate \(\oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for \(\mathbf{F} = \langle 2z, x, y^2\rangle\).

Show solution

Take \(S\) to be the flat elliptical piece of the plane inside the cylinder, oriented upward. As a graph \(z = 3 - y\) over the unit disk \(D\), the upward normal is \(\langle -g_x, -g_y, 1\rangle = \langle 0, 1, 1\rangle\).

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \langle R_y - Q_z,\ P_z - R_x,\ Q_x - P_y\rangle = \langle 2y - 0,\ 2 - 0,\ 1 - 0\rangle = \langle 2y, 2, 1\rangle . \] \[ \oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_D\langle 2y,2,1\rangle\cdot\langle 0,1,1\rangle\,dA = \iint_D 3\,dA = 3\pi . \]

Direct check. \(\mathbf{r} = \langle\cos t, \sin t, 3 - \sin t\rangle\), \(\mathbf{r}' = \langle -\sin t, \cos t, -\cos t\rangle\), \(\mathbf{F} = \langle 6 - 2\sin t,\ \cos t,\ \sin^2 t\rangle\):

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{r}' = -6\sin t + 2\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t - \sin^2 t\cos t . \]

Over \([0,2\pi]\): \(0 + 2\pi + \pi - 0 = 3\pi\). Agreed.

5.9 The Divergence theorem

Theorem (Divergence / Gauss)

Let \(E\) be a simple solid region whose boundary surface \(S\) is piecewise smooth and oriented outward. If \(\mathbf{F}\) has continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing \(E\), then

\[ \iint_S\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_E\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,dV . \]

Proof sketch. For a small box \([x, x+\Delta x]\times[y,y+\Delta y]\times[z,z+\Delta z]\), the outward flux through the two faces perpendicular to \(x\) is \(\approx\big[P(x+\Delta x,\cdot) - P(x,\cdot)\big]\Delta y\Delta z\approx P_x\,\Delta V\); likewise \(Q_y\,\Delta V\) and \(R_z\,\Delta V\) from the other pairs. So the flux out of each little box is \(\approx(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F})\,\Delta V\). Fill \(E\) with boxes: each interior face is shared by two boxes with opposite outward normals, so those fluxes cancel, leaving only the flux through \(S\). (A rigorous proof for a "type 1" region proves \(\iint R\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint R_z\,dV\) using the FTC in \(z\), exactly as in Green's theorem.)

Regions with holes. If \(E\) lies between an outer surface \(S_2\) and an inner surface \(S_1\), the theorem still holds with \(S_1\) oriented toward the hole (outward from \(E\)). Hence: if \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 0\) in \(E\), the outward flux through \(S_2\) equals the outward flux through \(S_1\) (each with its normal pointing away from the hole).

Application (Gauss's law)

Let \(S\) be any closed surface enclosing the origin, and \(\mathbf{E}\) the field of a point charge \(q\) at the origin. Choose a small sphere \(S_1\) around the origin inside \(S\). Between them, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0\) (Example 5.7b), so the flux through \(S\) equals the flux through \(S_1\), which is \(q/\varepsilon_0\) (Example 5.10). If \(S\) does not enclose the charge, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0\) throughout the inside and the flux is 0. By superposition, for any charge distribution,

\[ \iint_S\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\iiint_E\rho\,dV \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \iiint_E\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} - \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\right)dV = 0 \text{ for every } E \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} . \]

The same move — "integral law for every region, so the integrands match" — turns the conservation of charge \(\frac{d}{dt}\iiint\rho\,dV = -\iint\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\) into the continuity equation \(\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0\).

Study tip

Use the Divergence theorem when the surface is closed and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\) is simpler than \(\mathbf{F}\) (often a constant or a function of \(\rho\)). If the surface is open (say a paraboloid cap), close it off with a flat lid, apply the theorem, and subtract the flux through the lid.

Example 5.14

Confirm the answer to Example 5.11 using the Divergence theorem.

Show solution

Close the paraboloid with the disk \(S_0\): \(z = 0\), \(x^2+y^2\le4\), oriented downward (\(\mathbf{n} = -\mathbf{k}\)), so together they bound the solid \(E\) under the paraboloid. \(\nabla\cdot\langle y, x, z\rangle = 0 + 0 + 1 = 1\), so

\[ \iint_{S\cup S_0}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_E 1\,dV = \text{vol}(E) = 8\pi \quad\text{(Example 4.4)} . \]

On the lid, \(\mathbf{F}\cdot(-\mathbf{k}) = -z = 0\), so the lid contributes nothing, and the flux through the paraboloid is \(8\pi\), as found directly.

Example 5.15

Find the outward flux of \(\mathbf{F} = \langle x^3, y^3, z^3\rangle\) through the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2 = a^2\).

Show solution

A direct surface integral would be messy. Instead, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 3x^2 + 3y^2 + 3z^2 = 3\rho^2\):

\[ \iint_S\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^a 3\rho^2\cdot\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta = 3\cdot\frac{a^5}{5}\cdot 2\cdot 2\pi = \frac{12\pi a^5}{5} . \]

5.10 The big picture: one theorem in many guises

Every major theorem of the course has the same shape: the integral of a derivative over a region equals the integral of the original function over the boundary of the region, with the boundary carrying the induced orientation.

Study tip (which tool?)

  • Line integral of a field with zero curl on a simply connected domain: find a potential (FTLI).
  • Closed plane curve: Green. Closed space curve: Stokes (pick the easiest spanning surface).
  • Flux of a curl through an open surface: Stokes (replace by a line integral, or by a simpler surface with the same boundary).
  • Flux through a closed surface: Divergence theorem. Through an open surface with a messy field: close it, then subtract the lid.
  • Field singular inside the region: excise a small sphere or circle around the singularity.

    Answer

    The boundary is the unit circle in \(z = 0\), counter-clockwise from above: \(\mathbf{r} = \langle\cos t, \sin t, 0\rangle\). There \(\mathbf{F} = \langle -\sin t, \cos t, 1\rangle\), \(\mathbf{r}' = \langle -\sin t, \cos t, 0\rangle\), dot product 1. Answer: \(2\pi\). (The awkward \(e^{xz}\) never needs to be differentiated.)
