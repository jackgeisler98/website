1. Measurement, units & dimensional analysis

Halliday Ch. 1 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 1

Physics makes quantitative predictions, and a number without a unit is not a measurement. Every physical quantity is a number times a unit, and every equation must balance in units as well as in numbers. That single requirement is surprisingly powerful: it catches algebra errors instantly, and it can even tell you the form of a law (how a pendulum's period depends on its length, for instance) before you solve anything. Treat units as algebraic symbols that multiply, divide and cancel.

1.1 SI units & conversions

Definition (SI base units)

The International System (SI) builds every unit from seven base units: the metre (m, length), kilogram (kg, mass), second (s, time), ampere (A, current), kelvin (K, temperature), mole (mol, amount) and candela (cd, luminous intensity). Since 2019 all seven are defined by fixing exact values of constants of nature: for example the second via the caesium hyperfine frequency, the metre via \(c = 299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s}\), and the kilogram via Planck's constant \(h\).

Mechanics needs only m, kg and s. Everything else is derived: newton \(1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2\), joule \(1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}\), watt \(1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}\), pascal \(1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2\). Prefixes scale by powers of ten: n (\(10^{-9}\)), \(\mu\) (\(10^{-6}\)), m (\(10^{-3}\)), k (\(10^{3}\)), M (\(10^{6}\)), G (\(10^{9}\)).

Chain-link conversion. Multiply by fractions equal to one, arranged so unwanted units cancel. For example \(1\ \text{g/cm}^3 = (1\ \text{g/cm}^3)(1\ \text{kg}/1000\ \text{g})(100\ \text{cm}/1\ \text{m})^3 = 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3\). Note that the conversion factor gets cubed along with the unit.

Example 1.1

A test-stand fuel line delivers 38 gallons per minute (US gallon \(= 3.785\ \text{L}\)). Express the flow in \(\text{m}^3/\text{s}\), and find the mass flow rate if the fuel density is \(0.81\ \text{g/cm}^3\).

Show solution

Volume flow, using \(1\ \text{L} = 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3\):

\[ Q = 38\,\frac{\text{gal}}{\text{min}}\cdot\frac{3.785\ \text{L}}{1\ \text{gal}}\cdot\frac{10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3}{1\ \text{L}}\cdot\frac{1\ \text{min}}{60\ \text{s}} = 2.40\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}. \]

Density in SI: \(0.81\ \text{g/cm}^3 = 810\ \text{kg/m}^3\). Mass flow \(\dot m = \rho Q = 810 \times 2.40\times10^{-3} = 1.94\ \text{kg/s}\).

Check: units \(\text{kg/m}^3 \cdot \text{m}^3/\text{s} = \text{kg/s}\). About 2 kg each second is plausible for a 38 gal/min line.

1.2 Dimensional analysis

Principle (Dimensional homogeneity)

Every term in a valid physical equation has the same dimensions, written in terms of mass \([M]\), length \([L]\) and time \([T]\). Arguments of functions such as \(\sin\), \(e^x\) and \(\ln\) must be dimensionless.

Some useful dimensions: velocity \([L T^{-1}]\), acceleration \([L T^{-2}]\), force \([M L T^{-2}]\), energy \([M L^2 T^{-2}]\), power \([M L^2 T^{-3}]\), pressure \([M L^{-1} T^{-2}]\), the gravitational constant \(G\): \([M^{-1} L^3 T^{-2}]\).

To find a law, assume the quantity is a product of powers of the variables it could depend on, then match exponents of \(M\), \(L\), \(T\). The method cannot supply dimensionless constants (like \(2\pi\)) and fails if you leave out a relevant variable, but it is fast and often exactly right up to a constant.

Example 1.2

The period \(T\) of a simple pendulum might depend on its length \(\ell\), bob mass \(m\) and gravitational acceleration \(g\). Use dimensional analysis to find the form of \(T\).

Show solution

Assume \(T = C\,\ell^a m^b g^c\) with \(C\) dimensionless. Dimensions:

\[ [T^1] = [L]^a [M]^b [L T^{-2}]^c = [L^{a+c} M^b T^{-2c}]. \]

Match exponents: \(T\): \(1 = -2c \Rightarrow c = -\tfrac12\); \(M\): \(b = 0\); \(L\): \(a + c = 0 \Rightarrow a = \tfrac12\). So

\[ T = C\sqrt{\ell/g}. \]

The mass drops out entirely, a genuine physical prediction. Solving the equation of motion (Chapter 15) gives \(C = 2\pi\) for small swings.

Study tip

Make a habit of checking units on every final symbolic answer before plugging in numbers. If \(v\) comes out as \(\sqrt{g/h}\) you have made an error, since that has units of \(\text{s}^{-1}\), not \(\text{m/s}\).

1.3 Significant figures & estimation

Report results to roughly the precision of the least precise input: in multiplication and division keep the fewest significant figures among the factors; in addition and subtraction keep the fewest decimal places. Carry extra digits through intermediate steps and round only at the end. In these notes \(g = 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2\) unless stated otherwise.

Order-of-magnitude (Fermi) estimates replace exact data with round numbers to get an answer within a factor of a few. They are invaluable for sanity-checking test data and simulation outputs: if a computed load is \(10^3\) times your estimate, something is wrong.

Common mistake

Converting areas and volumes with a linear factor: \(1\ \text{m}^2 = 10^4\ \text{cm}^2\), not \(100\ \text{cm}^2\); \(1\ \text{m}^3 = 10^6\ \text{cm}^3 = 1000\ \text{L}\).

Example 1.3

Estimate the mass of air in a room 5 m × 4 m × 2.5 m (air density about \(1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3\)).

Show solution

\(V = 5 \times 4 \times 2.5 = 50\ \text{m}^3\), so \(m = \rho V \approx 1.2 \times 50 = 60\ \text{kg}\), roughly the mass of a person. Air is light per litre but rooms are big.

Practice problems

  1. Show that \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x\) is dimensionally consistent.
    Answer

    \([v^2] = L^2T^{-2}\); \([a\,\Delta x] = (L T^{-2})(L) = L^2T^{-2}\). All terms match (the 2 is dimensionless).

  2. The speed of deep-water ocean waves depends only on \(g\) and the wavelength \(\lambda\). Find the form of \(v\).
    Answer

    \(v = C g^a\lambda^b\): \(L T^{-1} = L^{a+b}T^{-2a}\), so \(a = \tfrac12\), \(b = \tfrac12\): \(v = C\sqrt{g\lambda}\). (Exact theory gives \(C = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}\).)

  3. Express one light-year (distance light travels in one year, \(1\ \text{yr} = 3.156\times10^7\ \text{s}\)) in metres.
    Answer

    \(d = c\,t = (2.998\times10^8)(3.156\times10^7) = 9.46\times10^{15}\ \text{m}\).

  4. Aerodynamic drag is \(D = \tfrac12 C\rho A v^2\). Show that the drag coefficient \(C\) is dimensionless.
    Answer

    \([\rho A v^2] = (M L^{-3})(L^2)(L^2T^{-2}) = M L T^{-2}\), which is already a force, so \(C\) carries no dimensions.

  5. Escape speed from a planet could depend on \(G\), the planet's mass \(M\) and radius \(R\). Find its form.
    Answer

    \(v = C G^a M^b R^c\): \(M\): \(-a + b = 0\); \(T\): \(-2a = -1\); \(L\): \(3a + c = 1\). So \(a = b = \tfrac12\), \(c = -\tfrac12\): \(v = C\sqrt{GM/R}\) (exact: \(C = \sqrt2\), Chapter 13).

  6. Convert a pressure of 14.7 lbf/in\(^2\) to pascals (1 lbf = 4.448 N, 1 in = 0.0254 m).
    Answer

    \(14.7 \times 4.448 / (0.0254)^2 = 65.39/6.452\times10^{-4} = 1.013\times10^5\ \text{Pa}\), one standard atmosphere.

2. Motion in one dimension

Halliday Ch. 2 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 3

Kinematics describes motion without asking what causes it. In one dimension the whole story is a single function \(x(t)\): its first derivative is velocity and its second is acceleration. Calculus runs both ways: differentiate position to get acceleration, or integrate acceleration (with initial conditions) to recover position. The familiar "constant-acceleration equations" are nothing more than these integrals done once for the special case \(a = \text{const}\).

2.1 Position, velocity & acceleration

Definitions

Displacement \(\Delta x = x_2 - x_1\) (signed). Average velocity \(v_{\text{avg}} = \Delta x/\Delta t\). Average speed = total distance / \(\Delta t\) (never negative, and not the magnitude of average velocity in general).

\[ v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt}, \qquad a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}. \]

Graphically, \(v\) is the slope of the \(x\)–\(t\) curve and \(a\) is the slope of the \(v\)–\(t\) curve; conversely, displacement is the signed area under \(v(t)\), and change in velocity is the area under \(a(t)\). An object speeds up when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign and slows down when they have opposite signs; the sign of \(a\) alone does not tell you.

2.2 Constant acceleration

Derivation. With \(a\) constant, integrate \(dv/dt = a\) from \(0\) to \(t\): \(v = v_0 + at\). Integrate again, \(dx/dt = v_0 + at\): \(x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2\). To eliminate time use the chain rule, \(a = \dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{dv}{dx}\dfrac{dx}{dt} = v\dfrac{dv}{dx}\), so \(\int_{v_0}^{v} v\,dv = \int_{x_0}^{x} a\,dx\) gives \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)\).

Constant-acceleration equations

\[ \begin{aligned} v &= v_0 + at & x - x_0 &= v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2 \\ v^2 &= v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0) & x - x_0 &= \tfrac12(v_0 + v)\,t \end{aligned} \]

Each equation omits one of \(x - x_0,\ v,\ t,\ a\) (the last omits \(a\); a fifth form \(x - x_0 = vt - \tfrac12at^2\) omits \(v_0\)). Pick the one that omits the quantity you neither know nor want.

Common mistake

Using these equations when \(a\) is not constant (drag, springs, a thrust profile). Also mixing sign conventions: choose a positive direction once and give every vector quantity (\(x, v, a\)) a sign relative to it.

Example 2.1

A car travels at \(27\ \text{m/s}\). The driver reacts in \(0.75\ \text{s}\), then brakes at a constant \(6.0\ \text{m/s}^2\). Find the total stopping distance and time.

Show solution

Knowns: \(v_0 = 27\ \text{m/s}\), \(t_r = 0.75\ \text{s}\), \(a = -6.0\ \text{m/s}^2\), final \(v = 0\).

Reaction phase (constant speed): \(d_1 = v_0 t_r = 27(0.75) = 20.3\ \text{m}\).

Braking phase: from \(0 = v_0^2 + 2a d_2\), \(d_2 = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2|a|} = \dfrac{729}{12} = 60.8\ \text{m}\), taking \(t_2 = v_0/|a| = 4.5\ \text{s}\).

Total: \(d = 81\ \text{m}\) in \(5.25\ \text{s}\).

Check: braking distance scales as \(v_0^2\), so doubling speed quadruples \(d_2\), the physics behind speed limits near schools.

2.3 Free fall

Near Earth's surface, neglecting air resistance, every object accelerates downward at \(g \approx 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2\) regardless of mass. With \(y\) up, \(a = -g\) and the constant-acceleration equations apply. At the peak of a vertical throw \(v = 0\) but \(a = -g\) still.

Example 2.2

A ball is thrown straight up at \(15\ \text{m/s}\) from a point \(2.0\ \text{m}\) above the ground. Find its maximum height above the ground and the time until it lands.

Show solution

Take \(y = 0\) at the ground, up positive: \(y_0 = 2.0\ \text{m}\), \(v_0 = 15\ \text{m/s}\), \(a = -g\).

Peak (\(v = 0\)): \(y_{\max} - y_0 = v_0^2/(2g) = 225/19.6 = 11.5\ \text{m}\), so \(y_{\max} = 13.5\ \text{m}\).

Landing (\(y = 0\)): \(0 = y_0 + v_0 t - \tfrac12 g t^2 \Rightarrow 4.9t^2 - 15t - 2.0 = 0\).

\[ t = \frac{15 + \sqrt{15^2 + 4(4.9)(2.0)}}{2(4.9)} = \frac{15 + 16.25}{9.8} = 3.19\ \text{s}. \]

The negative root (\(-0.13\ \text{s}\)) is the time the ball would have left the ground if launched from there, and is discarded. Check: without the 2 m head start the flight would last \(2v_0/g = 3.06\ \text{s}\); a slightly longer time makes sense.

2.4 Variable acceleration

General kinematics by integration

\[ v(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t a(t')\,dt', \qquad x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t v(t')\,dt'. \]

If \(a\) depends on velocity, separate variables: \(dt = dv/a(v)\). If \(a\) depends on position, use \(v\,dv = a(x)\,dx\).

Example 2.3

A rocket sled starts from rest at \(x = 0\) with thrust tapering so that \(a(t) = 12 - 3t\) (SI units) for \(0 \le t \le 4\ \text{s}\). Find its speed and position at \(t = 4\ \text{s}\).

Show solution \[ v(t) = \int_0^t (12 - 3t')\,dt' = 12t - 1.5t^2, \qquad x(t) = \int_0^t v\,dt' = 6t^2 - 0.5t^3. \]

At \(t = 4\ \text{s}\): \(v = 48 - 24 = 24\ \text{m/s}\) and \(x = 96 - 32 = 64\ \text{m}\).

Check: the average acceleration is \(24/4 = 6\ \text{m/s}^2\), the mean of the initial 12 and final 0, as expected for a linear ramp. A constant \(6\ \text{m/s}^2\) would give \(x = 48\ \text{m}\); the sled covers more because its acceleration is front-loaded.

Example 2.4

A boat coasting with its engine off experiences \(a = -kv\) (linear water drag). If it starts at \(v_0\), find \(v(t)\) and the total distance it coasts.

Show solution

Separate: \(\dfrac{dv}{v} = -k\,dt \Rightarrow \ln\dfrac{v}{v_0} = -kt \Rightarrow v = v_0 e^{-kt}\). Integrate for position:

\[ x(t) = \int_0^t v_0e^{-kt'}dt' = \frac{v_0}{k}\left(1 - e^{-kt}\right) \to \frac{v_0}{k}\ \text{as}\ t\to\infty. \]

The boat never quite stops but travels a finite distance \(v_0/k\). Units: \(k\) is in \(\text{s}^{-1}\), so \(v_0/k\) is in metres. (Same math as an RC discharge with time constant \(1/k\).)

Practice problems

  1. A model rocket accelerates straight up from rest at \(20\ \text{m/s}^2\) for 8.0 s, then coasts. Find its maximum altitude (ignore drag).
    Answer

    Burnout: \(v = 160\ \text{m/s}\), \(h_1 = \tfrac12(20)(64) = 640\ \text{m}\). Coast: \(h_2 = 160^2/19.6 = 1306\ \text{m}\). Max altitude \(\approx 1.95\ \text{km}\).

  2. A stone is dropped into a well; the splash is heard 2.40 s later. Take sound speed 343 m/s. How deep is the well?
    Answer

    Fall time \(t_1\), sound time \(d/343\), with \(d = 4.9t_1^2\): \(0.014286\,t_1^2 + t_1 - 2.40 = 0 \Rightarrow t_1 = 2.323\ \text{s}\), \(d = 4.9(2.323)^2 \approx 26.4\ \text{m}\).

  3. A particle has \(v(t) = 3t^2 - 12\) (m/s). Find its displacement and the distance travelled from \(t = 0\) to \(3\ \text{s}\).
    Answer

    \(x(t) - x_0 = t^3 - 12t\). Displacement: \(27 - 36 = -9\ \text{m}\). It reverses at \(t = 2\ \text{s}\): \(0\to2\) gives \(-16\ \text{m}\), \(2\to3\) gives \(+7\ \text{m}\). Distance = 23 m.

  4. Car A starts from rest with \(a = 3.0\ \text{m/s}^2\) just as car B passes it at a steady 18 m/s. When and where does A catch B?
    Answer

    \(1.5t^2 = 18t \Rightarrow t = 12\ \text{s}\), at \(x = 216\ \text{m}\); A is then moving at 36 m/s.

  5. A ball is dropped from 45 m. How long does it fall and how fast does it land?
    Answer

    \(t = \sqrt{2h/g} = \sqrt{90/9.8} = 3.03\ \text{s}\); \(v = \sqrt{2gh} = 29.7\ \text{m/s}\).

  6. A spring-driven slider has \(a(x) = -\omega^2 x\). If it starts at \(x = A\) from rest, use \(v\,dv = a\,dx\) to find \(v(x)\).
    Answer

    \(\tfrac12v^2 = -\tfrac12\omega^2(x^2 - A^2)\), so \(|v| = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}\), maximum \(\omega A\) at \(x = 0\) (a preview of SHM).

3. Vectors

Halliday Ch. 3 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 2

Displacement, velocity, force and momentum all have a direction as well as a size, and they combine geometrically: two 5 N forces can add to anything from 0 to 10 N. Vectors are the bookkeeping that handles this. In practice you almost always work in components, because components add like ordinary numbers, axis by axis. The two vector products then capture the two ways directions interact: the dot product (how aligned, giving work) and the cross product (how perpendicular, giving torque and angular momentum).

3.1 Components & unit vectors

Definition (Components)

A vector \(\vec a\) in the \(xy\)-plane of magnitude \(a\) at angle \(\theta\) measured counterclockwise from \(+x\) has components \(a_x = a\cos\theta\), \(a_y = a\sin\theta\). In unit-vector notation \(\vec a = a_x\hat\imath + a_y\hat\jmath + a_z\hat k\), with

\[ a = |\vec a| = \sqrt{a_x^2 + a_y^2 + a_z^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{a_y}{a_x}\ \ (\text{2D}). \]

Addition is componentwise: \(\vec r = \vec a + \vec b \iff r_x = a_x + b_x\), etc. Geometrically this is the tip-to-tail rule. Vectors are unaffected by translating the coordinate axes, and their magnitudes and the relationships between them are unaffected by rotating the axes; choose axes that make the problem easy (for example along an incline).

Common mistake

Calculators return \(\tan^{-1}(a_y/a_x)\) in \((-90^\circ, 90^\circ)\). If \(a_x \lt 0\) add \(180^\circ\). Always sketch the vector to see which quadrant it is in.

Example 3.1

A survey drone flies 4.0 km east, then 3.0 km in a direction \(60^\circ\) north of west. Find its net displacement.

Show solution

East = \(+x\), north = \(+y\). \(\vec a = (4.0, 0)\) km. The second leg points at \(180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ\) from \(+x\): \(\vec b = 3.0(\cos120^\circ, \sin120^\circ) = (-1.50, 2.60)\) km.

\[ \vec r = (2.50,\ 2.60)\ \text{km}, \quad r = \sqrt{2.50^2 + 2.60^2} = 3.61\ \text{km}, \quad \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{2.60}{2.50} = 46.1^\circ\ \text{N of E}. \]

Check: \(r\) lies between \(|4 - 3| = 1\) and \(4 + 3 = 7\) km, as it must.

3.2 Dot & cross products

Scalar (dot) product

\[ \vec a\cdot\vec b = ab\cos\phi = a_xb_x + a_yb_y + a_zb_z \]

Zero for perpendicular vectors; \(\vec a\cdot\vec a = a^2\). The component of \(\vec b\) along \(\vec a\) is \(\vec a\cdot\vec b/a\). Physics use: work \(W = \vec F\cdot\vec d\), power \(P = \vec F\cdot\vec v\).

Vector (cross) product

\[ \vec a\times\vec b = \begin{vmatrix} \hat\imath & \hat\jmath & \hat k \\ a_x & a_y & a_z \\ b_x & b_y & b_z \end{vmatrix} = (a_yb_z - a_zb_y)\hat\imath + (a_zb_x - a_xb_z)\hat\jmath + (a_xb_y - a_yb_x)\hat k \]

Magnitude \(ab\sin\phi\) (area of the parallelogram), direction by the right-hand rule: perpendicular to both. Anticommutative: \(\vec b\times\vec a = -\vec a\times\vec b\). Physics use: torque \(\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F\), angular momentum \(\vec\ell = \vec r\times\vec p\), magnetic force.

Study tip

Memorise the cyclic order \(\hat\imath\times\hat\jmath = \hat k\), \(\hat\jmath\times\hat k = \hat\imath\), \(\hat k\times\hat\imath = \hat\jmath\); going backwards around the cycle gives a minus sign. Verify any cross product by dotting it with both factors: both results must be zero.

Example 3.2

For \(\vec a = 3\hat\imath - 2\hat\jmath + \hat k\) and \(\vec b = \hat\imath + 4\hat\jmath - 2\hat k\), find \(\vec a\cdot\vec b\), the angle between them, and \(\vec a\times\vec b\).

Show solution

\(\vec a\cdot\vec b = 3 - 8 - 2 = -7\). Magnitudes \(a = \sqrt{14} = 3.742\), \(b = \sqrt{21} = 4.583\).

\[ \cos\phi = \frac{-7}{(3.742)(4.583)} = -0.408 \Rightarrow \phi = 114^\circ. \] \[ \vec a\times\vec b = \big((-2)(-2) - (1)(4)\big)\hat\imath + \big((1)(1) - (3)(-2)\big)\hat\jmath + \big((3)(4) - (-2)(1)\big)\hat k = 0\,\hat\imath + 7\hat\jmath + 14\hat k. \]

Check: \(\vec a\cdot(\vec a\times\vec b) = 0 - 14 + 14 = 0\) and \(\vec b\cdot(\vec a\times\vec b) = 0 + 28 - 28 = 0\). Also \(|\vec a\times\vec b| = \sqrt{245} = 15.65\) and \(ab\sin\phi = 17.15(0.913) = 15.65\).

Practice problems

  1. Find the components of a vector of magnitude 12 at \(210^\circ\) from \(+x\).
    Answer

    \((12\cos210^\circ, 12\sin210^\circ) = (-10.4, -6.0)\).

  2. Find the angle between \(\hat\imath + \hat\jmath\) and \(\hat\jmath + \hat k\).
    Answer

    \(\cos\phi = 1/(\sqrt2\sqrt2) = 1/2\), so \(\phi = 60^\circ\).

  3. A force \(\vec F = (2, -1, 3)\) N acts over a displacement \(\vec d = (4, 2, 1)\) m. Find the work done.
    Answer

    \(W = 8 - 2 + 3 = 9\ \text{J}\).

  4. Find the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\vec a = 2\hat\imath\) m and \(\vec b = \hat\imath + 3\hat\jmath\) m.
    Answer

    \(\vec a\times\vec b = (2)(3)\hat k = 6\hat k\), area \(6\ \text{m}^2\).

  5. Find the torque \(\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F\) for \(\vec r = 0.50\hat\imath\) m and \(\vec F = 20\hat\jmath + 10\hat k\) N.
    Answer

    \(\vec\tau = (0)\hat\imath + (0 - 0.5\cdot10)\hat\jmath + (0.5\cdot20)\hat k = (0, -5.0, 10)\ \text{N·m}\), magnitude 11.2 N·m.

4. Motion in two & three dimensions

Halliday Ch. 4 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 4

Motion in a plane or in space is just one-dimensional motion done separately along each axis, with time as the common thread. The key insight for projectiles is that horizontal and vertical motions are independent: gravity changes only the vertical velocity. Circular motion adds a new idea: even at constant speed, a changing direction of velocity is an acceleration, pointing toward the centre.

4.1 Position, velocity & acceleration vectors

Definitions

\[ \vec r(t) = x\hat\imath + y\hat\jmath + z\hat k, \qquad \vec v = \frac{d\vec r}{dt}, \qquad \vec a = \frac{d\vec v}{dt}. \]

The velocity is always tangent to the path. The acceleration can be split into a tangential part \(a_t = d|\vec v|/dt\) (changes speed) and a radial (centripetal) part \(a_r = v^2/R\) toward the centre of curvature (changes direction).

4.2 Projectile motion

Assumptions: uniform gravity \(g\) downward, no air resistance, flat non-rotating Earth. Launching from the origin with speed \(v_0\) at angle \(\theta_0\) and integrating \(\vec a = -g\hat\jmath\) twice:

\[ x = (v_0\cos\theta_0)\,t, \qquad y = (v_0\sin\theta_0)\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2, \qquad v_y = v_0\sin\theta_0 - gt. \]

Eliminating \(t = x/(v_0\cos\theta_0)\) shows the path is a parabola. Setting \(y = 0\) gives the flight time \(2v_0\sin\theta_0/g\), hence the range; setting \(v_y = 0\) gives the peak.

Projectile results (level ground)

\[ y = x\tan\theta_0 - \frac{g x^2}{2v_0^2\cos^2\theta_0}, \qquad R = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta_0}{g}, \qquad H = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta_0}{2g}. \]

\(R\) is maximised at \(\theta_0 = 45^\circ\), and complementary angles (\(\theta_0\) and \(90^\circ - \theta_0\)) give the same range. The range formula holds only if launch and landing heights are equal.

Example 4.1

A ball is launched at \(25\ \text{m/s}\), \(35^\circ\) above horizontal, from the top of a \(20\ \text{m}\) cliff. Find the time of flight, horizontal distance, and impact velocity.

Show solution

Components: \(v_{0x} = 25\cos35^\circ = 20.48\ \text{m/s}\), \(v_{0y} = 25\sin35^\circ = 14.34\ \text{m/s}\). Origin at the launch point; the ground is at \(y = -20\ \text{m}\).

\[ -20 = 14.34t - 4.9t^2 \Rightarrow t = \frac{14.34 + \sqrt{14.34^2 + 4(4.9)(20)}}{9.8} = \frac{14.34 + 24.45}{9.8} = 3.96\ \text{s}. \]

Horizontal distance \(x = 20.48 \times 3.96 = 81.1\ \text{m}\). At impact \(v_y = 14.34 - 9.8(3.96) = -24.4\ \text{m/s}\), \(v_x = 20.5\ \text{m/s}\):

\[ v = \sqrt{20.48^2 + 24.45^2} = 31.9\ \text{m/s}, \quad 50.0^\circ\ \text{below horizontal}. \]

Check with energy: \(v = \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh} = \sqrt{625 + 392} = 31.9\ \text{m/s}\). Agreement.

Common mistake

Using \(R = v_0^2\sin2\theta_0/g\) when the landing height differs from the launch height (like the cliff above). Fall back on the component equations. Also, at the top of the arc the velocity is not zero, only \(v_y\) is.

4.3 Circular motion

Derivation. For uniform motion on a circle of radius \(r\), \(\vec r = r(\cos\omega t\,\hat\imath + \sin\omega t\,\hat\jmath)\). Differentiating, \(\vec v = r\omega(-\sin\omega t\,\hat\imath + \cos\omega t\,\hat\jmath)\), so \(v = r\omega\) and \(\vec v\perp\vec r\). Differentiating again, \(\vec a = -\omega^2\vec r\): the acceleration points to the centre with magnitude \(\omega^2 r = v^2/r\).

Uniform circular motion

\[ a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}, \qquad |\vec a| = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_t^2}\ \ (\text{non-uniform}). \]

Example 4.2

A human centrifuge has an 8.0 m arm. What speed, period and rotation rate (rpm) give a centripetal acceleration of \(6g\)?

Show solution

\(v = \sqrt{a_c r} = \sqrt{6(9.8)(8.0)} = \sqrt{470} = 21.7\ \text{m/s}\). \(T = 2\pi r/v = 50.3/21.7 = 2.32\ \text{s}\), i.e. \(60/2.32 = 25.9\ \text{rpm}\).

Check: \(a_c = \omega^2r\) with \(\omega = 2\pi/2.32 = 2.71\ \text{rad/s}\): \(2.71^2 \times 8 = 58.8\ \text{m/s}^2 = 6g\).

4.4 Relative motion

Galilean velocity addition

If frame B moves at constant velocity \(\vec v_{BA}\) relative to frame A, a particle P has

\[ \vec v_{PA} = \vec v_{PB} + \vec v_{BA}, \qquad \vec a_{PA} = \vec a_{PB}. \]

Observers in frames moving at constant relative velocity measure the same acceleration. Valid for speeds \(\ll c\).

Example 4.3

A light aircraft has airspeed 220 km/h. A 60 km/h wind blows toward the east. What heading must the pilot fly to travel due north, and what is the ground speed?

Show solution

\(\vec v_{PG} = \vec v_{PA} + \vec v_{AG}\) (plane–ground = plane–air + air–ground). We need the east components to cancel: \(220\sin\theta = 60\), so \(\theta = \sin^{-1}(0.273) = 15.8^\circ\) west of north.

Ground speed \(= \sqrt{220^2 - 60^2} = 212\ \text{km/h}\). Check: less than the airspeed, since part of the effort fights the wind.

Practice problems

  1. What is the maximum level-ground range for a launch speed of 30 m/s?
    Answer

    \(R_{\max} = v_0^2/g = 900/9.8 = 91.8\ \text{m}\) at \(45^\circ\).

  2. At what angles must a 30 m/s projectile be launched to land 60 m away on level ground?
    Answer

    \(\sin2\theta = 60(9.8)/900 = 0.653\): \(2\theta = 40.8^\circ\) or \(139.2^\circ\), so \(\theta = 20.4^\circ\) or \(69.6^\circ\).

  3. Find Earth's centripetal acceleration in its orbit (radius \(1.496\times10^{11}\) m, period \(3.156\times10^7\) s).
    Answer

    \(v = 2\pi r/T = 2.98\times10^4\ \text{m/s}\), \(a = v^2/r = 5.93\times10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}^2\).

  4. A boat moves at 4.0 m/s relative to the water and heads straight across a 120 m river flowing at 3.0 m/s. Find the crossing time, downstream drift and ground speed.
    Answer

    \(t = 120/4 = 30\ \text{s}\); drift \(= 3(30) = 90\ \text{m}\); ground speed \(= \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5.0\ \text{m/s}\).

  5. A particle has \(\vec r(t) = 3t^2\hat\imath + (4t - t^3)\hat\jmath\) (m). Find \(\vec v\) and \(\vec a\) at \(t = 1\ \text{s}\).
    Answer

    \(\vec v = 6t\hat\imath + (4 - 3t^2)\hat\jmath = (6, 1)\ \text{m/s}\); \(\vec a = 6\hat\imath - 6t\hat\jmath = (6, -6)\ \text{m/s}^2\).

  6. A car on a 50 m radius curve is at 15 m/s and speeding up at 2.0 m/s\(^2\). Find the magnitude of its acceleration.
    Answer

    \(a_c = 225/50 = 4.5\ \text{m/s}^2\), \(a_t = 2.0\); \(|\vec a| = \sqrt{4.5^2 + 2^2} = 4.92\ \text{m/s}^2\).

5. Force & motion: Newton's laws

Halliday Ch. 5 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 5

Kinematics describes motion; dynamics explains it. Newton's insight is that forces do not cause velocity, they cause changes in velocity. A body left alone keeps moving in a straight line forever; a net force bends or speeds up that motion in proportion to the force and in inverse proportion to the mass. Almost every mechanics problem reduces to: identify the forces on one body, add them as vectors, set the sum equal to \(m\vec a\).

5.1 The three laws

Newton's first law

If no net force acts on a body, its velocity does not change. Frames in which this holds are inertial frames; the first law is really the statement that such frames exist. Earth's surface is approximately inertial (its rotation causes small corrections).

Newton's second law

\[ \vec F_{\text{net}} = \sum \vec F = m\vec a \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \sum F_x = ma_x,\ \sum F_y = ma_y,\ \sum F_z = ma_z. \]

Sum only the external forces acting on the chosen body (or system). Valid in inertial frames for speeds \(\ll c\) and constant mass; the more general form is \(\vec F = d\vec p/dt\) (Chapter 9).

Newton's third law

When body A exerts a force on body B, B exerts an equal and opposite force on A: \(\vec F_{AB} = -\vec F_{BA}\). The two forces of a third-law pair are the same type (both gravitational, both contact, etc.) and act on different bodies, so they never cancel in a single body's free-body diagram.

5.2 Common forces

  • Gravitational force (weight) \(\vec F_g = m\vec g\), downward. "Apparent weight" is what a scale reads, i.e. the normal force.
  • Normal force \(\vec F_N\): perpendicular to a contact surface, as large as needed (up to breaking) to prevent interpenetration. It is not automatically \(mg\).
  • Tension \(T\): pull along a rope or cable. For a massless rope over a massless, frictionless pulley, the tension is the same throughout.
  • Friction and drag: Chapter 6. Spring force \(F = -kx\): Chapter 7.

Example 5.1

A 70 kg technician stands on a bathroom scale in an elevator. What does the scale read when the elevator accelerates upward at 2.0 m/s\(^2\)? Downward at 2.0 m/s\(^2\)?

Show solution

Forces on the person: \(F_N\) up, \(mg\) down. Up positive: \(F_N - mg = ma \Rightarrow F_N = m(g + a)\).

Upward acceleration: \(F_N = 70(9.8 + 2.0) = 826\ \text{N}\). Downward (\(a = -2.0\)): \(F_N = 70(7.8) = 546\ \text{N}\). At rest it reads 686 N.

Limits: \(a = -g\) (free fall) gives \(F_N = 0\): apparent weightlessness, as in orbit.

5.3 The free-body diagram method

Study tip: the recipe

(1) Choose the body. (2) Draw it as a dot with every force acting on it, each labelled with its source. (3) Choose axes, ideally one along the acceleration (along an incline, toward a centre). (4) Write \(\sum F = ma\) per axis. (5) For connected bodies, add constraint equations (same rope \(\Rightarrow\) same \(|a|\)). (6) Solve symbolically, then check limits.

Common mistake

Putting "\(ma\)" on the free-body diagram as if it were a force, or including forces the body exerts on something else. Only forces acting on the body belong on its diagram.

Example 5.2

A 4.0 kg block on a frictionless \(30^\circ\) incline is tied by a rope over a light pulley at the top to a hanging 3.0 kg mass. Find the acceleration and the rope tension.

Show solution

Guess the hanging mass descends; take that as the positive direction for both (block moves up the slope). Block (\(m_1\)) along the incline: \(T - m_1g\sin\theta = m_1a\). Hanging mass (\(m_2\)): \(m_2g - T = m_2a\). Add to eliminate \(T\):

\[ a = \frac{m_2 - m_1\sin\theta}{m_1 + m_2}\,g = \frac{3.0 - 2.0}{7.0}(9.8) = 1.4\ \text{m/s}^2, \qquad T = m_2(g - a) = 3.0(8.4) = 25.2\ \text{N}. \]

Check: \(a \gt 0\) confirms the guess. Limit \(\theta = 0\): \(a = m_2g/(m_1 + m_2)\), the familiar table-and-pulley result. \(T\) lies between \(m_1g\sin\theta = 19.6\) N and \(m_2g = 29.4\) N, as it must.

Example 5.3

A 24 N horizontal push acts on a 2.0 kg block, which pushes a 4.0 kg block in front of it on a frictionless floor. Find the acceleration and the contact force between the blocks.

Show solution

System of both blocks: \(a = F/(m_1 + m_2) = 24/6.0 = 4.0\ \text{m/s}^2\). The front block alone has only the contact force: \(F_c = m_2a = 16\ \text{N}\).

Check on the rear block: \(24 - 16 = 8 = 2.0(4.0)\). Pushing from the other side would give \(F_c = 8\ \text{N}\): the force needed depends on how much mass it must accelerate.

Practice problems

  1. An Atwood machine has masses 5.0 kg and 3.0 kg on a light pulley. Find the acceleration and tension.
    Answer

    \(a = (5 - 3)g/8 = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2\); \(T = 3(9.8 + 2.45) = 36.8\ \text{N}\).

  2. A 50 N sign hangs from two cables, each at \(40^\circ\) above horizontal. Find the tension in each.
    Answer

    \(2T\sin40^\circ = 50 \Rightarrow T = 38.9\ \text{N}\).

  3. A block slides from rest 3.0 m down a frictionless \(25^\circ\) incline. Find its final speed.
    Answer

    \(a = g\sin25^\circ = 4.14\ \text{m/s}^2\); \(v = \sqrt{2(4.14)(3.0)} = 4.99\ \text{m/s}\).

  4. A \(5.0\times10^5\) kg launch vehicle has \(7.5\times10^6\) N of thrust at liftoff. Find its initial acceleration.
    Answer

    \(a = T/m - g = 15 - 9.8 = 5.2\ \text{m/s}^2\) (thrust-to-weight ratio 1.53).

  5. A horse pulls a cart. By the third law the cart pulls back equally on the horse. Why does anything move?
    Answer

    The pair acts on different bodies. The horse accelerates because the ground's forward friction on its hooves exceeds the cart's backward pull; the cart accelerates because the horse's pull exceeds the friction on its wheels.

6. Force & motion II: friction, drag & circular motion

Halliday Ch. 6 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 6

Real surfaces resist sliding and real air resists motion. Friction is a self-adjusting force up to a limit: a heavy crate stays put because static friction matches your push exactly, until the push exceeds the maximum and the crate breaks loose. Drag grows with speed, so a falling object eventually reaches a speed where drag balances weight. And for any object moving on a curve, some real force (friction, tension, normal force, gravity) must supply the inward \(mv^2/r\).

6.1 Friction

Empirical friction model

\[ f_s \le \mu_s F_N \ \ (\text{static, no sliding}), \qquad f_k = \mu_k F_N \ \ (\text{kinetic, sliding}), \qquad \mu_k \lt \mu_s\ \text{typically}. \]

Friction acts parallel to the surface, opposing relative motion (or the tendency to move). The model is approximate: the coefficients depend on the surface pair and condition and are roughly independent of contact area and sliding speed.

Common mistake

Writing \(f_s = \mu_sF_N\) when the object is not on the verge of slipping. Static friction is only as large as needed; \(\mu_sF_N\) is its ceiling. Also, \(F_N\) changes whenever other forces have components perpendicular to the surface.

Example 6.1

A crate rests on a ramp with \(\mu_s = 0.40\), \(\mu_k = 0.30\). (a) Does it slide at \(20^\circ\)? (b) At what angle does it start to slide? (c) Find its acceleration if the ramp is tilted to \(25^\circ\).

Show solution

Axes along and perpendicular to the ramp: \(F_N = mg\cos\theta\). Holding the crate requires \(f_s = mg\sin\theta \le \mu_s mg\cos\theta\), i.e. \(\tan\theta \le \mu_s\).

(a) \(\tan20^\circ = 0.364 \lt 0.40\): it stays. (b) \(\theta_c = \tan^{-1}0.40 = 21.8^\circ\), independent of mass.

(c) Sliding: \(ma = mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta\):

\[ a = g(\sin25^\circ - 0.30\cos25^\circ) = 9.8(0.4226 - 0.2719) = 1.48\ \text{m/s}^2. \]

6.2 Drag & terminal speed

Drag force

\[ D = \tfrac12 C\rho A v^2 \quad (\text{high Reynolds number}), \qquad v_t = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{C\rho A}}. \]

\(C\) is the drag coefficient (about 0.5 for a sphere, about 1 for a person belly-down), \(\rho\) the fluid density, \(A\) the frontal area. Terminal speed \(v_t\) follows from \(D = mg\). For slow, small objects in viscous fluid, drag is linear instead: \(D = bv\).

Linear drag, solved. With \(m\,dv/dt = mg - bv\) and \(v(0) = 0\), separating variables gives

\[ v(t) = v_t\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad v_t = \frac{mg}{b}, \quad \tau = \frac{m}{b}. \]

After one time constant the speed is 63% of terminal. This is exactly the step response of a first-order system (an RC circuit charging).

Example 6.2

Estimate the terminal speed of an 80 kg skydiver falling belly-down: \(C \approx 1.0\), \(A = 0.70\ \text{m}^2\), \(\rho = 1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3\).

Show solution \[ v_t = \sqrt{\frac{2(80)(9.8)}{(1.0)(1.2)(0.70)}} = \sqrt{1867} = 43\ \text{m/s}\ (\approx 155\ \text{km/h}). \]

Units: \(\sqrt{\text{N}/(\text{kg/m}^3\cdot\text{m}^2)} = \sqrt{\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}\). Diving head-first reduces \(CA\) and roughly doubles \(v_t\), as skydivers know.

6.3 Dynamics of circular motion

Principle

For a body moving on a circle of radius \(R\) at speed \(v\), the net force component toward the centre must equal \(mv^2/R\). "Centripetal force" is not a new force; it is the name for whatever combination of real forces points inward.

Example 6.3

A highway curve of radius 120 m is designed for 25 m/s. (a) Find the bank angle needed so no friction is required. (b) On a flat curve, what minimum \(\mu_s\) would be needed?

Show solution

(a) Forces: \(F_N\) perpendicular to the road, \(mg\) down. Vertical: \(F_N\cos\theta = mg\). Horizontal (inward): \(F_N\sin\theta = mv^2/R\). Divide:

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{Rg} = \frac{625}{1176} = 0.531 \Rightarrow \theta = 28.0^\circ. \]

(b) Flat: \(\mu_s mg \ge mv^2/R \Rightarrow \mu_s \ge v^2/(Rg) = 0.53\). Mass cancels in both answers, so the design works for trucks and cars alike.

Study tip

For circular-motion problems choose the radial axis pointing toward the centre, so the right-hand side is \(+mv^2/R\). On a banked curve the axes are horizontal and vertical (not along the incline), because the acceleration is horizontal.

Practice problems

  1. Find the maximum speed on a flat curve of radius 80 m if \(\mu_s = 0.60\).
    Answer

    \(v = \sqrt{\mu_sgR} = \sqrt{0.6(9.8)(80)} = 21.7\ \text{m/s}\).

  2. What minimum speed at the top keeps a cart on a vertical loop of radius 6.0 m?
    Answer

    At the top with \(F_N = 0\): \(mg = mv^2/R\), \(v = \sqrt{gR} = 7.67\ \text{m/s}\).

  3. A 20 kg box is pulled at constant velocity by a rope at \(30^\circ\) above horizontal; \(\mu_k = 0.25\). Find the tension.
    Answer

    \(T\cos30^\circ = \mu_k(mg - T\sin30^\circ) \Rightarrow T = \mu_kmg/(\cos30^\circ + \mu_k\sin30^\circ) = 49/0.991 = 49.4\ \text{N}\).

  4. A conical pendulum has string length 1.2 m at \(25^\circ\) from vertical. Find its period.
    Answer

    \(T\cos\theta = mg\), \(T\sin\theta = m\omega^2L\sin\theta\) gives \(\omega^2 = g/(L\cos\theta)\): period \(= 2\pi\sqrt{L\cos\theta/g} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.111} = 2.09\ \text{s}\).

  5. A 0.50 kg probe falls in a fluid with linear drag \(b = 0.25\ \text{kg/s}\). Find \(v_t\) and the time constant.
    Answer

    \(v_t = mg/b = 19.6\ \text{m/s}\); \(\tau = m/b = 2.0\ \text{s}\).

  6. At what speed does a car lose contact with the crest of a hill of radius 40 m?
    Answer

    \(mg - F_N = mv^2/R\) with \(F_N = 0\): \(v = \sqrt{gR} = 19.8\ \text{m/s}\).

7. Kinetic energy & work

Halliday Ch. 7 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 7

Newton's second law tracks forces moment by moment. Energy methods trade that detail for bookkeeping: instead of asking how the acceleration evolves, ask how much a force has pushed along the path. That accumulated push, the work, equals the change in a single scalar, the kinetic energy. When you only need speeds at two positions (not times), energy methods are usually faster and avoid vector components entirely.

7.1 Kinetic energy & work

Definitions

Kinetic energy \(K = \tfrac12mv^2\) (unit: joule, \(1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}^2\)). Work done by a force on a body moving along a path from \(\vec r_i\) to \(\vec r_f\):

\[ W = \int_{\vec r_i}^{\vec r_f}\vec F\cdot d\vec r \qquad \xrightarrow{\ \vec F\ \text{constant}\ } \qquad W = \vec F\cdot\vec d = Fd\cos\phi. \]

Work is a scalar that can be positive (force has a component along the motion), negative (against the motion), or zero (perpendicular, e.g. the normal force on a level floor, or the centripetal force in uniform circular motion).

7.2 The work–kinetic energy theorem

Derivation (1D, extends component-wise). Using \(a = v\,dv/dx\):

\[ W_{\text{net}} = \int_{x_i}^{x_f}F_{\text{net}}\,dx = \int_{x_i}^{x_f}m v\frac{dv}{dx}\,dx = \int_{v_i}^{v_f}mv\,dv = \tfrac12mv_f^2 - \tfrac12mv_i^2. \]

Work–kinetic energy theorem

\[ W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = K_f - K_i. \]

Holds for a particle (or a rigid body treated as a particle) for any net force, constant or not. "Net" means the sum of the work done by every force.

Example 7.1

A 30 kg crate starts from rest and is dragged 10 m across a floor (\(\mu_k = 0.30\)) by a rope pulling with 150 N at \(20^\circ\) above horizontal. Find the work done by each force and the final speed.

Show solution

Normal force (vertical balance): \(F_N = mg - T\sin20^\circ = 294 - 51.3 = 242.7\ \text{N}\), so \(f_k = 0.30(242.7) = 72.8\ \text{N}\).

\[ W_T = Td\cos20^\circ = 150(10)(0.9397) = 1410\ \text{J}, \qquad W_f = -f_kd = -728\ \text{J}, \qquad W_{N} = W_{g} = 0. \]

\(W_{\text{net}} = 682\ \text{J} = \tfrac12(30)v^2 \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{45.4} = 6.74\ \text{m/s}\).

Check: gravity and the normal force are perpendicular to the motion, so they do no work even though they affect friction through \(F_N\).

7.3 Variable forces & springs

Hooke's law

An ideal spring exerts \(F_x = -kx\), where \(x\) is the displacement from its relaxed length and \(k\) is the spring constant (N/m). It is a restoring force, valid for small deformations.

Work by a spring

\[ W_s = \int_{x_i}^{x_f}(-kx)\,dx = \tfrac12kx_i^2 - \tfrac12kx_f^2. \]

Positive when the spring returns toward its relaxed length, negative when it is being stretched or compressed further. Graphically, the work done by any 1D force is the area under the \(F(x)\) curve.

Example 7.2

A 2.0 kg block slides at 3.0 m/s on a frictionless surface into a spring with \(k = 450\ \text{N/m}\). Find the maximum compression.

Show solution

Only the spring does work, from \(x = 0\) to \(x = d\): \(-\tfrac12kd^2 = 0 - \tfrac12mv^2\), so

\[ d = v\sqrt{m/k} = 3.0\sqrt{2.0/450} = 0.200\ \text{m}. \]

Units: \(\sqrt{\text{kg}/(\text{N/m})} = \text{s}\), times m/s gives m. Stiffer spring \(\Rightarrow\) smaller \(d\), as expected.

Common mistake

Using \(W = Fd\) for a spring. The force varies with position, so you must integrate (or use the triangle area \(\tfrac12kx^2\)). Also, \(x\) is measured from the relaxed length, not from wherever the block happens to start.

7.4 Power

Power

\[ P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \vec F\cdot\vec v, \qquad P_{\text{avg}} = \frac{W}{\Delta t}, \qquad 1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s},\ \ 1\ \text{hp} = 746\ \text{W}. \]

Example 7.3

An elevator car of total mass 1200 kg rises at a constant 2.5 m/s. What power must the motor deliver (ignore friction)?

Show solution

Constant velocity \(\Rightarrow\) cable tension \(T = mg\). \(P = Tv = mgv = 1200(9.8)(2.5) = 29.4\ \text{kW}\) (about 39 hp). The kinetic energy is not changing; the motor's work goes entirely into gravitational potential energy (Chapter 8).

Practice problems

  1. How much work is needed to stretch a spring (\(k = 200\ \text{N/m}\)) from 0.10 m to 0.30 m beyond its relaxed length?
    Answer

    \(W = \tfrac12(200)(0.30^2 - 0.10^2) = 8.0\ \text{J}\).

  2. A force \(F(x) = 3x^2\) N acts on a 0.50 kg particle starting from rest at \(x = 0\). Find its speed at \(x = 2.0\) m.
    Answer

    \(W = \int_0^2 3x^2dx = 8.0\ \text{J} = \tfrac12(0.5)v^2 \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{32} = 5.66\ \text{m/s}\).

  3. A 1500 kg car goes from 0 to 27 m/s in 9.0 s. Find the average power delivered to it.
    Answer

    \(\Delta K = \tfrac12(1500)(729) = 5.47\times10^5\ \text{J}\); \(P = 6.08\times10^4\ \text{W} \approx 81\ \text{hp}\).

  4. A force \(\vec F = (3\hat\imath + 4\hat\jmath)\) N acts during a displacement \((2\hat\imath - \hat\jmath)\) m. Find the work.
    Answer

    \(W = 6 - 4 = 2.0\ \text{J}\).

  5. An engine delivers \(2.0\times10^5\) N of thrust to a vehicle moving at 800 m/s. What power is delivered to the vehicle?
    Answer

    \(P = Fv = 1.6\times10^8\ \text{W} = 160\ \text{MW}\).

  6. A ball is thrown up and caught at the same height. What net work does gravity do on it?
    Answer

    Zero: negative work on the way up cancels positive work on the way down (gravity is conservative, Chapter 8).

8. Potential energy & conservation of energy

Halliday Ch. 8 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 8

Some forces "give back" the work done against them: lift a book and gravity returns that energy when it falls; compress a spring and it pushes back just as hard. For these conservative forces we can store the work as potential energy, a function of position only. Then kinetic plus potential energy stays constant, and a single equation connects any two points of the motion. Friction is different: the energy it removes turns into thermal energy and does not come back as motion.

8.1 Conservative forces & potential energy

Definition (Conservative force)

A force is conservative if the work it does on a particle moving between two points is independent of path; equivalently, the work around every closed path is zero. Gravity, spring forces and electrostatic forces are conservative; kinetic friction and drag are not. For a conservative force define the potential energy change

\[ \Delta U = -W = -\int_{x_i}^{x_f}F(x)\,dx, \qquad F(x) = -\frac{dU}{dx}\ \ \left(\vec F = -\nabla U\ \text{in 3D}\right). \]

Standard potential energies

\[ U_g = mgy\ \ (\text{near surface}), \qquad U_s = \tfrac12kx^2, \qquad U_G = -\frac{GMm}{r}\ \ (\text{Chapter 13}). \]

Only differences in \(U\) matter; the zero is your choice, but keep it fixed within a problem.

8.2 Conservation of energy

Conservation of mechanical energy

If only conservative forces do work on a system, its mechanical energy is constant:

\[ E_{\text{mec}} = K + U, \qquad K_1 + U_1 = K_2 + U_2. \]

General energy statement

Work done on a system by external forces changes its total energy: \(W_{\text{ext}} = \Delta E_{\text{mec}} + \Delta E_{\text{th}} + \Delta E_{\text{int}}\). Kinetic friction over a sliding distance \(d\) produces \(\Delta E_{\text{th}} = f_kd\). For an isolated system, total energy is conserved, always.

Example 8.1

A block is released from rest at height \(h = 3.0\ \text{m}\) on a frictionless curved ramp, then slides onto a rough horizontal floor with \(\mu_k = 0.20\). How far does it slide before stopping?

Show solution

Ramp (frictionless): \(mgh = \tfrac12mv^2\). Floor: friction removes all the kinetic energy, \(\mu_kmg\,d = \tfrac12mv^2 = mgh\). Hence

\[ d = \frac{h}{\mu_k} = \frac{3.0}{0.20} = 15\ \text{m}. \]

Mass and \(g\) cancel, and the ramp's shape is irrelevant: that is the power of energy methods. Doubling \(\mu_k\) halves \(d\), sensible.

Example 8.2

A vertical spring launcher (\(k = 800\ \text{N/m}\)) is compressed 0.15 m and fires a 0.10 kg ball straight up. How high does the ball rise above its release point (the compressed position)?

Show solution

Take \(y = 0\) at the compressed position. Initially \(K = 0\), \(U = \tfrac12kx^2\); at the top \(K = 0\), \(U = mgh\):

\[ h = \frac{kx^2}{2mg} = \frac{800(0.0225)}{2(0.10)(9.8)} = \frac{18}{1.96} = 9.18\ \text{m}. \]

The spring stores 9.0 J. Using the energy picture we did not need to know when the ball leaves the spring.

Study tip

Energy methods give speeds and positions, not times or directions. If a problem asks "how fast" or "how high", reach for energy first. If it asks "how long" or "what force", you likely need Newton's laws or kinematics too.

8.3 Potential-energy curves

Plot \(U(x)\) with a horizontal line at the total energy \(E\). Since \(K = E - U \ge 0\), the particle is confined to regions where \(U \le E\); where \(U = E\) it momentarily stops (a turning point). The force \(F = -dU/dx\) pushes downhill on the graph. Equilibria are where \(dU/dx = 0\): stable at a minimum (\(U'' \gt 0\)), unstable at a maximum (\(U'' \lt 0\)), neutral where \(U\) is flat.

Example 8.3

A 2.0 kg particle moves in \(U(x) = x^3 - 3x\) (J, with \(x\) in m). (a) Find and classify the equilibria. (b) It is released from rest at \(x = 0\). Describe the motion and find its maximum speed.

Show solution

(a) \(F = -U' = 3 - 3x^2 = 0\) at \(x = \pm1\). \(U'' = 6x\): stable at \(x = 1\) (\(U = -2\ \text{J}\)), unstable at \(x = -1\) (\(U = +2\ \text{J}\)).

(b) \(E = U(0) = 0\). At \(x = 0\), \(F = +3\ \text{N}\), so it moves right. Turning points where \(U = 0\): \(x = 0\) and \(x = \sqrt3 = 1.73\ \text{m}\). It oscillates between them. Maximum speed at the minimum of \(U\):

\[ K_{\max} = E - U(1) = 2\ \text{J} \Rightarrow v_{\max} = \sqrt{2K/m} = 1.41\ \text{m/s}. \]

Common mistake

Counting a conservative force twice: either include gravity's work in \(W\) or its potential energy in \(U\), never both.

Practice problems

  1. A 1.5 m pendulum is released from \(40^\circ\). Find the bob's speed at the bottom.
    Answer

    \(v = \sqrt{2gL(1 - \cos40^\circ)} = \sqrt{29.4(0.234)} = 2.62\ \text{m/s}\).

  2. A 3.0 kg block slides from rest 4.0 m down a \(30^\circ\) incline with \(\mu_k = 0.25\). Find its final speed.
    Answer

    \(\tfrac12v^2 = gd(\sin30^\circ - \mu_k\cos30^\circ) = 39.2(0.2835)\), so \(v = 4.71\ \text{m/s}\).

  3. A 0.50 kg mass is attached to a hanging relaxed spring (\(k = 50\ \text{N/m}\)) and released from rest. Find the maximum stretch.
    Answer

    \(mgd = \tfrac12kd^2 \Rightarrow d = 2mg/k = 0.196\ \text{m}\), twice the static stretch.

  4. From what minimum height must a cart start (from rest, frictionless) to complete a vertical loop of radius \(R\)?
    Answer

    Need \(v_{\text{top}}^2 \ge gR\): \(mgh = mg(2R) + \tfrac12mgR \Rightarrow h = 2.5R\).

  5. A molecular potential is \(U(r) = A/r^2 - B/r\) (\(A, B \gt 0\)). Find the equilibrium separation.
    Answer

    \(U' = -2A/r^3 + B/r^2 = 0 \Rightarrow r_0 = 2A/B\), a stable minimum.

  6. A 1200 kg car brakes from 20 m/s to rest on a level road. How much thermal energy is produced?
    Answer

    \(\Delta E_{\text{th}} = \tfrac12(1200)(400) = 2.4\times10^5\ \text{J}\), mostly in the brake discs.

9. Center of mass & linear momentum

Halliday Ch. 9 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 9

A thrown wrench tumbles in a complicated way, yet one special point, the center of mass, follows a clean parabola as if all the mass were concentrated there and all the external forces acted on it. Internal forces cancel in pairs (third law), so they cannot move the center of mass. The same cancellation makes total momentum conserved whenever external forces are negligible, which is the key to collisions, explosions, recoil and rocket propulsion.

9.1 Center of mass

Definition (Center of mass)

\[ \vec r_{\text{com}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_i m_i\vec r_i \qquad \text{or} \qquad \vec r_{\text{com}} = \frac{1}{M}\int\vec r\,dm, \qquad M = \sum m_i = \int dm. \]

For a uniform body the center of mass lies on any line or plane of symmetry. It need not lie within the material (a ring, a boomerang).

Newton's second law for a system

\[ \vec F_{\text{net,ext}} = M\vec a_{\text{com}}. \]

Derived by differentiating \(M\vec r_{\text{com}} = \sum m_i\vec r_i\) twice and noting that internal forces cancel in third-law pairs. Valid for a system of fixed total mass.

Example 9.1

A rod of length \(L\) has linear density \(\lambda(x) = \lambda_0(1 + x/L)\), \(0 \le x \le L\) (heavier toward \(x = L\)). Find its center of mass.

Show solution

With \(dm = \lambda\,dx\):

\[ M = \int_0^L\lambda_0\left(1 + \frac{x}{L}\right)dx = \frac32\lambda_0L, \qquad \int_0^Lx\,\lambda\,dx = \lambda_0\left(\frac{L^2}{2} + \frac{L^2}{3}\right) = \frac56\lambda_0L^2. \] \[ x_{\text{com}} = \frac{(5/6)\lambda_0L^2}{(3/2)\lambda_0L} = \frac59L \approx 0.556L. \]

Check: shifted from \(L/2\) toward the heavier end, but not past \(2L/3\) (the limit for density \(\propto x\)).

9.2 Momentum & impulse

Definitions

Linear momentum \(\vec p = m\vec v\); for a system \(\vec P = \sum\vec p_i = M\vec v_{\text{com}}\). Impulse of a force over a time interval:

\[ \vec J = \int_{t_i}^{t_f}\vec F\,dt = \vec F_{\text{avg}}\,\Delta t. \]

Impulse–momentum theorem & conservation

\[ \vec F_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec p}{dt} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec J = \Delta\vec p. \]

If the net external force on a system is zero (isolated system), \(\vec P\) is constant. If only one component of external force vanishes, that component of momentum is conserved. During a brief collision, external forces such as gravity are usually negligible compared with the huge internal contact forces, so momentum is conserved across the collision.

9.3 Collisions

Momentum is conserved in every collision of an isolated system; kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions. In a completely inelastic collision the bodies stick together, which loses the most kinetic energy consistent with momentum conservation.

1D elastic collision, target at rest. Momentum: \(m_1v_{1i} = m_1v_{1f} + m_2v_{2f}\). Energy: \(m_1v_{1i}^2 = m_1v_{1f}^2 + m_2v_{2f}^2\). Rewrite as \(m_1(v_{1i} - v_{1f}) = m_2v_{2f}\) and \(m_1(v_{1i} - v_{1f})(v_{1i} + v_{1f}) = m_2v_{2f}^2\); dividing gives \(v_{1i} + v_{1f} = v_{2f}\) (relative speed of approach equals relative speed of separation). Solving:

Collision results

\[ v_{1f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1i}, \qquad v_{2f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1i} \qquad (\text{elastic, } v_{2i} = 0) \] \[ V = \frac{m_1v_{1i} + m_2v_{2i}}{m_1 + m_2} \qquad (\text{completely inelastic}) \]

Study tip

Check the limits: equal masses swap velocities; a light ball on a heavy target bounces back with nearly its original speed; a heavy ball on a light target sends the target off at nearly \(2v_{1i}\). In 2D, conserve each momentum component separately.

Example 9.2

A 10 g bullet moving at 400 m/s embeds in a 2.0 kg block hanging from a string (ballistic pendulum). How high does the block swing, and what fraction of the bullet's kinetic energy is lost?

Show solution

Two stages. Collision (momentum conserved, energy not): \(V = \dfrac{mv}{m + M} = \dfrac{0.010(400)}{2.010} = 1.99\ \text{m/s}\).

Swing (mechanical energy conserved): \(h = V^2/(2g) = 3.96/19.6 = 0.202\ \text{m}\).

Fraction of KE kept: \(\dfrac{\tfrac12(m + M)V^2}{\tfrac12mv^2} = \dfrac{m}{m + M} = 0.005\), so 99.5% is lost to deformation and heat.

Warning illustrated: you cannot apply energy conservation across the collision itself.

Example 9.3

A 2.0 kg cart moving at 6.0 m/s collides elastically head-on with a 4.0 kg cart at rest. Find both final velocities.

Show solution

\(v_{1f} = \dfrac{2 - 4}{6}(6.0) = -2.0\ \text{m/s}\) (rebounds), \(v_{2f} = \dfrac{4}{6}(6.0) = 4.0\ \text{m/s}\).

Check momentum: \(12 = 2(-2) + 4(4) = 12\). Energy: \(36\ \text{J} = 4 + 32\ \text{J}\).

9.4 Variable mass: rockets

Derivation. In free space, a rocket of mass \(M\) and velocity \(v\) ejects mass \(-dM \gt 0\) in time \(dt\) at speed \(v_{\text{rel}}\) backward relative to the rocket. Momentum before: \(Mv\). After: \((M + dM)(v + dv) + (-dM)(v + dv - v_{\text{rel}})\). Setting these equal and dropping second-order terms gives \(M\,dv = -v_{\text{rel}}\,dM\). Integrating from \(M_i\) to \(M_f\):

Rocket equations

\[ \Delta v = v_{\text{rel}}\ln\frac{M_i}{M_f} \qquad (\text{Tsiolkovsky}), \qquad T = R\,v_{\text{rel}}\ \ (\text{thrust}), \]

where \(R = |dM/dt|\) is the propellant mass flow rate. Gravity and drag reduce the achievable \(\Delta v\) ("losses").

Example 9.4

A stage has exhaust speed 3.0 km/s, initial-to-final mass ratio 8.0 and burns 250 kg/s. Find its ideal \(\Delta v\) and its thrust.

Show solution

\(\Delta v = 3000\ln8 = 3000(2.079) = 6.24\ \text{km/s}\). \(T = Rv_{\text{rel}} = 250(3000) = 7.5\times10^5\ \text{N}\).

Low Earth orbit needs about 9.4 km/s including losses, which is why launch vehicles stage: the logarithm punishes carrying empty tank mass.

Common mistake

Applying \(\vec F = m\vec a\) to a variable-mass body as a whole. Use momentum bookkeeping on the rocket-plus-exhaust system, as in the derivation.

Practice problems

  1. A 0.145 kg baseball arrives at 40 m/s and leaves the bat at 50 m/s in the opposite direction; contact lasts 1.2 ms. Find the impulse and average force.
    Answer

    \(J = 0.145(90) = 13.1\ \text{N·s}\); \(F_{\text{avg}} = 13.05/0.0012 = 1.09\times10^4\ \text{N}\).

  2. Two skaters (60 kg and 90 kg) at rest push apart; the 60 kg skater moves off at 3.0 m/s. Find the other's velocity.
    Answer

    \(0 = 60(3.0) + 90v \Rightarrow v = -2.0\ \text{m/s}\).

  3. A 1500 kg car moving east at 20 m/s collides and locks with a 3000 kg truck moving north at 15 m/s. Find the wreck's velocity.
    Answer

    \(\vec P = (30\,000, 45\,000)\ \text{kg·m/s}\), so \(\vec V = (6.67, 10.0)\ \text{m/s}\): 12.0 m/s at \(56.3^\circ\) north of east.

  4. What mass ratio gives \(\Delta v = 9.0\ \text{km/s}\) with \(v_{\text{rel}} = 4.4\ \text{km/s}\) (single stage)?
    Answer

    \(M_i/M_f = e^{9.0/4.4} = e^{2.045} = 7.7\).

  5. Find the Earth–Moon center of mass (\(M_E = 5.97\times10^{24}\) kg, \(M_M = 7.35\times10^{22}\) kg, separation \(3.84\times10^8\) m).
    Answer

    \(x = dM_M/(M_E + M_M) = 4.67\times10^6\ \text{m}\) from Earth's centre, inside Earth (\(R_E = 6.37\times10^6\) m).

  6. A 1000 kg cannon fires a 10 kg shell horizontally at 500 m/s. Find the recoil speed.
    Answer

    \(v = 10(500)/1000 = 5.0\ \text{m/s}\).

10. Rotation

Halliday Ch. 10 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 10

A rigid body spinning about a fixed axis is described by a single angle \(\theta(t)\), just as 1D motion is described by \(x(t)\). Every linear quantity has a rotational twin: \(\omega\) for \(v\), \(\alpha\) for \(a\), torque for force, and moment of inertia for mass. The moment of inertia is the new idea: it depends not only on how much mass there is but on how far it sits from the axis, which is why a flywheel puts its mass at the rim.

10.1 Rotational kinematics

Definitions

Angular position \(\theta\) in radians (\(\theta = s/r\)); angular velocity \(\omega = d\theta/dt\) (rad/s); angular acceleration \(\alpha = d\omega/dt\). Counterclockwise is positive; as vectors, \(\vec\omega\) points along the axis by the right-hand rule. A point at radius \(r\) has

\[ v = \omega r, \qquad a_t = \alpha r, \qquad a_r = \omega^2r. \]

Constant angular acceleration

\[ \omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t, \qquad \theta - \theta_0 = \omega_0t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2, \qquad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha(\theta - \theta_0). \]

Example 10.1

A flywheel spins up from rest with \(\alpha = 3.0\ \text{rad/s}^2\) for 10 s. Find its final angular velocity and the number of revolutions turned.

Show solution

\(\omega = 30\ \text{rad/s}\) (287 rpm). \(\theta = \tfrac12(3.0)(100) = 150\ \text{rad} = 150/2\pi = 23.9\ \text{rev}\).

10.2 Rotational kinetic energy & moment of inertia

Summing \(\tfrac12m_iv_i^2\) with \(v_i = \omega r_i\) gives \(K = \tfrac12\left(\sum m_ir_i^2\right)\omega^2\). The bracket is the rotational inertia.

Definition (Moment of inertia)

\[ I = \sum_i m_ir_i^2 = \int r_\perp^2\,dm, \qquad K_{\text{rot}} = \tfrac12I\omega^2, \]

where \(r_\perp\) is the perpendicular distance from the rotation axis. \(I\) depends on the axis.

Derivations. Thin rod about its centre: \(dm = (M/L)dx\), \(I = \int_{-L/2}^{L/2}x^2(M/L)\,dx = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2\). Uniform disk about its axis: rings of radius \(r\), \(dm = \dfrac{M}{\pi R^2}2\pi r\,dr\), so \(I = \dfrac{2M}{R^2}\int_0^Rr^3dr = \tfrac12MR^2\). Solid sphere: stack disks of radius \(\sqrt{R^2 - z^2}\) and mass \(dm = \rho\pi(R^2 - z^2)dz\); each contributes \(\tfrac12dm(R^2 - z^2)\), and \(\tfrac12\rho\pi\int_{-R}^{R}(R^2 - z^2)^2dz = \tfrac12\rho\pi\cdot\tfrac{16}{15}R^5\). With \(\rho = 3M/(4\pi R^3)\) this is \(\tfrac25MR^2\).

Common moments of inertia

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{hoop / thin ring (axis)}: MR^2 && \text{solid disk / cylinder (axis)}: \tfrac12MR^2 \\ &\text{rod (centre)}: \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 && \text{rod (end)}: \tfrac13ML^2 \\ &\text{solid sphere}: \tfrac25MR^2 && \text{thin spherical shell}: \tfrac23MR^2 \end{aligned} \]

10.3 The parallel-axis theorem

Parallel-axis theorem

\[ I = I_{\text{com}} + Mh^2, \]

where \(h\) is the distance between the given axis and a parallel axis through the center of mass.

Proof. Put the com at the origin and the new axis through \((a, b)\), \(h^2 = a^2 + b^2\). Then \(I = \int[(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2]dm = \int(x^2 + y^2)dm - 2a\int x\,dm - 2b\int y\,dm + h^2\int dm\). The middle integrals vanish because the com is at the origin. \(\square\)

Example: rod about its end, \(I = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 + M(L/2)^2 = \tfrac13ML^2\). The theorem only works from the com axis; \(I_{\text{com}}\) is always the smallest among parallel axes.

10.4 Torque & Newton's second law for rotation

Definition (Torque)

\[ \vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F, \qquad \tau = rF\sin\phi = r_\perp F = rF_t, \]

where \(r_\perp\) is the moment arm (perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of action of \(\vec F\)).

Newton's second law for rotation

\[ \tau_{\text{net}} = I\alpha \qquad (\text{rigid body, fixed axis}). \]

Rotational work and power: \(W = \int\tau\,d\theta = \Delta K_{\text{rot}}\), \(P = \tau\omega\).

Example 10.2

A 2.0 kg mass hangs from a light cord wrapped around a pulley that is a uniform disk (\(M = 4.0\ \text{kg}\), \(R = 0.20\ \text{m}\)) on a frictionless axle. Find the acceleration of the mass, the tension and \(\alpha\).

Show solution

Mass (down positive): \(mg - T = ma\). Pulley: \(TR = I\alpha = \tfrac12MR^2(a/R) \Rightarrow T = \tfrac12Ma\). Combine:

\[ a = \frac{mg}{m + M/2} = \frac{19.6}{4.0} = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2, \qquad T = \tfrac12(4.0)(4.9) = 9.8\ \text{N}, \qquad \alpha = a/R = 24.5\ \text{rad/s}^2. \]

Limits: \(M \to 0\) gives free fall; a heavy pulley gives small \(a\). The pulley's "effective mass" is \(I/R^2 = M/2\).

Example 10.3

A uniform rod of length 1.2 m, pivoted at one end, is released from rest horizontally. Find its angular speed and the speed of its free end as it swings through vertical.

Show solution

The com drops \(L/2\): \(Mg\tfrac L2 = \tfrac12\left(\tfrac13ML^2\right)\omega^2 \Rightarrow \omega = \sqrt{3g/L} = \sqrt{24.5} = 4.95\ \text{rad/s}\).

Tip speed \(v = \omega L = \sqrt{3gL} = 5.94\ \text{m/s}\), faster than a stone dropped from height \(L\) (\(\sqrt{2gL} = 4.85\ \text{m/s}\)): the inner parts of the rod "push" the tip.

Common mistake

Using degrees or rpm in \(v = \omega r\) or \(K = \tfrac12I\omega^2\). These formulas require radians (rad/s). Also, the tension in a cord over a massive pulley is different on each side.

Practice problems

  1. A disk drive spins at 7200 rpm. Find \(\omega\), and the speed and centripetal acceleration of a point 4.5 cm from the axis.
    Answer

    \(\omega = 7200(2\pi)/60 = 754\ \text{rad/s}\); \(v = 33.9\ \text{m/s}\); \(a = \omega^2r = 2.56\times10^4\ \text{m/s}^2\).

  2. Four 0.50 kg point masses sit at the corners of a 0.40 m square. Find \(I\) about a perpendicular axis through the centre.
    Answer

    \(r^2 = 0.2^2 + 0.2^2 = 0.08\ \text{m}^2\); \(I = 4(0.5)(0.08) = 0.16\ \text{kg·m}^2\).

  3. A 6.0 N·m torque acts on a uniform disk (10 kg, radius 0.30 m) from rest. Find \(\alpha\) and the time to reach 100 rad/s.
    Answer

    \(I = 0.45\ \text{kg·m}^2\); \(\alpha = 13.3\ \text{rad/s}^2\); \(t = 7.5\ \text{s}\).

  4. Find \(I\) for a solid sphere about an axis tangent to its surface.
    Answer

    \(\tfrac25MR^2 + MR^2 = \tfrac75MR^2\).

  5. A wheel decelerates uniformly from 120 rad/s to rest in 40 revolutions. Find \(\alpha\).
    Answer

    \(\theta = 80\pi = 251\ \text{rad}\); \(\alpha = -\omega_0^2/(2\theta) = -14\,400/503 = -28.6\ \text{rad/s}^2\).

  6. Estimate Earth's rotational kinetic energy, treating it as a uniform sphere (\(M = 5.97\times10^{24}\) kg, \(R = 6.37\times10^6\) m).
    Answer

    \(I = 0.4MR^2 = 9.7\times10^{37}\ \text{kg·m}^2\), \(\omega = 7.29\times10^{-5}\ \text{rad/s}\), \(K = 2.6\times10^{29}\ \text{J}\). (Earth is denser at its core, so the true \(I\) is about 17% smaller.)

11. Rolling, torque & angular momentum

Halliday Ch. 11 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 11

Rolling combines translation and rotation, tied together by the no-slip condition. Because some energy must go into spin, a rolling object accelerates down a slope more slowly than a sliding one, and the more of its mass sits far from its axis, the slower it goes. Angular momentum is the rotational version of momentum, and its conservation explains spinning skaters, collapsing stars, satellite attitude control with reaction wheels, and the strange sideways motion of a gyroscope.

11.1 Rolling

Rolling without slipping

A wheel of radius \(R\) rolls without slipping when the contact point is instantaneously at rest: \(v_{\text{com}} = \omega R\), \(a_{\text{com}} = \alpha R\). Equivalently, the motion is pure rotation about the contact point at that instant; the top of the wheel moves at \(2v_{\text{com}}\).

Rolling energy & acceleration down an incline

\[ K = \tfrac12Mv_{\text{com}}^2 + \tfrac12I_{\text{com}}\omega^2 = \tfrac12Mv_{\text{com}}^2\left(1 + \frac{I_{\text{com}}}{MR^2}\right), \qquad a_{\text{com}} = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + I_{\text{com}}/(MR^2)}. \]

Derivation of the incline result. Along the slope: \(Mg\sin\theta - f_s = Ma\). About the com: \(f_sR = I\alpha = Ia/R\). Eliminate \(f_s = Ia/R^2\) to get the formula. Static friction is what supplies the torque; since the contact point does not slide, it does no work, so mechanical energy is conserved in rolling without slipping.

Example 11.1

A solid sphere, a solid cylinder and a hoop are released together from rest on a \(30^\circ\) incline and roll without slipping down a vertical drop of 2.0 m. Find each acceleration and final speed.

Show solution

Let \(\beta = I_{\text{com}}/MR^2\): sphere \(\tfrac25\), cylinder \(\tfrac12\), hoop 1. Then \(a = g\sin\theta/(1 + \beta)\) and, by energy, \(v = \sqrt{2gh/(1 + \beta)}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{sphere}&: a = 4.9/1.4 = 3.50\ \text{m/s}^2, & v &= \sqrt{39.2/1.4} = 5.29\ \text{m/s} \\ \text{cylinder}&: a = 4.9/1.5 = 3.27\ \text{m/s}^2, & v &= \sqrt{39.2/1.5} = 5.11\ \text{m/s} \\ \text{hoop}&: a = 4.9/2 = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2, & v &= \sqrt{39.2/2} = 4.43\ \text{m/s} \end{aligned} \]

The sphere wins regardless of mass or radius. A frictionless sliding block (\(\beta = 0\)) would beat them all at 6.26 m/s.

11.2 Angular momentum

Definitions

Angular momentum of a particle about a point O: \(\vec\ell = \vec r\times\vec p = m(\vec r\times\vec v)\), magnitude \(r_\perp p\). For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, the component along the axis is \(L = I\omega\).

Newton's second law in angular form

\[ \vec\tau_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec L}{dt}. \]

Proof for a particle: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}(\vec r\times\vec p) = \vec v\times m\vec v + \vec r\times\dfrac{d\vec p}{dt} = \vec r\times\vec F\). For a system, internal torques cancel (assuming central internal forces), leaving external torques. Torque and \(\vec L\) must be taken about the same point (a fixed point or the com).

11.3 Conservation of angular momentum

Conservation of angular momentum

If the net external torque on a system is zero, \(\vec L\) is constant: \(I_i\omega_i = I_f\omega_f\). This holds even if the system changes shape, and even when kinetic energy is not conserved.

Example 11.2

A disk (\(I_1 = 0.12\ \text{kg·m}^2\)) spins at 40 rad/s on a frictionless axle. A second disk (\(I_2 = 0.080\ \text{kg·m}^2\)), not rotating, is dropped onto it coaxially and they stick. Find the final angular velocity and the kinetic energy lost.

Show solution

No external torque about the axle: \(\omega_f = \dfrac{I_1\omega_1}{I_1 + I_2} = \dfrac{4.8}{0.20} = 24\ \text{rad/s}\).

\(K_i = \tfrac12(0.12)(1600) = 96\ \text{J}\), \(K_f = \tfrac12(0.20)(576) = 57.6\ \text{J}\). Lost: 38.4 J (40%) to friction between the disks, the rotational analogue of a perfectly inelastic collision (this is how a clutch engages).

Example 11.3

A skater spinning at 2.0 rev/s with arms out (\(I = 4.0\ \text{kg·m}^2\)) pulls them in to \(I = 1.6\ \text{kg·m}^2\). Find the new spin rate and the ratio of final to initial kinetic energy.

Show solution

\(\omega_f = (4.0/1.6)(2.0) = 5.0\ \text{rev/s}\). Since \(K = L^2/(2I)\) with \(L\) fixed, \(K_f/K_i = I_i/I_f = 2.5\). The extra energy comes from the work her muscles do pulling her arms inward against their tendency to fly outward.

Study tip: linear–angular dictionary

\(x \leftrightarrow \theta\), \(v \leftrightarrow \omega\), \(a \leftrightarrow \alpha\), \(m \leftrightarrow I\), \(F \leftrightarrow \tau\), \(p \leftrightarrow L\), \(F = ma \leftrightarrow \tau = I\alpha\), \(K = \tfrac12mv^2 \leftrightarrow \tfrac12I\omega^2\), \(P = Fv \leftrightarrow \tau\omega\). If you can solve the linear problem you can solve the angular one.

11.4 Gyroscopic precession

A gyroscope spinning rapidly with angular momentum \(L = I\omega\), supported at one end of its axle a distance \(r\) from its com, feels a gravitational torque \(\tau = Mgr\) that is horizontal and perpendicular to \(\vec L\). Since \(d\vec L = \vec\tau\,dt\) is perpendicular to \(\vec L\), the torque changes the direction of \(\vec L\), not its magnitude: the axle swings around horizontally instead of falling. In time \(dt\) the axle turns through \(d\phi = dL/L = \tau\,dt/L\), so the precession rate is

Precession rate (fast-spin approximation)

\[ \Omega = \frac{Mgr}{I\omega}. \]

Faster spin means slower precession. The same physics governs control-moment gyros on spacecraft and the precession of Earth's axis under solar and lunar torques (period about 26 000 years).

Common mistake

Assuming kinetic energy is conserved whenever angular momentum is. They are independent: shape changes and inelastic couplings change \(K\) while \(L\) stays fixed.

  1. Find the total kinetic energy of a 7.0 kg bowling ball (solid sphere) rolling without slipping at 5.0 m/s.
    Answer

  2. A yo-yo is a uniform disk of radius \(R\) unwinding from an axle of radius \(R/4\). Find its downward acceleration.
    Answer

    Answer

    \(\vec\ell = m\,\vec r\times\vec v = 2(12\hat k) = 24\hat k\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}\).

  4. A merry-go-round (uniform disk, 200 kg, radius 2.0 m) turns at 1.5 rad/s. A 40 kg child steps onto its rim, moving radially. Find the new \(\omega\).
    Answer

    \(I_i = 400\), \(I_f = 400 + 40(4) = 560\ \text{kg·m}^2\); \(\omega_f = 600/560 = 1.07\ \text{rad/s}\).

  5. A star of radius \(7\times10^5\) km rotating once per 25 days collapses to a 10 km neutron star (uniform-sphere model, no mass loss). Find the new period.
    Answer

    \(T \propto R^2\): \(T_f = 25(86\,400)(10/7\times10^5)^2 = 2.16\times10^6/4.9\times10^9 = 4.4\times10^{-4}\ \text{s}\), about 2300 rotations per second (real pulsars spin slower because of mass and angular-momentum loss).

12. Equilibrium & elasticity

Halliday Ch. 12 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 12

Structures, brackets, test fixtures and ladders are meant not to accelerate. Static equilibrium turns Newton's laws into algebra: forces sum to zero and torques sum to zero. The clever part is choosing the pivot point for the torque equation so that unknown forces drop out. Real materials then stretch, compress and shear slightly under these loads; elasticity quantifies how much, and tells you when a part will yield.

12.1 Static equilibrium

Conditions for static equilibrium

\[ \sum\vec F_{\text{ext}} = 0, \qquad \sum\vec\tau_{\text{ext}} = 0\ \ \text{about any point}, \qquad \vec P = 0. \]

If the net force is zero, the net torque is the same about every point, so you may choose the most convenient one. Gravity acts at the center of gravity, which coincides with the com when \(\vec g\) is uniform over the body.

Study tip

Take torques about the point where the most unknown forces act (a hinge, a contact point); those forces then have zero moment arm. In 2D you get at most three independent equations, so at most three unknowns can be found. More unknowns means the structure is statically indeterminate and you need elasticity to solve it.

Example 12.1

A uniform 6.0 m plank weighing 200 N rests on supports at each end. A 700 N worker stands 1.5 m from the left end. Find the support forces.

Show solution

Torques about the left support (counterclockwise positive): \(F_R(6.0) - 200(3.0) - 700(1.5) = 0 \Rightarrow F_R = 1650/6 = 275\ \text{N}\).

Vertical forces: \(F_L = 900 - 275 = 625\ \text{N}\).

Check with torques about the right end: \(F_L(6) = 200(3) + 700(4.5) = 3750 \Rightarrow F_L = 625\ \text{N}\). The nearer support carries more, as expected.

Example 12.2

A uniform 5.0 m ladder weighing 150 N leans against a frictionless wall at \(60^\circ\) to the floor. What minimum coefficient of static friction at the floor prevents slipping?

Show solution

Forces: weight \(W\) at the midpoint, wall force \(N_w\) (horizontal), floor normal \(N_f\), floor friction \(f\). Vertical: \(N_f = W = 150\ \text{N}\). Horizontal: \(f = N_w\).

Torques about the foot (removes \(N_f\) and \(f\)): \(N_w L\sin60^\circ = W\tfrac L2\cos60^\circ\), so

\[ N_w = \frac{W}{2\tan60^\circ} = \frac{150}{3.464} = 43.3\ \text{N}, \qquad \mu_s \ge \frac{f}{N_f} = \frac{43.3}{150} = 0.29. \]

Limit: as the ladder approaches vertical, \(\tan\theta\to\infty\) and no friction is needed, consistent with experience. The length cancels.

12.2 Stress, strain & elastic moduli

Definitions

Stress = force per area (Pa); strain = fractional deformation (dimensionless). In the linear elastic region stress \(=\) modulus \(\times\) strain:

\[ \frac{F}{A} = E\frac{\Delta L}{L}\ \ (\text{tension/compression}), \qquad \frac{F}{A} = G\frac{\Delta x}{L}\ \ (\text{shear}), \qquad \Delta p = -B\frac{\Delta V}{V}\ \ (\text{hydraulic}). \]

\(E\) is Young's modulus, \(G\) the shear modulus, \(B\) the bulk modulus. Beyond the yield strength deformation becomes permanent; at the ultimate strength the material breaks.

Typical values: steel \(E \approx 200\ \text{GPa}\), aluminium \(\approx 70\ \text{GPa}\), concrete \(\approx 30\ \text{GPa}\); water \(B \approx 2.2\ \text{GPa}\). A rod behaves like a spring with \(k = EA/L\).

Example 12.3

A 10 m steel cable of diameter 4.0 mm supports a 2000 N load. Find the stress, strain and elongation (\(E = 200\ \text{GPa}\)).

Show solution

\(A = \pi(0.0020)^2 = 1.26\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2\). Stress \(= 2000/1.26\times10^{-5} = 1.59\times10^8\ \text{Pa} = 159\ \text{MPa}\).

Strain \(= 1.59\times10^8/2.0\times10^{11} = 7.96\times10^{-4}\); \(\Delta L = 7.96\times10^{-4}(10) = 7.96\ \text{mm}\).

Check: 159 MPa is below the yield strength of typical cable steel (several hundred MPa), so the linear model is valid.

Common mistake

Using diameter instead of radius in \(A = \pi r^2\) (a factor of 4 error), or forgetting that a torque equation needs the perpendicular moment arm, not the distance along the beam, when forces are angled.

Practice problems

  1. A 3.0 m horizontal beam (400 N, uniform) is hinged at a wall and held by a cable from its far end at \(30^\circ\) above horizontal. A 600 N sign hangs from the far end. Find the cable tension and the hinge force.
    Answer

    Torques about the hinge: \(T\sin30^\circ(3) = 400(1.5) + 600(3) \Rightarrow T = 1600\ \text{N}\). Hinge: the cable pulls the beam toward the wall, so the hinge pushes it outward with \(H_x = T\cos30^\circ = 1386\ \text{N}\); vertically \(H_y = 1000 - T\sin30^\circ = 200\ \text{N}\) up.

  2. A 30 kg child sits 2.0 m from a seesaw's pivot. Where must a 45 kg child sit to balance?
    Answer

    \(30(2.0) = 45d \Rightarrow d = 1.33\ \text{m}\) on the other side.

  3. An aluminium rod (\(E = 70\ \text{GPa}\)), 2.0 m long with cross-section 1.0 cm\(^2\), carries 5000 N. Find its elongation.
    Answer

    \(\Delta L = FL/(AE) = 5000(2)/(10^{-4}\cdot7\times10^{10}) = 1.43\ \text{mm}\).

  4. By what fraction is seawater compressed at 4000 m depth (\(B = 2.2\ \text{GPa}\), \(\rho \approx 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3\))?
    Answer

    \(\Delta p = \rho gh = 3.92\times10^7\ \text{Pa}\); \(\Delta V/V = -\Delta p/B = -1.8\%\). Liquids are nearly, not perfectly, incompressible.

  5. Model a 2.0 m steel rod of cross-section 2.0 cm\(^2\) as a spring. Find \(k\).
    Answer

    \(k = EA/L = 2\times10^{11}(2\times10^{-4})/2 = 2.0\times10^7\ \text{N/m}\).

13. Gravitation

Halliday Ch. 13 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 13

The same force that drops an apple holds the Moon in orbit; Newton's step was to see that the Moon is falling too, just moving sideways fast enough to keep missing Earth. Gravity weakens as the inverse square of distance, which makes its potential energy fall off as \(-1/r\), gives closed elliptical orbits, and sets a finite escape speed. Orbital mechanics, from GPS satellites to interplanetary transfers, is this chapter applied with care.

13.1 Newton's law of gravitation

Law of universal gravitation

\[ F = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}, \qquad G = 6.674\times10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2, \]

attractive, along the line joining two point masses. Forces from several bodies add as vectors (superposition).

Shell theorem

A uniform spherical shell attracts an external particle as if all its mass were at its centre, and exerts zero net force on a particle inside it. Consequently a spherically symmetric body acts like a point mass outside itself, and inside a uniform sphere only the mass at smaller radius counts: \(g(r) = GMr/R^3\), growing linearly from zero at the centre.

Surface gravity is \(g = GM/R^2\). Measured \(g\) differs slightly from this because Earth rotates (reducing apparent weight by up to \(\omega^2R \approx 0.034\ \text{m/s}^2\) at the equator), is not perfectly spherical, and is not uniform.

13.2 Gravitational potential energy

Derivation. Choose \(U(\infty) = 0\). Moving \(m\) in from infinity to \(r\), gravity (radial component \(-GMm/r'^2\)) does work \(W = \int_\infty^r\left(-\dfrac{GMm}{r'^2}\right)dr' = \dfrac{GMm}{r}\), so \(U(r) = -W = -GMm/r\). Near the surface, \(U(R + h) - U(R) \approx GMmh/R^2 = mgh\), recovering the familiar result.

Potential energy & escape speed

\[ U = -\frac{GMm}{r}, \qquad v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}}. \]

Escape speed follows from \(\tfrac12mv^2 - GMm/R = 0\) (just reaching infinity with zero speed). It is independent of the direction of launch (ignoring atmosphere).

13.3 Kepler's laws & orbits

Kepler's laws

  1. Orbits: planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus.
  2. Areas: the line from the Sun to a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times. This is conservation of angular momentum, since gravity is a central force: \(dA/dt = L/(2m)\).
  3. Periods: \(T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3\), where \(a\) is the semi-major axis.

Derivation for a circular orbit. Gravity supplies the centripetal force: \(GMm/r^2 = mv^2/r\), so \(v = \sqrt{GM/r}\). The period \(T = 2\pi r/v\) gives \(T^2 = 4\pi^2r^3/(GM)\). The kinetic energy is \(K = \tfrac12mv^2 = GMm/(2r) = -\tfrac12U\), so the total is \(E = -GMm/(2r)\). For ellipses, replace \(r\) by \(a\).

Orbit relations

\[ v_{\text{circ}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}, \qquad T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2a^3}{GM}, \qquad E = -\frac{GMm}{2a}, \qquad r_pv_p = r_av_a. \]

Study tip

Use \(GM_E = 3.986\times10^{14}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2\) directly: it is known far more precisely than \(G\) or \(M_E\) separately. Bound orbits have \(E \lt 0\); \(E = 0\) is the escape (parabolic) boundary.

Common mistake

Using altitude instead of distance from Earth's centre in \(r\). Also, a satellite that fires its engine forward rises to a higher orbit where it ends up moving slower: total energy goes up but kinetic energy goes down.

Example 13.1

A space station orbits 400 km above Earth (\(R_E = 6.37\times10^6\) m). Find \(g\) at that altitude, the orbital speed, and the period.

Show solution

\(r = 6.77\times10^6\ \text{m}\). \(g = GM/r^2 = 3.986\times10^{14}/(6.77\times10^6)^2 = 8.70\ \text{m/s}^2\), only 11% less than at the surface. Astronauts float because they are in free fall, not because gravity is absent.

\[ v = \sqrt{GM/r} = \sqrt{5.89\times10^7} = 7.67\ \text{km/s}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = 5.54\times10^3\ \text{s} = 92\ \text{min}. \]

Example 13.2

Find the radius and altitude of a geostationary orbit (period = one sidereal day, 86 164 s).

Show solution \[ r = \left(\frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\frac{3.986\times10^{14}(86\,164)^2}{39.48}\right)^{1/3} = (7.50\times10^{22})^{1/3} = 4.22\times10^7\ \text{m}. \]

Altitude \(= 4.216\times10^7 - 6.37\times10^6 \approx 3.58\times10^7\ \text{m}\) (about 35 800 km). Speed \(2\pi r/T = 3.07\ \text{km/s}\), slower than low orbit, consistent with \(v \propto r^{-1/2}\).

Example 13.3

Compute the escape speeds from Earth and from the Moon (\(M = 7.35\times10^{22}\) kg, \(R = 1.737\times10^6\) m).

Show solution

Earth: \(\sqrt{2(3.986\times10^{14})/6.371\times10^6} = \sqrt{1.251\times10^8} = 11.2\ \text{km/s}\).

Moon: \(\sqrt{2(6.674\times10^{-11})(7.35\times10^{22})/1.737\times10^6} = \sqrt{5.65\times10^6} = 2.38\ \text{km/s}\). The low lunar escape speed is why the Apollo ascent stage could be so small.

Practice problems

  1. Find the Sun's mass from Earth's orbit (\(a = 1.496\times10^{11}\) m, \(T = 3.156\times10^7\) s).
    Answer

    \(M = 4\pi^2a^3/(GT^2) = 39.48(3.348\times10^{33})/(6.674\times10^{-11}\cdot9.96\times10^{14}) = 1.99\times10^{30}\ \text{kg}\).

    Answer

    \(M_E/x^2 = M_M/(d - x)^2 \Rightarrow x = d/(1 + \sqrt{M_M/M_E}) = d/1.111 = 3.46\times10^8\ \text{m}\) from Earth.

  3. How much energy is needed to move a 1000 kg satellite from a 400 km circular orbit to geostationary orbit?
    Answer

    \(\Delta E = \tfrac12GMm(1/r_1 - 1/r_2) = 1.993\times10^{17}(1.477\times10^{-7} - 2.37\times10^{-8}) = 2.47\times10^{10}\ \text{J}\).

  4. Mars orbits at \(a = 1.524\) AU. Find its period in years.
    Answer

    In solar units \(T^2 = a^3\): \(T = 1.524^{1.5} = 1.88\ \text{yr}\).

  5. For a uniform-density Earth, what is \(g\) halfway to the centre?
    Answer

    \(g(r) = g_0r/R = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2\).

  6. A comet has perihelion 0.60 AU and aphelion 30 AU. Find the ratio of its perihelion speed to aphelion speed.
    Answer

    Angular momentum: \(v_p/v_a = r_a/r_p = 50\).

14. Fluids

Halliday Ch. 14 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 14

A fluid cannot resist shear at rest, so it pushes perpendicular to every surface it touches; that push per area is pressure. In a fluid at rest, pressure grows with depth because each layer supports the weight of the fluid above it, and that depth dependence is the whole origin of buoyancy. In moving fluid, energy conservation per unit volume gives Bernoulli's equation: where the fluid speeds up, its pressure drops. Lift, carburettors, Pitot tubes and flow meters all follow.

14.1 Pressure & hydrostatics

Definitions

Density \(\rho = m/V\) (water \(1000\ \text{kg/m}^3\), air \(\approx 1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3\)). Pressure \(p = F/A\) for a force perpendicular to area \(A\); \(1\ \text{atm} = 1.013\times10^5\ \text{Pa}\). Gauge pressure = absolute pressure \(-\) atmospheric pressure.

Derivation. A horizontal slab of fluid of area \(A\) and thickness \(dy\) is in equilibrium: \(p(y)A - p(y + dy)A - \rho gA\,dy = 0\), so \(dp/dy = -\rho g\). For an incompressible fluid this integrates to:

Hydrostatic pressure

\[ p = p_0 + \rho gh \qquad (h = \text{depth below the level where } p = p_0). \]

Pressure depends only on depth, not on the container's shape. For the atmosphere, \(\rho\) varies with \(p\) and the result is approximately exponential instead.

Pascal's principle

A change in pressure applied to an enclosed incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid. A hydraulic press therefore multiplies force by the area ratio, \(F_2 = F_1A_2/A_1\), while the output piston moves a correspondingly smaller distance (energy is conserved).

Example 14.1

Find the gauge and absolute pressure at 30 m depth in seawater (\(\rho = 1025\ \text{kg/m}^3\)).

Show solution

\(p_g = \rho gh = 1025(9.8)(30) = 3.01\times10^5\ \text{Pa}\). Absolute: \(p = 1.013\times10^5 + 3.01\times10^5 = 4.03\times10^5\ \text{Pa} \approx 4.0\ \text{atm}\). Rule of thumb: every 10 m of water adds about one atmosphere.

14.2 Buoyancy

Archimedes' principle

A body wholly or partly immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces: \(F_b = \rho_{\text{fluid}}V_{\text{disp}}g\).

Reason: the surrounding fluid would push the same way on a "fluid parcel" of the same shape, and that parcel is in equilibrium, so the net pressure force equals its weight.

A floating body displaces its own weight: the submerged fraction is \(\rho_{\text{body}}/\rho_{\text{fluid}}\).

Example 14.2

What fraction of an iceberg (\(\rho = 917\ \text{kg/m}^3\)) floats above seawater (\(1025\ \text{kg/m}^3\))?

Show solution

Floating: \(\rho_iVg = \rho_wV_{\text{sub}}g \Rightarrow V_{\text{sub}}/V = 917/1025 = 0.895\). About 10.5% shows above the surface.

Common mistake

Using the body's density in \(F_b\). The buoyant force involves the fluid's density and the displaced volume. Also, use absolute pressure in gas laws but gauge pressure is fine for pressure differences.

14.3 Continuity & Bernoulli's equation

Ideal fluid assumptions: steady (velocity at each point constant in time), incompressible, nonviscous, irrotational. Under these, mass conservation in a flow tube gives the continuity equation, and energy conservation gives Bernoulli.

Equation of continuity

\[ A_1v_1 = A_2v_2 = Q\ \ (\text{volume flow rate, m}^3/\text{s}). \]

Derivation of Bernoulli. Follow a slug of fluid of volume \(\Delta V\) through a tube from section 1 to section 2. The surrounding fluid does net work \(W = p_1\Delta V - p_2\Delta V\) on it (pushing in at 1, being resisted at 2). By the work–energy theorem this equals the change in kinetic plus gravitational potential energy, \(\tfrac12\rho\Delta V(v_2^2 - v_1^2) + \rho\Delta Vg(y_2 - y_1)\). Divide by \(\Delta V\) and rearrange:

Bernoulli's equation

\[ p + \tfrac12\rho v^2 + \rho gy = \text{constant along a streamline}. \]

Every term is an energy per unit volume (Pa = J/m\(^3\)). With \(v = 0\) it reduces to hydrostatics.

Example 14.3

Water stands 4.0 m deep in an open tank. A 2.0 cm diameter hole opens at the bottom. Find the exit speed and volume flow rate.

Show solution

Points: top surface (\(p_0\), \(v \approx 0\), \(y = h\)) and jet (\(p_0\), \(v\), \(y = 0\)). Bernoulli gives Torricelli's law:

\[ v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{78.4} = 8.85\ \text{m/s}, \qquad Q = \pi(0.010)^2(8.85) = 2.78\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s} = 2.8\ \text{L/s}. \]

Same speed as a stone dropped 4 m: each parcel of water effectively falls from the surface. (Real orifices discharge less because the jet contracts.)

Example 14.4

Water flows at 2.0 m/s and 200 kPa in a horizontal pipe of 10 cm diameter, which narrows to 5.0 cm. Find the speed and pressure in the narrow section.

Show solution

Continuity: area ratio \((10/5)^2 = 4\), so \(v_2 = 8.0\ \text{m/s}\). Bernoulli (same height):

\[ p_2 = p_1 - \tfrac12\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = 200\,000 - 500(64 - 4) = 170\ \text{kPa}. \]

Pressure drops where the flow speeds up: the Venturi meter measures this drop to infer flow rate.

Practice problems

  1. A hydraulic lift has pistons of 2.0 cm and 20 cm diameter. What force on the small piston lifts a 12 000 N car?
    Answer

    Area ratio 100: \(F = 120\ \text{N}\).

  2. An object weighs 5.0 N in air and 3.2 N fully submerged in water. Find its density.
    Answer

    \(F_b = 1.8\ \text{N}\); \(\rho = \rho_w W/F_b = 1000(5.0/1.8) = 2.8\times10^3\ \text{kg/m}^3\) (aluminium-like).

  3. Find the height of a mercury barometer column (\(\rho = 13\,600\ \text{kg/m}^3\)) at 101.3 kPa.
    Answer

    \(h = p/(\rho g) = 101\,300/(13\,600\cdot9.8) = 0.760\ \text{m}\).

  4. Air (\(\rho = 1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3\)) flows at 70 m/s over a wing's top and 60 m/s under it. Estimate the lift on 20 m\(^2\) of wing.
    Answer

    \(\Delta p = \tfrac12(1.2)(4900 - 3600) = 780\ \text{Pa}\); lift \(\approx 780(20) = 1.56\times10^4\ \text{N}\).

  5. A Pitot tube reads a dynamic pressure of 1500 Pa in air (\(\rho = 1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3\)). Find the airspeed.
    Answer

    \(v = \sqrt{2\Delta p/\rho} = \sqrt{2500} = 50\ \text{m/s}\).

  6. How much mass can 1.0 m\(^3\) of helium (\(0.18\ \text{kg/m}^3\)) lift in air (\(1.20\ \text{kg/m}^3\)), ignoring the envelope?
    Answer

    Net upward force \((1.20 - 0.18)(9.8) = 10.0\ \text{N}\), i.e. about 1.0 kg.

15. Oscillations

Halliday Ch. 15 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 15

Displace almost any system from a stable equilibrium and it is pushed back with a force roughly proportional to the displacement, because any smooth potential-energy minimum looks like a parabola up close. The result is simple harmonic motion: sinusoidal oscillation at a frequency set by stiffness and inertia, independent of amplitude. Add friction and the oscillation decays; add a periodic driving force and you get resonance. This is the physics behind vibration testing, isolation mounts, clocks, and (with \(L, R, C\) in place of \(m, b, k\)) every RLC circuit.

15.1 Simple harmonic motion

Definition (SHM)

Motion governed by \(\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\omega^2x\). For a mass on an ideal spring, \(m\ddot x = -kx\) gives \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\).

Solution. The second-order linear ODE has characteristic equation \(r^2 + \omega^2 = 0\), \(r = \pm i\omega\), so the general real solution is \(x = c_1\cos\omega t + c_2\sin\omega t\), equivalently

SHM solution

\[ x(t) = x_m\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad v = -\omega x_m\sin(\omega t + \phi), \qquad a = -\omega^2x. \] \[ \omega = 2\pi f = \frac{2\pi}{T}, \qquad x_m = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (v_0/\omega)^2}, \qquad \tan\phi = -\frac{v_0}{\omega x_0}. \]

Amplitude \(x_m\) and phase \(\phi\) come from initial conditions; \(\omega\) comes only from the system. Maximum speed \(\omega x_m\), maximum acceleration \(\omega^2x_m\).

Example 15.1

A 0.50 kg mass on a spring (\(k = 200\ \text{N/m}\)) is at \(x_0 = 0.050\ \text{m}\) moving at \(v_0 = +1.0\ \text{m/s}\) at \(t = 0\). Find \(\omega\), \(f\), the amplitude, the phase, and the maximum speed and acceleration.

Show solution

\(\omega = \sqrt{200/0.50} = 20\ \text{rad/s}\), \(f = 3.18\ \text{Hz}\).

\[ x_m = \sqrt{0.050^2 + (1.0/20)^2} = 0.0707\ \text{m}. \]

Phase: \(x_m\cos\phi = 0.050\) and \(-\omega x_m\sin\phi = 1.0\) give \(\cos\phi = 0.707\), \(\sin\phi = -0.707\): \(\phi = -\pi/4\). (The quadrant matters: \(\tan\phi = -1\) alone would also allow \(3\pi/4\).)

\(v_{\max} = 20(0.0707) = 1.41\ \text{m/s}\), \(a_{\max} = 400(0.0707) = 28.3\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Energy check: \(\tfrac12kx_m^2 = 0.50\ \text{J}\), and initially \(\tfrac12(0.5)(1)^2 + \tfrac12(200)(0.05)^2 = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.50\ \text{J}\).

15.2 Energy in SHM

Energy

\[ U = \tfrac12kx^2 = \tfrac12kx_m^2\cos^2(\omega t + \phi), \quad K = \tfrac12mv^2 = \tfrac12kx_m^2\sin^2(\omega t + \phi), \quad E = \tfrac12kx_m^2. \]

Energy sloshes between kinetic and potential twice per cycle; each averages \(E/2\).

Study tip

To find \(\omega\) for any system, write its energy as \(E = \tfrac12m_{\text{eff}}\dot q^2 + \tfrac12k_{\text{eff}}q^2\); then \(\omega = \sqrt{k_{\text{eff}}/m_{\text{eff}}}\). For a vertical spring, gravity only shifts the equilibrium (by \(mg/k\)); \(\omega\) is unchanged.

15.3 Pendulums

Simple pendulum. The tangential force on the bob is \(-mg\sin\theta\), so \(mL\ddot\theta = -mg\sin\theta\). For small angles \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) (error under 1% for \(\theta \lt 14^\circ\)), giving SHM with \(\omega = \sqrt{g/L}\).

Physical pendulum. A rigid body pivoted a distance \(h\) from its com feels torque \(-mgh\sin\theta \approx -mgh\theta\), so \(I\ddot\theta = -mgh\theta\) with \(I\) about the pivot.

Pendulum periods (small angles)

\[ T_{\text{simple}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}, \qquad T_{\text{physical}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgh}}, \qquad T_{\text{torsion}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{\kappa}}. \]

Example 15.2

A uniform metre stick swings from a pivot at one end. Find its small-oscillation period and the length of the equivalent simple pendulum.

Show solution

\(I = \tfrac13mL^2\), \(h = L/2\):

\[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{mL^2/3}{mgL/2}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2L}{3g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{29.4}} = 1.64\ \text{s}. \]

Equivalent simple pendulum length \(2L/3 = 0.667\ \text{m}\): shorter than the stick, because mass near the pivot swings "faster" than a point bob at the end would.

15.4 Damped & driven oscillations

Damping. With a viscous damping force \(-b\dot x\): \(m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = 0\). Trying \(x = e^{rt}\) gives \(r = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}\) with \(\gamma = b/(2m)\). For light damping (\(\gamma \lt \omega_0\), underdamped):

Damped oscillator

\[ x(t) = x_me^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega't + \phi), \qquad \omega' = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}, \qquad E(t) \approx E_0e^{-2\gamma t}, \qquad Q = \frac{\omega_0}{2\gamma} = \frac{m\omega_0}{b}. \]

\(\gamma = \omega_0\) is critical damping (fastest return without overshoot, used in door closers and meter movements); \(\gamma \gt \omega_0\) is overdamped. For a lightly damped system \(Q \approx 2\pi\times\)(energy stored)/(energy lost per cycle).

Driven oscillations. Adding \(F_0\cos\omega_dt\), the steady-state solution oscillates at the driving frequency with amplitude

Steady-state response

\[ x_m(\omega_d) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 + (b\omega_d/m)^2}}. \]

The amplitude peaks near \(\omega_d \approx \omega_0\) (resonance), where it is about \(Q\) times the static deflection \(F_0/k\). The half-power bandwidth is \(\Delta\omega \approx \omega_0/Q\).

Engineering connection. In a sine-sweep vibration test, the response peaks you see are the structure's natural frequencies, and their sharpness gives \(Q\) (or the damping ratio \(\zeta = 1/(2Q)\)). Isolation mounts work by placing \(\omega_0\) well below the excitation frequency, where the response falls off.

Example 15.3

A 2.0 kg mass on a spring (\(k = 800\ \text{N/m}\)) has damping \(b = 4.0\ \text{kg/s}\). Find \(\omega_0\), \(\omega'\), \(Q\), and the time for the amplitude to halve. Then find the steady-state amplitude when driven at resonance by \(F_0 = 10\ \text{N}\).

Show solution

\(\omega_0 = \sqrt{400} = 20\ \text{rad/s}\); \(\gamma = 4/(2\cdot2) = 1.0\ \text{s}^{-1}\); \(\omega' = \sqrt{399} = 19.97\ \text{rad/s}\) (barely shifted); \(Q = 20/2 = 10\).

Half amplitude: \(e^{-\gamma t} = \tfrac12 \Rightarrow t = \ln2/\gamma = 0.69\ \text{s}\), about 2.2 cycles.

At \(\omega_d = \omega_0\): \(x_m = \dfrac{F_0/m}{b\omega_0/m} = \dfrac{F_0}{b\omega_0} = \dfrac{10}{80} = 0.125\ \text{m}\). Static deflection \(F_0/k = 0.0125\ \text{m}\): amplification \(= 10 = Q\), as predicted.

Common mistake

Confusing \(\omega\) (rad/s) with \(f\) (Hz): \(\omega = 2\pi f\). Also, the pendulum formula \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\) fails for large amplitudes (the true period is longer).

Practice problems

  1. Find the period of a 1.0 m pendulum on Mars (\(g = 3.71\ \text{m/s}^2\)).
    Answer

    \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{1/3.71} = 3.26\ \text{s}\) (vs 2.01 s on Earth).

  2. A 0.25 kg mass oscillates on a spring with period 0.40 s. Find \(k\).
    Answer

    \(k = 4\pi^2m/T^2 = 39.48(0.25)/0.16 = 61.7\ \text{N/m}\).

  3. At what displacement is the kinetic energy equal to the potential energy in SHM?
    Answer

    \(\tfrac12kx^2 = \tfrac12E\) with \(E = \tfrac12kx_m^2\): \(x = \pm x_m/\sqrt2\).

  4. A mass hung on a spring stretches it 4.9 cm at equilibrium. Find the natural frequency.
    Answer

    \(k/m = g/\delta = 200\ \text{s}^{-2}\); \(f = \sqrt{200}/(2\pi) = 2.25\ \text{Hz}\).

  5. A lightly damped oscillator loses 5% of its amplitude each cycle. Estimate \(Q\).
    Answer

    Energy ratio per cycle \(0.95^2 = 0.9025\), fraction lost 0.0975; \(Q \approx 2\pi/0.0975 \approx 64\).

  6. Springs \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) support a mass in parallel, then in series. Find the effective stiffness in each case.
    Answer

    Parallel (same stretch, forces add): \(k = k_1 + k_2\). Series (same force, stretches add): \(1/k = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2\). (Springs combine like conductances \(1/R\), the opposite of resistors.)

16. Waves I: transverse waves on strings

Halliday Ch. 16 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 16

A wave is a disturbance that travels while the medium itself only jiggles in place. It carries energy and momentum without carrying matter. Because the wave equation is linear, waves pass through each other and simply add: two crests make a bigger crest, a crest and a trough cancel. Confine a wave between two fixed ends and only certain wavelengths fit, producing standing waves at discrete resonant frequencies, which is how every string instrument, and many structural vibration modes, work.

16.1 Traveling waves

Definitions

Any function \(y(x,t) = f(x - vt)\) is a shape moving in the \(+x\) direction at speed \(v\) (and \(f(x + vt)\) moves in \(-x\)). A sinusoidal traveling wave:

\[ y(x,t) = y_m\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}, \quad \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f, \quad v = \frac{\omega}{k} = \lambda f. \]

\(y_m\) is the amplitude, \(k\) the angular wave number (rad/m), \(\lambda\) the wavelength. In a transverse wave the medium moves perpendicular to the propagation direction; in a longitudinal wave (sound) it moves parallel.

Distinguish the wave speed \(v\) (constant, set by the medium) from the transverse velocity of a string element, \(u = \partial y/\partial t = -\omega y_m\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)\), which oscillates with maximum \(\omega y_m\).

Example 16.1

A wave on a string is \(y = (0.020\ \text{m})\sin(4.0x - 300t)\) (SI units). Find its direction, wavelength, frequency, speed and maximum transverse speed.

Show solution

The form \(kx - \omega t\) means travel in \(+x\). \(\lambda = 2\pi/4.0 = 1.57\ \text{m}\); \(f = 300/2\pi = 47.7\ \text{Hz}\); \(v = \omega/k = 75\ \text{m/s}\); \(u_{\max} = \omega y_m = 300(0.020) = 6.0\ \text{m/s}\).

Check: \(\lambda f = 1.571(47.75) = 75\ \text{m/s}\).

16.2 Wave speed, energy & the wave equation

Derivation. Take a string with tension \(\tau\) (Halliday's symbol; not torque here) and linear density \(\mu\), with small slopes. An element of length \(dx\) feels tension at each end along the local tangent; the net vertical force is \(\tau\left[\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\big|_{x + dx} - \frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\big|_x\right] = \tau\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}dx\). Newton's second law for the element of mass \(\mu\,dx\):

The wave equation

\[ \frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2}, \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\mu}}. \]

Any \(f(x \pm vt)\) satisfies it (check by the chain rule). Speed is set by the medium (a restoring "stiffness" over an inertia), not by frequency or amplitude.

Power transmitted by a sinusoidal wave

\[ P_{\text{avg}} = \tfrac12\mu v\omega^2y_m^2. \]

Proportional to the square of both amplitude and frequency, a pattern common to all waves.

Example 16.2

A string with \(\mu = 0.050\ \text{kg/m}\) under 80 N tension carries a 60 Hz wave of amplitude 6.0 mm. Find the wave speed, wavelength and average power.

Show solution

\(v = \sqrt{80/0.050} = 40\ \text{m/s}\); \(\lambda = v/f = 0.667\ \text{m}\); \(\omega = 2\pi(60) = 377\ \text{rad/s}\).

\[ P = \tfrac12(0.050)(40)(377)^2(0.0060)^2 = (1.0)(1.42\times10^5)(3.6\times10^{-5}) = 5.1\ \text{W}. \]

16.3 Superposition & interference

Principle of superposition

Overlapping waves add algebraically: \(y'(x,t) = y_1(x,t) + y_2(x,t)\). They do not alter each other's travel. Valid because the wave equation is linear (small amplitudes).

Two waves of equal amplitude and frequency traveling the same way, offset by phase \(\phi\), combine (using \(\sin\alpha + \sin\beta = 2\sin\frac{\alpha + \beta}{2}\cos\frac{\alpha - \beta}{2}\)) into

\[ y' = \left[2y_m\cos\tfrac{\phi}{2}\right]\sin\left(kx - \omega t + \tfrac{\phi}{2}\right). \]

\(\phi = 0\): fully constructive (amplitude \(2y_m\)); \(\phi = \pi\): fully destructive (zero). Phasors (rotating vectors whose projections are the waves) handle unequal amplitudes, exactly like AC circuit analysis.

16.4 Standing waves & resonance

Two identical waves traveling in opposite directions add to

\[ y' = \left[2y_m\sin kx\right]\cos\omega t. \]

Each point oscillates in place with amplitude \(|2y_m\sin kx|\): nodes (zero amplitude) at \(x = n\lambda/2\), antinodes halfway between. Adjacent nodes are \(\lambda/2\) apart.

String fixed at both ends

\[ \lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \qquad f_n = \frac{nv}{2L} = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{\mu}}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]

The \(n = 1\) mode is the fundamental (first harmonic); all harmonics are integer multiples of it.

Example 16.3

A guitar string has vibrating length 0.65 m and \(\mu = 1.2\times10^{-3}\ \text{kg/m}\). What tension tunes its fundamental to 196 Hz?

Show solution

\(v = 2Lf_1 = 2(0.65)(196) = 254.8\ \text{m/s}\); \(\tau = \mu v^2 = 1.2\times10^{-3}(6.49\times10^4) = 77.9\ \text{N}\).

Since \(f \propto \sqrt\tau\), raising the pitch by a semitone (factor \(2^{1/12}\)) needs about 12% more tension.

Common mistake

Thinking a higher frequency travels faster on the same string. The speed is fixed by \(\tau\) and \(\mu\); a higher frequency simply has a shorter wavelength.

Practice problems

  1. A wave is \(y = 0.030\sin(2\pi x/0.50 - 2\pi(40)t)\) (SI). Find \(\lambda\), \(f\) and \(v\).
    Answer

    \(\lambda = 0.50\ \text{m}\), \(f = 40\ \text{Hz}\), \(v = 20\ \text{m/s}\).

  2. The third harmonic of a 1.2 m string is 450 Hz. Find the fundamental and the wave speed.
    Answer

    \(f_1 = 150\ \text{Hz}\); \(v = 2Lf_1 = 360\ \text{m/s}\).

  3. By what factor does the wave speed change if the tension doubles?
    Answer

    \(v \propto \sqrt\tau\): a factor \(\sqrt2 \approx 1.41\).

  4. Two equal-amplitude waves interfere to give a resultant with the same amplitude as each wave. What is their phase difference?
    Answer

    \(2\cos(\phi/2) = 1 \Rightarrow \phi = 2\pi/3 = 120^\circ\).

  5. If the amplitude of a wave on a string is doubled at fixed frequency, what happens to the power?
    Answer

    \(P \propto y_m^2\): it quadruples.

17. Waves II: sound

Halliday Ch. 17 · OpenStax Vol. 1 Ch. 17

Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave: air molecules shuffle back and forth along the direction of travel, creating alternating compressions and rarefactions. Its speed is set by the medium's springiness (bulk modulus) against its inertia (density). Everything from the last chapter carries over (superposition, standing waves, energy flow), with three new ideas: the logarithmic decibel scale that matches human hearing, beats between nearby frequencies, and the Doppler shift caused by moving sources and observers.

17.1 Sound waves

Speed of sound

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}}\ \ (\text{general fluid}), \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma RT}{M}}\ \ (\text{ideal gas}). \]

Same structure as \(\sqrt{\tau/\mu}\): stiffness over inertia. In air at 20 °C, \(v \approx 343\ \text{m/s}\); in water about 1480 m/s; in steel about 5900 m/s (longitudinal). The gas formula uses the adiabatic bulk modulus \(B = \gamma p\) because compressions happen too fast for heat to flow (Chapter 19).

A sinusoidal sound wave can be described by displacement \(s = s_m\cos(kx - \omega t)\) or by pressure variation \(\Delta p = \Delta p_m\sin(kx - \omega t)\), with \(\Delta p_m = v\rho\omega s_m\). The pressure wave is \(90^\circ\) out of phase with the displacement wave: pressure extremes occur where displacement is zero.

Example 17.1

Compute the speed of sound in air at 20 °C (\(\gamma = 1.40\), \(M = 0.0290\ \text{kg/mol}\)), and at \(-50\) °C (typical airliner cruise altitude).

Show solution \[ v = \sqrt{\frac{1.40(8.314)(293.15)}{0.0290}} = \sqrt{1.177\times10^5} = 343\ \text{m/s}. \]

Since \(v \propto \sqrt T\): \(v(223\ \text{K}) = 343\sqrt{223.15/293.15} = 299\ \text{m/s}\). That is why Mach number, not airspeed, is quoted at altitude.

17.2 Intensity & decibels

Intensity & sound level

\[ I = \frac{P}{A} = \tfrac12\rho v\omega^2s_m^2, \qquad I = \frac{P_s}{4\pi r^2}\ \ (\text{isotropic point source}), \qquad \beta = (10\ \text{dB})\log_{10}\frac{I}{I_0}, \]

with reference intensity \(I_0 = 10^{-12}\ \text{W/m}^2\) (threshold of hearing). Every factor of 10 in intensity adds 10 dB; doubling adds 3 dB; doubling the distance from a point source subtracts 6 dB.

Example 17.2

A source radiates 20 W of acoustic power uniformly in all directions. Find the sound level at 10 m and at 40 m.

Show solution

\(I(10\ \text{m}) = 20/(4\pi\cdot100) = 1.59\times10^{-2}\ \text{W/m}^2\), so \(\beta = 10\log_{10}(1.59\times10^{10}) = 102\ \text{dB}\).

At 40 m the intensity is \(16\times\) smaller: \(\beta = 102 - 10\log_{10}16 = 102 - 12 = 90\ \text{dB}\).

17.3 Interference & pipes

Two in-phase sources produce a path-length difference \(\Delta L\) at a listener, giving a phase difference \(\phi = 2\pi\Delta L/\lambda\). Fully constructive when \(\Delta L = m\lambda\); fully destructive when \(\Delta L = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda\).

In an air column, an open end is a displacement antinode (pressure node) and a closed end is a displacement node. Fitting standing waves:

Resonant pipes

\[ \text{open at both ends: } f_n = \frac{nv}{2L},\ n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \qquad \text{closed at one end: } f_n = \frac{nv}{4L},\ n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots \]

A pipe closed at one end has only odd harmonics and a fundamental an octave lower than an open pipe of the same length.

Example 17.3

An organ pipe 0.80 m long is closed at one end. Find its three lowest resonant frequencies (\(v = 343\ \text{m/s}\)).

Show solution

\(f_1 = v/(4L) = 343/3.2 = 107\ \text{Hz}\); then \(f_3 = 322\ \text{Hz}\), \(f_5 = 536\ \text{Hz}\). The quarter-wavelength fundamental is \(\lambda = 3.2\ \text{m}\), four times the pipe length.

17.4 Beats & the Doppler effect

Beats. Adding \(\cos\omega_1t + \cos\omega_2t = 2\cos\left(\tfrac{\omega_1 - \omega_2}{2}t\right)\cos\left(\tfrac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}t\right)\): a tone at the average frequency whose loudness swells and fades. Loudness peaks twice per envelope cycle, so the beat frequency is \(f_{\text{beat}} = |f_1 - f_2|\). Musicians tune by eliminating beats.

Doppler effect (sound)

\[ f' = f\,\frac{v \pm v_D}{v \mp v_S}, \]

\(v\) = speed of sound in the (still) air, \(v_D\) = detector speed, \(v_S\) = source speed. Choose signs so that motion toward the other party raises the frequency: upper signs for approaching, lower for receding. If \(v_S \gt v\), a shock wave forms with Mach cone half-angle \(\sin\theta = v/v_S\).

Example 17.4

An ambulance siren emits 800 Hz while moving at 30 m/s. What frequency does a stationary pedestrian hear as it approaches, and after it passes?

Show solution

Approaching (source toward detector, denominator decreases): \(f' = 800\cdot\dfrac{343}{343 - 30} = 877\ \text{Hz}\).

Receding: \(f' = 800\cdot\dfrac{343}{343 + 30} = 736\ \text{Hz}\).

Check: the shift is asymmetric (+77, \(-64\) Hz), a signature of a moving source; for \(v_S \ll v\) both approach \(\pm f v_S/v = \pm 70\ \text{Hz}\).

Common mistake

Memorising sign rules mechanically and getting a lower pitch for an approaching source. Always sanity-check: approach means higher frequency. Also, source and detector motion are not interchangeable for sound (the medium defines a preferred frame).

Practice problems

  1. A 440 Hz tuning fork and a string give 3 beats/s. Tightening the string slightly makes the beats faster. What was the string's frequency?
    Answer

    Either 437 or 443 Hz; tightening raises \(f\) and increases the difference, so it was 443 Hz.

  2. Two in-phase speakers are at distances differing by 0.85 m from a listener. What is the lowest frequency giving destructive interference there?
    Answer

    \(\Delta L = \lambda/2 \Rightarrow \lambda = 1.70\ \text{m}\), \(f = 343/1.70 = 202\ \text{Hz}\).

  3. How much does the sound level rise when intensity increases 100-fold?
    Answer

    \(10\log_{10}100 = 20\ \text{dB}\).

  4. Find the fundamental of a 0.50 m pipe open at both ends.
    Answer

    \(f_1 = v/(2L) = 343\ \text{Hz}\).

  5. A car drives at 25 m/s toward a wall, sounding a 500 Hz horn. What frequency does the driver hear in the echo?
    Answer

    Wall (stationary detector) hears \(500\cdot343/318 = 539\ \text{Hz}\); it re-emits this as a stationary source to a moving detector: \(539\cdot368/343 = 579\ \text{Hz}\). Compactly \(f'' = f(v + u)/(v - u)\).

  6. A jet flies at Mach 2.0. Find the half-angle of its shock cone.
    Answer

    \(\sin\theta = 1/2 \Rightarrow \theta = 30^\circ\).

18. Temperature, heat & the first law of thermodynamics

Halliday Ch. 18 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 1 & 3

Temperature measures how vigorously the microscopic constituents of matter jiggle; heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Neither is "contained" in a body as heat: what a body has is internal energy, which can be changed either by heating it or by doing work on it. The first law of thermodynamics is simply energy conservation written with both channels included. The rest of the chapter covers how materials respond to heat (expansion, temperature rise, phase change) and how heat moves (conduction, convection, radiation).

18.1 Temperature & thermal expansion

Zeroth law of thermodynamics

If bodies A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body T, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This is what makes thermometers meaningful: temperature is the property that is equal for bodies in thermal equilibrium.

Scales: \(T_K = T_C + 273.15\); \(T_F = \tfrac95T_C + 32\). The kelvin scale is absolute (0 K is the lower limit) and must be used in every formula that contains \(T\) other than as a difference.

Thermal expansion

\[ \Delta L = L\alpha\,\Delta T, \qquad \Delta V = V\beta\,\Delta T, \qquad \beta \approx 3\alpha\ \ (\text{isotropic solids}). \]

Typical \(\alpha\): steel \(12\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}\), aluminium \(23\times10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}\). Holes expand as if filled with the material.

Example 18.1

A 25 m steel rail is laid at \(-10\) °C. How large a gap must be left for it to reach 40 °C without buckling?

Show solution

\(\Delta L = (12\times10^{-6})(25)(50) = 1.5\times10^{-2}\ \text{m} = 15\ \text{mm}\). If constrained instead, the thermal stress would be \(E\alpha\Delta T = (2\times10^{11})(12\times10^{-6})(50) = 120\ \text{MPa}\), which is large.

18.2 Heat capacity & phase change

Definitions

Heat \(Q\) is energy transferred between a system and its environment because of a temperature difference (positive when absorbed). Specific heat \(c\): \(Q = mc\,\Delta T\). Latent heat \(L\): \(Q = Lm\) to change phase at constant temperature.

Values used in these notes: water \(c = 4186\ \text{J/(kg·K)}\), ice \(c \approx 2100\ \text{J/(kg·K)}\), aluminium \(c = 900\ \text{J/(kg·K)}\); water \(L_F = 333\ \text{kJ/kg}\) (melting), \(L_V = 2256\ \text{kJ/kg}\) (boiling).

Example 18.2

A 0.20 kg aluminium part at 90 °C is quenched in 0.50 kg of water at 20 °C in an insulated container. Find the final temperature.

Show solution

Heat lost by aluminium = heat gained by water (\(\sum Q = 0\)):

\[ 0.20(900)(90 - T) = 0.50(4186)(T - 20) \Rightarrow 180(90 - T) = 2093(T - 20) \Rightarrow T = \frac{16\,200 + 41\,860}{2273} = 25.5\ ^\circ\text{C}. \]

Check: close to the water's initial temperature because water's heat capacity (2093 J/K) dwarfs the aluminium's (180 J/K).

Example 18.3

How much heat turns 0.10 kg of ice at \(-10\) °C into steam at 100 °C?

Show solution \[ \begin{aligned} Q &= mc_{\text{ice}}(10) + mL_F + mc_w(100) + mL_V \\ &= 2100 + 33\,300 + 41\,860 + 225\,600 = 3.03\times10^5\ \text{J}. \end{aligned} \]

Vaporisation dominates (75%): boiling water away takes over five times the heat needed to bring it from 0 to 100 °C.

18.3 Work & the first law

A gas pushing a piston of area \(A\) through distance \(ds\) does work \(dW = F\,ds = pA\,ds = p\,dV\). Hence the work done by the gas is \(W = \int_{V_i}^{V_f}p\,dV\), the area under the path on a \(p\)–\(V\) diagram. It depends on the path, not just the end states, and so does \(Q\).

First law of thermodynamics

\[ \Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W \qquad \left(dE_{\text{int}} = dQ - dW\right), \]

with \(Q\) the heat added to the system and \(W\) the work done by the system (Halliday's convention; some texts and most chemists use \(W\) done on the system, flipping the sign). \(E_{\text{int}}\) is a state function even though \(Q\) and \(W\) are not.

Special cases

Adiabatic: \(Q = 0 \Rightarrow \Delta E_{\text{int}} = -W\). Constant volume: \(W = 0 \Rightarrow \Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q\). Cyclic: \(\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0 \Rightarrow Q = W\). Free expansion: \(Q = W = 0 \Rightarrow \Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0\).

Example 18.4

A gas expands at constant pressure \(2.0\times10^5\ \text{Pa}\) from 0.010 to 0.025 m\(^3\) while absorbing 8000 J of heat. Find the work done and the change in internal energy.

Show solution

\(W = p\Delta V = 2.0\times10^5(0.015) = 3000\ \text{J}\). \(\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 8000 - 3000 = 5000\ \text{J}\). Units: \(\text{Pa·m}^3 = \text{N·m} = \text{J}\).

Common mistake

Mixing sign conventions between textbooks. Decide once: here \(W \gt 0\) means the gas expands and does work on its surroundings.

18.4 Heat transfer

Conduction, radiation

\[ P_{\text{cond}} = kA\frac{T_H - T_C}{L}, \qquad R = \frac{L}{k}\ \ (\text{thermal resistance per area; layers add in series}), \] \[ P_{\text{rad}} = \sigma\varepsilon AT^4, \qquad P_{\text{net}} = \sigma\varepsilon A\left(T^4 - T_{\text{env}}^4\right), \qquad \sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \text{W/(m}^2\text{·K}^4). \]

\(k\) is thermal conductivity, \(\varepsilon\) (0 to 1) the emissivity. Convection (heat carried by bulk fluid motion) is usually modelled empirically as \(P = hA\Delta T\).

Conduction is the thermal analogue of Ohm's law: heat current \(P\) = temperature difference / thermal resistance \(L/(kA)\). Layered walls are resistors in series; parallel heat paths are resistors in parallel.

Example 18.5

A spacecraft radiator (\(\varepsilon = 0.85\)) must reject 500 W to deep space at a surface temperature of 300 K. Ignoring absorbed sunlight and back-radiation, what area is needed?

Show solution \[ A = \frac{P}{\sigma\varepsilon T^4} = \frac{500}{(5.67\times10^{-8})(0.85)(8.1\times10^9)} = \frac{500}{390} = 1.28\ \text{m}^2. \]

Because of the \(T^4\), running the radiator 10% hotter cuts the area by about 32%. In vacuum, radiation is the only way to dump heat overboard.

    Answer

  2. A steel plate has a 2.000 cm hole at 20 °C. To what temperature must the plate be heated for the hole to accept a 2.010 cm pin?
    Answer

    \(\Delta T = 0.010/(2.000\cdot12\times10^{-6}) = 417\ \text{K}\); \(T \approx 437\ ^\circ\text{C}\).

    \(P = 0.80(1.5)(15)/0.004 = 4500\ \text{W}\). Real windows lose far less because still-air films at each surface add much more thermal resistance than the glass.

  4. A sphere of radius 0.10 m at 500 K (\(\varepsilon = 0.90\)) sits in 300 K surroundings. Find the net radiated power.

19. The kinetic theory of gases

Halliday Ch. 19 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 2 & 3

A gas is an enormous number of tiny molecules flying around and bouncing off the walls. Kinetic theory connects that microscopic picture to the macroscopic quantities we measure: pressure is the average momentum delivered to the walls per second per area, and temperature is a measure of the average translational kinetic energy per molecule. From those two facts follow the ideal gas law, the speeds of molecules, why diatomic gases need more heat per degree than monatomic ones, and the \(pV^\gamma\) law for rapid compressions in engines and compressors.

19.1 The ideal gas law

Ideal gas law

\[ pV = nRT = NkT, \qquad R = 8.314\ \text{J/(mol·K)}, \quad k = \frac{R}{N_A} = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}, \quad N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}. \]

Valid for low density (molecules far apart, negligible intermolecular forces); \(T\) in kelvin, \(p\) absolute.

Isothermal work

\[ W = \int_{V_i}^{V_f}\frac{nRT}{V}\,dV = nRT\ln\frac{V_f}{V_i}\qquad (T\ \text{constant}). \]

Since \(E_{\text{int}}\) of an ideal gas depends only on \(T\), an isothermal process has \(\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0\) and \(Q = W\).

Example 19.1

2.0 mol of ideal gas at 300 K is compressed isothermally from 0.040 m\(^3\) to 0.010 m\(^3\). Find the work done by the gas and the heat exchanged.

Show solution

\(W = 2.0(8.314)(300)\ln(0.25) = 4988(-1.386) = -6.92\times10^3\ \text{J}\). Negative: work is done on the gas. With \(\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0\), \(Q = W = -6.92\ \text{kJ}\): heat flows out to keep the temperature fixed.

19.2 Pressure, temperature & molecular speed

Derivation. A molecule of mass \(m\) in a cubic box of side \(L\) bounces elastically off a wall perpendicular to \(x\), delivering momentum \(2mv_x\) per hit, once every \(2L/v_x\). Its average force on the wall is \(mv_x^2/L\). Summing over \(N\) molecules and dividing by area \(L^2\): \(p = \dfrac{Nm}{V}\overline{v_x^2}\). Random motion makes \(\overline{v_x^2} = \tfrac13\overline{v^2}\), so

\[ p = \frac{Nm\,v_{\text{rms}}^2}{3V} = \frac{nM\,v_{\text{rms}}^2}{3V}. \]

Comparing with \(pV = nRT\) gives the results below, where \(M\) is the molar mass.

Kinetic-theory results

\[ v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}, \qquad K_{\text{avg}} = \tfrac12mv_{\text{rms}}^2 = \tfrac32kT, \qquad \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt2\,\pi d^2N/V}\ \ (\text{mean free path}). \]

Temperature is a direct measure of average translational kinetic energy, the same for every gas at a given \(T\). The speeds follow the Maxwell distribution, with most-probable speed \(\sqrt{2RT/M}\) and mean speed \(\sqrt{8RT/(\pi M)}\), both a bit below \(v_{\text{rms}}\).

Example 19.2

Find the rms speed of nitrogen molecules (\(M = 0.028\ \text{kg/mol}\)) at 300 K, and their average translational kinetic energy.

Show solution

\(v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3(8.314)(300)/0.028} = \sqrt{2.67\times10^5} = 517\ \text{m/s}\), comparable to (and larger than) the speed of sound, as it should be since sound is carried by molecular motion.

\(K_{\text{avg}} = 1.5(1.381\times10^{-23})(300) = 6.21\times10^{-21}\ \text{J}\) (about 0.039 eV).

19.3 Molar specific heats & equipartition

Equipartition of energy

In thermal equilibrium, each independent quadratic degree of freedom (translation along an axis, rotation about an axis, and each of the kinetic and potential terms of a vibration) holds on average \(\tfrac12kT\) per molecule, i.e. \(\tfrac12RT\) per mole. Classical result; quantum effects "freeze out" modes whose energy spacing is large compared with \(kT\).

Monatomic gas: 3 translational degrees of freedom, \(E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac32nRT\), so \(C_V = \tfrac32R\). Diatomic near room temperature: 3 translational + 2 rotational, \(C_V = \tfrac52R\) (vibration is frozen out until high temperature).

Derivation of \(C_p - C_V = R\). At constant pressure, \(Q = nC_p\Delta T = \Delta E_{\text{int}} + p\Delta V = nC_V\Delta T + nR\Delta T\). Extra heat is needed because the gas also does work as it expands.

Molar heats

\[ \Delta E_{\text{int}} = nC_V\Delta T\ \ (\text{any process}), \qquad C_p = C_V + R, \qquad \gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_V} = \tfrac53\ (\text{monatomic}),\ \tfrac75\ (\text{diatomic}). \]

Common mistake

Using \(\Delta E_{\text{int}} = nC_p\Delta T\) for a constant-pressure process. For an ideal gas \(\Delta E_{\text{int}} = nC_V\Delta T\) for every process; \(C_p\) appears only in the heat \(Q\) at constant pressure.

19.4 Adiabatic processes

Derivation. With \(dQ = 0\): \(nC_VdT = -p\,dV\). Differentiating \(pV = nRT\): \(p\,dV + V\,dp = nR\,dT = -\dfrac{R}{C_V}p\,dV\). Collect terms using \(1 + R/C_V = \gamma\): \(\dfrac{dp}{p} + \gamma\dfrac{dV}{V} = 0\). Integrating:

Reversible adiabatic process (ideal gas)

\[ pV^\gamma = \text{const}, \qquad TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}, \qquad W = \frac{p_iV_i - p_fV_f}{\gamma - 1} = nC_V(T_i - T_f). \]

On a \(p\)–\(V\) diagram adiabats are steeper than isotherms. Fast compressions (engines, sound waves, pumping a tyre) are approximately adiabatic.

Example 19.3

A diesel engine compresses air (\(\gamma = 1.40\)) adiabatically from 300 K and 1.0 atm by a volume ratio of 18. Find the final temperature and pressure.

Show solution \[ T_f = T_i\left(\frac{V_i}{V_f}\right)^{\gamma - 1} = 300(18)^{0.40} = 300(3.18) = 953\ \text{K}, \qquad p_f = p_i(18)^{1.40} = 57\ \text{atm}. \]

953 K (680 °C) exceeds diesel fuel's autoignition temperature, so no spark plug is needed. Check: \(p_fV_f/T_f = (57.2)(1/18)/953\) equals \(p_iV_i/T_i = 1/300\) to rounding, consistent with the ideal gas law.

Example 19.4

3.0 mol of a diatomic gas is heated by 50 K, once at constant volume and once at constant pressure. Find the heat required in each case.

Show solution

\(Q_V = n\tfrac52R\Delta T = 3(20.79)(50) = 3.12\ \text{kJ}\); \(Q_p = n\tfrac72R\Delta T = 3(29.10)(50) = 4.36\ \text{kJ}\). The difference, \(nR\Delta T = 1.25\ \text{kJ}\), is the expansion work.

Practice problems

  2. At what temperature is the rms speed of helium double its value at 300 K?
    Answer

    \(v_{\text{rms}} \propto \sqrt T\): \(T = 4(300) = 1200\ \text{K}\).

    Answer

    \(\lambda = 1/(\sqrt2\pi(1.37\times10^{-19})(2.69\times10^{25})) = 6.1\times10^{-8}\ \text{m}\), about 200 molecular diameters.

  4. A monatomic ideal gas at 400 K expands adiabatically to twice its volume. Find the final temperature.
    Answer

    \(T_f = 400(2)^{-2/3} = 400(0.630) = 252\ \text{K}\).

  5. How much work does 1.0 mol of that gas do in the expansion of problem 4?

    \(W = nC_V(T_i - T_f) = 1.5(8.314)(148) = 1.85\ \text{kJ}\).

20. Entropy & the second law of thermodynamics

Halliday Ch. 20 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 4

Energy conservation allows a cold coffee to spontaneously heat up by cooling the room; it never happens. The first law cannot explain why processes run one way, so we need a second law and a new state function, entropy, that measures how spread out energy is among the available microscopic arrangements. Isolated systems evolve toward higher entropy. The practical consequence is a hard ceiling on engine efficiency: no heat engine can convert heat entirely into work, and the best possible (Carnot) efficiency depends only on the reservoir temperatures.

20.1 Entropy

Definition (Entropy change)

\[ \Delta S = S_f - S_i = \int_i^f\frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T}. \]

Entropy is a state function. To find \(\Delta S\) for an irreversible process, invent any convenient reversible path between the same end states and integrate along it.

Useful entropy changes

\[ \Delta S_{\text{ideal gas}} = nR\ln\frac{V_f}{V_i} + nC_V\ln\frac{T_f}{T_i}, \qquad \Delta S = mc\ln\frac{T_f}{T_i}\ \ (\text{solid/liquid}), \qquad \Delta S = \frac{Q}{T}\ \ (\text{reservoir or phase change}). \]

The ideal-gas result follows from \(dQ = nC_VdT + p\,dV\) divided by \(T\), with \(p/T = nR/V\).

Second law of thermodynamics (entropy form)

For any process in a closed (isolated) system, \(\Delta S \ge 0\): equality for reversible processes, strict inequality for irreversible ones. Entropy of a part may decrease, but only if the rest of the system gains at least as much.

Statistical view. Boltzmann's \(S = k\ln W\), where \(W\) is the number of microstates consistent with the macrostate. A free expansion into double the volume multiplies \(W\) by \(2^N\), so \(\Delta S = Nk\ln2 = nR\ln2\), matching the thermodynamic result. Irreversibility is overwhelming probability.

Example 20.1

1.0 mol of ideal gas undergoes a free expansion into vacuum, doubling its volume in an insulated container. Find \(\Delta S\).

Show solution

The actual process has \(Q = 0\), but it is irreversible, so \(\Delta S \ne \int dQ/T\) along it. Since \(\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0\), \(T\) is unchanged: use a reversible isothermal expansion between the same states.

\[ \Delta S = nR\ln2 = 8.314(0.693) = 5.76\ \text{J/K} \gt 0. \]

Example 20.2

1.0 kg of water at 80 °C is mixed with 1.0 kg at 20 °C in an insulated vessel. Find the final temperature and the total entropy change.

Show solution

Equal masses: \(T_f = 50\ ^\circ\text{C} = 323.15\ \text{K}\).

\[ \Delta S = mc\left[\ln\frac{323.15}{353.15} + \ln\frac{323.15}{293.15}\right] = 4186(-0.0888 + 0.0974) = +36\ \text{J/K}. \]

The hot water loses entropy, the cold water gains more because the same heat enters at a lower temperature. The net increase signals irreversibility.

20.2 Heat engines

Definition (Heat engine & efficiency)

A heat engine runs in a cycle, absorbing \(|Q_H|\) from a hot reservoir, doing work \(|W|\), and rejecting \(|Q_L|\) to a cold reservoir. Over a cycle \(\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0\), so \(|W| = |Q_H| - |Q_L|\) and

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{|W|}{|Q_H|} = 1 - \frac{|Q_L|}{|Q_H|}. \]

Second law (engine form)

No cyclic engine can convert heat from a single reservoir entirely into work (\(\varepsilon = 1\) is impossible). Equivalent statement: no refrigerator can move heat from cold to hot with no work input. Both are equivalent to \(\Delta S \ge 0\).

20.3 The Carnot cycle

The Carnot cycle is two reversible isotherms joined by two reversible adiabats: isothermal expansion at \(T_H\) (a \(\to\) b), adiabatic expansion to \(T_L\) (b \(\to\) c), isothermal compression at \(T_L\) (c \(\to\) d), adiabatic compression back to \(T_H\) (d \(\to\) a).

Derivation of the efficiency (ideal-gas working substance). Isotherms: \(|Q_H| = nRT_H\ln(V_b/V_a)\), \(|Q_L| = nRT_L\ln(V_c/V_d)\). Adiabats: \(T_HV_b^{\gamma - 1} = T_LV_c^{\gamma - 1}\) and \(T_HV_a^{\gamma - 1} = T_LV_d^{\gamma - 1}\); dividing gives \(V_b/V_a = V_c/V_d\). Hence \(|Q_L|/|Q_H| = T_L/T_H\). (Equivalently, total entropy change per cycle is zero: \(|Q_H|/T_H = |Q_L|/T_L\).)

Carnot efficiency

\[ \varepsilon_C = 1 - \frac{T_L}{T_H}. \]

No engine operating between the same two reservoirs can be more efficient, and all reversible engines between them have exactly this efficiency, whatever the working substance. Temperatures in kelvin.

Real cycles fall short: an ideal Otto (gasoline) cycle with compression ratio \(r\) has \(\varepsilon = 1 - r^{1 - \gamma}\), e.g. \(1 - 8^{-0.4} = 56\%\) for \(r = 8\), and friction, heat leaks and finite-rate heat transfer reduce real engines to roughly half that.

Example 20.3

A power plant operates between 800 K steam and 300 K cooling water. (a) Find the Carnot limit. (b) The actual plant delivers 400 MW at 30% efficiency. Find the rates of heat input and rejection.

Show solution

(a) \(\varepsilon_C = 1 - 300/800 = 62.5\%\).

(b) \(P_H = 400/0.30 = 1333\ \text{MW}\); rejected \(P_L = 1333 - 400 = 933\ \text{MW}\), more than twice the electrical output, which is why plants need rivers or cooling towers.

20.4 Refrigerators & heat pumps

Coefficients of performance

\[ K_{\text{ref}} = \frac{|Q_L|}{|W|} \le \frac{T_L}{T_H - T_L}, \qquad K_{\text{hp}} = \frac{|Q_H|}{|W|} \le \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_L}. \]

The closer the temperatures, the better the performance. COP can (and usually does) exceed 1; it is not an efficiency.

Example 20.4

A freezer holds \(-18\) °C in a 25 °C kitchen. (a) Find its Carnot COP. (b) A real unit with COP 2.5 removes 300 W of heat from the freezer. What electrical power does it draw, and how much heat goes into the kitchen?

Show solution

(a) \(K_C = 255.15/(298.15 - 255.15) = 255.15/43 = 5.9\).

(b) \(P_W = 300/2.5 = 120\ \text{W}\); heat to the kitchen \(= 300 + 120 = 420\ \text{W}\). An open refrigerator door warms the room.

Study tip

For any claimed device, run two checks: energy (\(Q_H = W + Q_L\)) and entropy (\(Q_L/T_L \ge Q_H/T_H\) for an engine). A proposal that passes the first but fails the second is a perpetual-motion machine of the second kind.

Common mistake

Using Celsius temperatures in \(1 - T_L/T_H\) or in \(\Delta S = Q/T\). Also, computing \(\Delta S\) for an irreversible process as \(\int dQ/T\) along the actual path: use a reversible substitute path.

Practice problems

  1. Find the entropy change when 0.50 kg of ice melts at 0 °C.
    Answer

    \(\Delta S = mL_F/T = 0.5(333\,000)/273.15 = 610\ \text{J/K}\).

  2. A Carnot engine absorbs 2000 J per cycle at 500 K and rejects heat at 350 K. Find the work per cycle and heat rejected.
    Answer

    \(\varepsilon = 0.30\): \(W = 600\ \text{J}\), \(|Q_L| = 1400\ \text{J}\).

  3. An inventor claims an engine that takes 1000 J from a 500 K reservoir, rejects 550 J at 300 K, and does 450 J of work. Possible?
    Answer

    Energy balances, but \(\varepsilon = 45\% \gt \varepsilon_C = 40\%\). Entropy check: \(-1000/500 + 550/300 = -0.17\ \text{J/K} \lt 0\). Impossible.

  4. Find the maximum COP of a heat pump keeping a house at 20 °C when the outdoor air is at 5 °C, and when it is at \(-5\) °C.
    Answer

    \(293/15 = 19.5\) and \(293/25 = 11.7\). Real heat pumps reach perhaps a third of these.

  5. 2.0 mol of ideal gas expands isothermally to three times its volume. Find \(\Delta S\) of the gas.
    Answer

    \(\Delta S = nR\ln3 = 2(8.314)(1.099) = 18.3\ \text{J/K}\).

  6. 1000 J of heat leaks from a 400 K reservoir to a 300 K reservoir. Find the total entropy change.
    Answer

    \(\Delta S = -1000/400 + 1000/300 = -2.50 + 3.33 = +0.83\ \text{J/K}\).
