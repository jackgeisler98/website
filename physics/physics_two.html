Physics II: Electricity, Magnetism & Optics

The second semester of calculus-based physics: how charges create electric and magnetic fields, how those fields push on charges and currents, how changing fields generate each other, and how that mutual generation becomes light. The course ends with optics, first treating light as rays (mirrors, lenses, instruments) and then as a wave (interference and diffraction). Everything here is a consequence of four equations, Maxwell's equations, plus the Lorentz force law.

1. Coulomb's law

Halliday Ch. 21 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 5

Matter is held together by electric forces. Atoms are positively charged nuclei surrounded by negatively charged electrons, and in ordinary objects the two cancel almost perfectly. Move a tiny fraction of the electrons from one object to another and the imbalance produces forces that dwarf gravity: the electric attraction between a proton and an electron is about \(10^{39}\) times stronger than their gravitational attraction. Coulomb's law gives that force for two point charges; the principle of superposition then lets us build up the force from any arrangement of charges. Everything in electrostatics (fields, Gauss' law, potential, capacitance) is a reorganisation of these two ideas.

1.1 Electric charge

Definition (Electric charge)

Charge is an intrinsic property of particles that determines how strongly they take part in electromagnetic interactions. It comes in two kinds, labelled positive and negative by convention (Franklin's choice made the electron negative). Like charges repel, unlike charges attract. The SI unit is the coulomb (C), defined so that the elementary charge is exactly \(e = 1.602176634\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}\).

  • Quantization. Any free charge is an integer multiple of \(e\): \(q = ne\), \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\). (Quarks carry \(\pm e/3\) and \(\pm 2e/3\) but are never observed in isolation.) Macroscopic charges involve so many electrons that charge behaves as a continuous quantity.
  • Conservation. The net charge of an isolated system never changes. Charge can be moved around or created in equal and opposite amounts (pair production), but never created or destroyed on net.
  • Conductors vs insulators. In a conductor (metals, ionic solutions) some charges, usually the outer electrons, are free to move through the material. In an insulator (glass, plastic, dry air) charges are bound to their atoms. Semiconductors sit in between, with a carrier density that can be tuned by doping and temperature.
  • Induction. Bring a charged rod near a neutral conductor and its free electrons redistribute: the near side acquires charge opposite to the rod, the far side the same sign. The conductor is still neutral but is now attracted (the opposite charge is closer). Connect the far side to ground, break the connection, then remove the rod, and the conductor is left with a net charge opposite to the rod's.

Study tip

A charged object attracts a neutral object (a balloon on a wall, a comb picking up paper) because it polarizes it: induced opposite charge ends up slightly closer than induced like charge, and the force falls off with distance. So "attraction" alone does not prove the second object is charged; only repulsion does.

1.2 Coulomb's law

Law (Coulomb)

The force exerted by point charge \(q_1\) on point charge \(q_2\), separated by distance \(r\), is

\[ \vec{F}_{12} = k\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}\,\hat{r}_{12}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.988\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2, \]

where \(\hat{r}_{12}\) is the unit vector pointing from \(q_1\) toward \(q_2\) and \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{C}^2/(\text{N·m}^2)\) is the permittivity of free space. A positive product \(q_1q_2\) gives repulsion; a negative product gives attraction.

Validity: point charges (or spherically symmetric distributions, measured from their centres), at rest or moving slowly (electrostatics), in vacuum. In a uniform linear dielectric the force is reduced by the dielectric constant \(\kappa\). The forces obey Newton's third law: \(\vec{F}_{21} = -\vec{F}_{12}\).

The inverse-square form is the same as Newton's gravity, and for the same geometric reason: the influence spreads out over a sphere of area \(4\pi r^2\). That is why \(k\) is written as \(1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)\): the \(4\pi\) then disappears from Gauss' law (Chapter 3), which is the more fundamental statement.

How strong is it? For a proton and electron at any separation,

\[ \frac{F_e}{F_g} = \frac{k e^2}{G m_e m_p} = \frac{(8.988\times10^9)(1.602\times10^{-19})^2}{(6.674\times10^{-11})(9.109\times10^{-31})(1.673\times10^{-27})} \approx 2.3\times10^{39}. \]

Gravity dominates astronomy only because large bodies are almost exactly neutral.

Common mistake

Mixing signs into the magnitude formula. The reliable method: compute the magnitude \(F = k|q_1||q_2|/r^2\) with absolute values, then decide the direction by inspection (like signs repel, unlike attract), then resolve into components. Plugging signed charges into a scalar formula and then also "adding a direction" is the classic double-counting error.

1.3 Superposition & the shell theorems

Principle (Superposition)

The net force on a charge \(q_0\) due to charges \(q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n\) is the vector sum of the individual Coulomb forces, each computed as if the others were absent:

\[ \vec{F}_{0,\text{net}} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} k\,\frac{q_j q_0}{r_{j0}^2}\,\hat{r}_{j0}. \]

This is an experimental fact: Maxwell's equations are linear in the sources (in vacuum and in linear media), so fields and forces simply add.

Shell theorems

(1) A uniformly charged spherical shell attracts or repels a charge outside it as if all the shell's charge were concentrated at its centre. (2) A uniformly charged spherical shell exerts no net electrostatic force on a charge located anywhere inside it.

Consequence for conductors: excess charge placed on a conductor moves to its outer surface, because charges repel and the equilibrium configuration has zero field inside the metal (proved properly with Gauss' law in Chapter 3).

Example 1.1 — Two point charges

A charge \(q_1 = +3.0\ \mu\text{C}\) sits at the origin and \(q_2 = -5.0\ \mu\text{C}\) sits on the \(x\)-axis at \(x = 0.20\ \text{m}\). Find the force on \(q_2\).

Show solution

Knowns: \(|q_1| = 3.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{C}\), \(|q_2| = 5.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{C}\), \(r = 0.20\ \text{m}\).

Magnitude:

\[ F = \frac{k|q_1||q_2|}{r^2} = \frac{(8.988\times10^9\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2)(3.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{C})(5.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{C})}{(0.20\ \text{m})^2} = 3.37\ \text{N}. \]

Direction: unlike charges attract, so the force on \(q_2\) points toward \(q_1\), i.e. in the \(-x\) direction: \(\vec{F} = -3.4\ \hat{\imath}\ \text{N}\).

Check: units \(\text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2 \times \text{C}^2/\text{m}^2 = \text{N}\). A few newtons from microcoulombs at tens of centimetres is typical: a microcoulomb is already a large static charge.

Example 1.2 — Where is the force zero?

Charges \(q_1 = +4.0\ \text{nC}\) at \(x = 0\) and \(q_2 = +1.0\ \text{nC}\) at \(x = 0.30\ \text{m}\). Where on the \(x\)-axis can a third charge \(q_3\) be placed so the net force on it is zero?

Show solution

Reasoning about regions. Outside the pair (either side), both forces on \(q_3\) point the same way, so they cannot cancel. Between the charges, the two (like-sign) charges push \(q_3\) in opposite directions, so a balance point exists there, closer to the weaker charge.

Let \(q_3\) be at \(x\) with \(0 \lt x \lt 0.30\ \text{m}\). Setting magnitudes equal (\(k\) and \(q_3\) cancel):

\[ \frac{q_1}{x^2} = \frac{q_2}{(L-x)^2} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{L-x}{x} = \sqrt{\frac{q_2}{q_1}} = \frac{1}{2} \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac{2L}{3} = 0.20\ \text{m}. \]

The answer does not depend on the sign or size of \(q_3\). Check: the point is closer to the smaller charge, as it must be. (The equilibrium is stable for motion along the axis only if \(q_3\) is positive, but it is unstable for sideways displacements; Earnshaw's theorem says no static arrangement of charges gives fully stable equilibrium.)

Example 1.3 — Vector superposition in a triangle

Two charges of \(+2.0\ \mu\text{C}\) occupy the base corners of an equilateral triangle of side \(a = 0.10\ \text{m}\). A charge \(q_0 = +1.0\ \mu\text{C}\) sits at the top vertex. Find the net force on \(q_0\).

Show solution

Each base charge pushes \(q_0\) directly away from itself with magnitude

\[ F_1 = \frac{k q q_0}{a^2} = \frac{(8.988\times10^9)(2.0\times10^{-6})(1.0\times10^{-6})}{(0.10)^2}\ \text{N} = 1.80\ \text{N}. \]

Each force makes \(30^\circ\) with the vertical (the triangle's axis of symmetry). The horizontal components cancel by symmetry; the vertical components add:

\[ F_{\text{net}} = 2F_1\cos 30^\circ = 2(1.80\ \text{N})(0.866) = 3.11\ \text{N}, \]

directed straight up, away from the base. Check: the result lies between \(F_1\) (one charge) and \(2F_1\) (two collinear charges), as expected for two forces at \(60^\circ\) to each other.

Practice problems

  1. How many electrons must be removed from a neutral metal sphere to give it a charge of \(+1.0\ \mu\text{C}\)?
    Answer

    \(n = q/e = (1.0\times10^{-6})/(1.602\times10^{-19}) = 6.2\times10^{12}\) electrons, a tiny fraction of the roughly \(10^{24}\) electrons in a gram-scale piece of metal.

  2. Two identical small conducting spheres carry \(+6.0\ \text{nC}\) and \(-2.0\ \text{nC}\). They are touched together and then separated to \(5.0\ \text{cm}\). Find the force between them.
    Answer

    Total charge \(+4.0\ \text{nC}\) is shared equally (identical spheres): \(+2.0\ \text{nC}\) each. \(F = k(2.0\times10^{-9})^2/(0.050)^2 = 1.4\times10^{-5}\ \text{N}\), repulsive.

  3. Two point charges are moved so that their separation is one third of its original value. By what factor does the force change?
    Answer

    \(F \propto 1/r^2\), so \(F' = F/(1/3)^2 = 9F\).

  4. Charge \(+q\) is at \(x = 0\) and \(-4q\) is at \(x = L\). Where on the axis can a third charge sit in equilibrium?
    Answer

    Between unlike charges the forces point the same way, so look outside, on the side of the smaller-magnitude charge: \(x = -d\). Then \(q/d^2 = 4q/(d+L)^2 \Rightarrow d + L = 2d \Rightarrow d = L\). Position \(x = -L\).

  5. Four equal charges \(q\) sit at the corners of a square of side \(a\). Find the magnitude of the net force on any one of them.
    Answer

    Two neighbours at distance \(a\), perpendicular, give a resultant \(\sqrt{2}\,kq^2/a^2\) along the outward diagonal. The opposite corner at \(\sqrt{2}a\) gives \(kq^2/(2a^2)\) along the same diagonal. Total \(F = \dfrac{kq^2}{a^2}\left(\sqrt{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\right) \approx 1.91\,kq^2/a^2\), pointing diagonally outward.

2. Electric fields

Halliday Ch. 22 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 5

Coulomb's law looks like action at a distance: charge A somehow "knows" where charge B is. The field picture removes that puzzle. Every charge fills the space around it with an electric field \(\vec{E}\), and another charge responds only to the field at its own location. At first this seems like bookkeeping, but the field turns out to be physically real: it carries energy and momentum, and when charges accelerate, changes in the field propagate outward at the speed of light. This chapter computes fields from point charges, dipoles and continuous distributions, and then asks what fields do to charges and dipoles placed in them.

2.1 The electric field

Definition (Electric field)

The electric field at a point is the force per unit charge on a small positive test charge \(q_0\) placed there:

\[ \vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0} \qquad (\text{units N/C} = \text{V/m}). \]

The test charge must be small enough not to disturb the charges producing the field. Once \(\vec{E}\) is known, the force on any charge \(q\) at that point is \(\vec{F} = q\vec{E}\); for negative \(q\) the force is opposite to \(\vec{E}\).

Field of point charges

\[ \vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat{r} \qquad\qquad \vec{E}_{\text{net}} = \sum_j \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_j}{r_j^2}\,\hat{r}_j \]

\(\hat{r}\) points from the source charge to the field point. The field points away from positive charges and toward negative ones.

2.2 Field lines & the electric dipole

Field lines are a visual tool: the field vector is tangent to the line at each point, and the density of lines (lines per unit area perpendicular to them) is proportional to \(|\vec{E}|\). Lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges (or at infinity), and they never cross, since the field has one direction at each point.

Definition (Electric dipole)

Two charges \(+q\) and \(-q\) separated by distance \(d\). Its dipole moment is \(\vec{p} = q\vec{d}\), pointing from the negative to the positive charge, with magnitude \(p = qd\) (units C·m).

Derivation: field on the dipole axis. Put the dipole along \(z\), centred at the origin, with \(+q\) at \(z = d/2\). At a point on the axis at distance \(z \gt d/2\):

\[ E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{(z-d/2)^2} - \frac{1}{(z+d/2)^2}\right] = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^2}\left[\left(1-\frac{d}{2z}\right)^{-2} - \left(1+\frac{d}{2z}\right)^{-2}\right]. \]

For \(z \gg d\), expand with \((1\pm x)^{-2} \approx 1 \mp 2x\): the bracket becomes \(2d/z\), so

\[ E \approx \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qd}{z^3} = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{z^3}. \]

On the perpendicular bisector the field is half as large and points opposite to \(\vec{p}\): \(E \approx p/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3)\). The \(1/r^3\) falloff (faster than a point charge) is the signature of a neutral object: from far away the two charges nearly cancel, and only their separation \(d\) (through \(p\)) survives.

2.3 Continuous charge distributions

For macroscopic charged objects, replace the sum by an integral. Chop the object into elements of charge \(dq\), each producing a point-charge field \(d\vec{E}\), and integrate. Write \(dq\) in terms of the geometry:

\[ dq = \lambda\,ds \ (\text{line, C/m}), \qquad dq = \sigma\,dA \ (\text{surface, C/m}^2), \qquad dq = \rho\,dV \ (\text{volume, C/m}^3). \]

Study tip (the integration recipe)

(1) Draw a generic element \(dq\) and the vector from it to the field point. (2) Write \(dE = k\,dq/r^2\). (3) Before integrating, use symmetry to identify which components cancel; integrate only the surviving component, which brings in a factor like \(\cos\theta = z/r\). (4) Express everything in one integration variable. (5) Check limits: far away the result should look like \(kQ/r^2\).

Ring of charge (on axis). A ring of radius \(R\) carries total charge \(Q\) uniformly. At height \(z\) on its axis, every element is at the same distance \(r = \sqrt{z^2+R^2}\). Components perpendicular to the axis cancel in opposite pairs; the axial component of each is \(dE\cos\theta = dE\,(z/r)\):

\[ E_z = \int \frac{k\,dq}{r^2}\frac{z}{r} = \frac{kz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}\int dq = \frac{kQz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}. \]

Checks: \(E = 0\) at the centre (symmetry), and \(E \to kQ/z^2\) for \(z \gg R\).

Uniform disk (on axis). Build a disk of radius \(R\) and surface density \(\sigma\) from rings of radius \(s\) and width \(ds\), each with \(dq = \sigma\,2\pi s\,ds\). Using the ring result:

\[ E_z = \int_0^R \frac{k z\,\sigma 2\pi s\,ds}{(z^2+s^2)^{3/2}} = \pi k\sigma z\left[\frac{-2}{\sqrt{z^2+s^2}}\right]_0^R = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\right). \]

Letting \(R\to\infty\) gives the infinite sheet: \(E = \sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)\), uniform and independent of distance. That is the basis of the uniform field between capacitor plates.

Finite line (perpendicular bisector). A rod of length \(L\) along \(x\) from \(-L/2\) to \(L/2\) with density \(\lambda\); field point at distance \(y\) on the bisector. The \(x\)-components cancel, and

\[ E_y = \int_{-L/2}^{L/2} \frac{k\lambda\,dx}{x^2+y^2}\frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} = k\lambda y\left[\frac{x}{y^2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\right]_{-L/2}^{L/2} = \frac{k\lambda L}{y\sqrt{y^2+L^2/4}}. \]

As \(L\to\infty\), \(E \to 2k\lambda/y = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 y)\): the infinite line field falls off as \(1/r\).

Common mistake

Integrating the magnitude \(dE\) directly. Field contributions are vectors; integrating \(\int dE\) for the ring would give \(kQ/r^2\), which is wrong everywhere except far away. Always project onto a fixed axis first (\(dE_z = dE\cos\theta\)), and remember that \(\cos\theta\) generally varies with the integration variable.

2.4 A point charge in an electric field

A charge \(q\) of mass \(m\) in a field \(\vec{E}\) accelerates at \(\vec{a} = q\vec{E}/m\). In a uniform field this is exactly projectile motion with \(g\) replaced by \(qE/m\), which is the operating principle of the cathode-ray tube, the ink-jet printer and the electrostatic deflectors in mass spectrometers. For electrons, \(e/m_e \approx 1.76\times10^{11}\ \text{C/kg}\), so even modest fields produce enormous accelerations and gravity is completely negligible.

Millikan's oil-drop experiment balanced \(qE = mg\) for tiny charged droplets and found every measured \(q\) to be an integer multiple of \(e\): the first direct demonstration of charge quantization.

2.5 A dipole in an electric field

In a uniform field the forces \(+q\vec{E}\) and \(-q\vec{E}\) on a dipole cancel, but they form a couple that rotates the dipole toward alignment with \(\vec{E}\).

Torque and energy of a dipole

\[ \vec{\tau} = \vec{p}\times\vec{E}, \qquad U(\theta) = -\vec{p}\cdot\vec{E} = -pE\cos\theta. \]

\(U\) is minimum (\(-pE\), stable) when \(\vec{p}\parallel\vec{E}\) and maximum (\(+pE\), unstable) when antiparallel. The work an external agent must do to rotate the dipole from \(\theta_i\) to \(\theta_f\) is \(U(\theta_f)-U(\theta_i)\). In a nonuniform field there is also a net force, \(\vec{F} = \nabla(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{E})\), pulling the aligned dipole toward stronger field.

The zero of \(U\) at \(\theta = 90^\circ\) is a convention. Microwave ovens exploit dipole torque: the oscillating field (2.45 GHz) keeps twisting water molecules, and the molecular friction heats the food.

Example 2.1 — Field between two charges

\(q_1 = +5.0\ \text{nC}\) is at the origin and \(q_2 = -3.0\ \text{nC}\) is at \(x = 0.10\ \text{m}\). Find \(\vec{E}\) at the midpoint.

Show solution

Both charges are \(r = 0.050\ \text{m}\) from the midpoint. The field of \(q_1\) points away from it (\(+x\)); the field of \(q_2\) points toward it (also \(+x\)). So the magnitudes add:

\[ E = \frac{k(|q_1| + |q_2|)}{r^2} = \frac{(8.988\times10^9)(8.0\times10^{-9})}{(0.050)^2}\ \text{N/C} = 2.9\times10^{4}\ \text{N/C} \]

in the \(+x\) direction. Check: a proton released there would accelerate toward the negative charge, consistent with \(+x\).

Example 2.2 — Ring field and its maximum

A ring of radius \(R = 0.10\ \text{m}\) carries \(Q = +20\ \text{nC}\). (a) Find the field on the axis at \(z = 0.10\ \text{m}\). (b) At what \(z\) is the axial field strongest?

Show solution

(a) With \(z = R\): \(z^2 + R^2 = 0.020\ \text{m}^2\) and \((0.020)^{3/2} = 2.83\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3\).

\[ E = \frac{kQz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{(8.988\times10^9)(20\times10^{-9})(0.10)}{2.83\times10^{-3}}\ \text{N/C} = 6.4\times10^{3}\ \text{N/C}, \]

directed along the axis away from the ring.

(b) Set \(dE/dz = 0\):

\[ \frac{d}{dz}\frac{z}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{(z^2+R^2) - 3z^2}{(z^2+R^2)^{5/2}} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; z = \frac{R}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.071\ \text{m}. \]

Check: the field is zero at the centre and tends to zero far away, so a maximum in between is required.

Example 2.3 — Electron deflection

An electron enters the region between two deflection plates moving horizontally at \(v_0 = 3.0\times10^6\ \text{m/s}\). The plates are \(4.0\ \text{cm}\) long and produce a uniform downward field \(E = 500\ \text{N/C}\). How far is the electron deflected vertically by the time it leaves the plates?

Show solution

The force on the electron is \(-e\vec{E}\), i.e. upward. Acceleration magnitude:

\[ a = \frac{eE}{m_e} = \frac{(1.602\times10^{-19})(500)}{9.109\times10^{-31}}\ \text{m/s}^2 = 8.79\times10^{13}\ \text{m/s}^2. \]

Horizontal motion is uniform: \(t = L/v_0 = 0.040/(3.0\times10^6) = 1.33\times10^{-8}\ \text{s}\). Vertical deflection from rest:

\[ y = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = \frac{eEL^2}{2m_e v_0^2} = \tfrac12(8.79\times10^{13})(1.33\times10^{-8})^2\ \text{m} = 7.8\ \text{mm (upward)}. \]

Check: \(g/a \sim 10^{-13}\), so ignoring gravity is fine. The symbolic form shows \(y \propto E\) (linear deflection, which is why CRT oscilloscopes worked) and \(y \propto 1/v_0^2\).

Example 2.4 — Disk vs infinite sheet

A disk of radius \(0.20\ \text{m}\) carries uniform \(\sigma = 5.0\ \mu\text{C/m}^2\). Find the field on the axis \(5.0\ \text{cm}\) from its centre, and compare with the infinite-sheet approximation.

Show solution \[ \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{5.0\times10^{-6}}{2(8.854\times10^{-12})}\ \text{N/C} = 2.82\times10^5\ \text{N/C}, \qquad \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}} = \frac{0.05}{\sqrt{0.0025+0.040}} = 0.243. \] \[ E = (2.82\times10^5)(1 - 0.243)\ \text{N/C} = 2.1\times10^5\ \text{N/C}. \]

The infinite-sheet value \(2.8\times10^5\ \text{N/C}\) is 32% too high here because \(z/R = 0.25\) is not very small. The sheet approximation becomes good only for \(z \ll R\), the regime of a real parallel-plate capacitor whose gap is tiny compared with its plates.

Practice problems

  1. A charge \(+2.0\ \mu\text{C}\) is at \(x = 0\) and \(-8.0\ \mu\text{C}\) is at \(x = 1.0\ \text{m}\). Where on the \(x\)-axis is \(\vec{E} = 0\)?
    Answer

    Outside the pair on the side of the smaller charge, \(x = -d\): \(2/d^2 = 8/(d+1)^2 \Rightarrow d+1 = 2d \Rightarrow d = 1.0\ \text{m}\). So \(x = -1.0\ \text{m}\).

  2. A proton starts from rest in a uniform field of \(3.0\times10^4\ \text{N/C}\). What is its speed after it has moved \(2.0\ \text{cm}\)?
    Answer

    Work-energy: \(\tfrac12 mv^2 = eEd = (1.602\times10^{-19})(3.0\times10^4)(0.020) = 9.6\times10^{-17}\ \text{J}\). \(v = \sqrt{2(9.6\times10^{-17})/1.673\times10^{-27}} = 3.4\times10^5\ \text{m/s}\).

  3. A thin rod bent into a semicircle of radius \(R\) carries charge \(Q\) uniformly. Find the field at the centre of the semicircle.
    Answer

    \(\lambda = Q/(\pi R)\), \(dq = \lambda R\,d\theta\). Only the component along the symmetry axis survives: \(E = \int_0^\pi \frac{k\lambda R\,d\theta}{R^2}\sin\theta = \frac{2k\lambda}{R} = \frac{2kQ}{\pi R^2}\), pointing away from the arc's midpoint (for \(Q \gt 0\)).

  4. A dipole with \(p = 3.0\times10^{-29}\ \text{C·m}\) makes \(30^\circ\) with a uniform field of \(4.0\times10^5\ \text{N/C}\). Find the torque and the potential energy.
    Answer

    \(\tau = pE\sin30^\circ = 6.0\times10^{-24}\ \text{N·m}\); \(U = -pE\cos30^\circ = -1.0\times10^{-23}\ \text{J}\).

  5. A long straight wire carries \(\lambda = 4.0\ \text{nC/m}\). Find the field \(25\ \text{cm}\) from it.
    Answer

    \(E = 2k\lambda/r = 2(8.988\times10^9)(4.0\times10^{-9})/0.25 = 2.9\times10^2\ \text{N/C}\), radially outward.

  6. Using the disk result, show that very close to a charged disk (\(z \ll R\)) the field is \(\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)\), and very far away (\(z \gg R\)) it is \(kQ/z^2\) with \(Q = \sigma\pi R^2\).
    Answer

    Near: \(z/\sqrt{z^2+R^2} \to 0\). Far: \(z/\sqrt{z^2+R^2} = (1+R^2/z^2)^{-1/2} \approx 1 - R^2/(2z^2)\), so \(E \approx \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\frac{R^2}{2z^2} = \frac{\sigma\pi R^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^2} = \frac{kQ}{z^2}\).

3. Gauss' law

Halliday Ch. 23 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 6

Coulomb's law plus integration can find any electrostatic field, but the integrals are often painful. Gauss' law trades the integral over sources for an integral over a closed surface: the total field "flowing out" through any closed surface depends only on the charge inside. For general surfaces this is just a true statement; for highly symmetric charge distributions (spheres, infinite cylinders, infinite planes) it becomes a two-line method for finding \(\vec{E}\). Gauss' law is also more fundamental than Coulomb's law: it remains valid for moving charges and is the first of Maxwell's equations.

3.1 Electric flux

Definition (Electric flux)

For a surface \(S\) with area element \(d\vec{A}\) (magnitude \(dA\), direction normal to the surface), the electric flux is

\[ \Phi_E = \int_S \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} \qquad (\text{units N·m}^2/\text{C}). \]

For a closed surface we write \(\oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A}\) (the circle here denotes a closed surface integral) and take \(d\vec{A}\) to point outward. For a flat surface in a uniform field, \(\Phi_E = EA\cos\theta\).

Picture flux as the net number of field lines piercing the surface, counting outward lines as positive and inward as negative. A field line that enters a closed surface and leaves again contributes zero, which is why only enclosed charge matters.

3.2 Gauss' law

Law (Gauss)

For any closed surface (a "Gaussian surface"),

\[ \varepsilon_0 \oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = q_{\text{enc}}, \]

where \(q_{\text{enc}}\) is the net charge enclosed. \(\vec{E}\) is the total field, including contributions from charges outside the surface; those contribute zero net flux but certainly do not contribute zero field. Differential form (via the divergence theorem): \(\nabla\cdot\vec{E} = \rho/\varepsilon_0\).

Why it works. For a point charge at the centre of a sphere of radius \(r\): \(\Phi_E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\cdot4\pi r^2 = q/\varepsilon_0\), independent of \(r\). The \(1/r^2\) of the field exactly cancels the \(r^2\) of the area. Any other closed surface around the charge intercepts the same solid angle and therefore the same flux; a charge outside sends equal flux in and out. Superposition extends this to any distribution. Gauss' law is thus equivalent to the inverse-square law (and would fail if the exponent were not exactly 2).

3.3 Conductors in electrostatic equilibrium

Properties of a conductor in equilibrium

  1. \(\vec{E} = 0\) everywhere inside the conducting material (otherwise free charges would move, contradicting equilibrium).
  2. Any net charge resides on the surface. (Apply Gauss' law to a surface just inside the material: \(\vec{E} = 0\) there, so \(q_{\text{enc}} = 0\).)
  3. Just outside the surface, \(\vec{E}\) is perpendicular to the surface with magnitude \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0\), where \(\sigma\) is the local surface charge density.
  4. If the conductor has an empty cavity, the field in the cavity is zero and no charge sits on the cavity wall. If a charge \(q\) sits in the cavity, charge \(-q\) is induced on the cavity wall.

Derivation of \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0\). Use a small "pillbox" cylinder straddling the surface, with end caps of area \(A\). Inside the metal \(E = 0\); along the sides \(\vec{E}\) is perpendicular to \(d\vec{A}\). Only the outer cap contributes: \(\varepsilon_0 EA = \sigma A\). Compare the isolated sheet, \(\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)\): there the flux leaves through both caps.

Engineering link: shielding. Property 4 is the Faraday cage. A closed metal enclosure shields its interior from external static fields, because charges on the outer surface rearrange to cancel the applied field inside. That is why sensitive circuits sit in metal cans and why coaxial cables have a grounded outer conductor. (Shielding against time-varying fields also works, up to the skin depth of the metal.)

3.4 Using symmetry to find fields

Study tip (Gauss' law recipe)

(1) Identify the symmetry: spherical, cylindrical (infinite line) or planar (infinite sheet). (2) Use it to argue the direction of \(\vec{E}\) and that \(|\vec{E}|\) depends only on distance from the centre/axis/plane. (3) Choose a Gaussian surface on which \(\vec{E}\) is either perpendicular to the surface with constant magnitude, or parallel to it (zero flux). (4) Then \(\oint\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = E\cdot(\text{area})\). (5) Compute \(q_{\text{enc}}\), often by integrating \(\rho\), and solve for \(E\).

Using Gauss' law to "find the field" of an asymmetric object, e.g. a finite rod or a cube of charge. Gauss' law is still true there, but \(E\) is not constant over any convenient surface, so you cannot pull it out of the integral. Without symmetry, Gauss gives you flux, not field.

Example 3.1 — Uniformly charged insulating sphere

A non-conducting sphere of radius \(R = 5.0\ \text{cm}\) carries \(Q = 8.0\ \text{nC}\) spread uniformly through its volume. Derive \(E(r)\) and evaluate it at \(r = 2.0\ \text{cm}\) and \(r = 10\ \text{cm}\).

Show solution

By spherical symmetry \(\vec{E} = E(r)\hat{r}\). Use a concentric Gaussian sphere of radius \(r\): \(\oint\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = E\cdot4\pi r^2\).

Inside (\(r \lt R\)): \(\rho = Q/(\tfrac43\pi R^3)\), so \(q_{\text{enc}} = \rho\cdot\tfrac43\pi r^3 = Q r^3/R^3\).

\[ \varepsilon_0 E\,4\pi r^2 = Q\frac{r^3}{R^3} \;\Rightarrow\; E = \frac{Q r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^3} = \frac{kQr}{R^3}. \]

Outside (\(r \gt R\)): \(q_{\text{enc}} = Q\), so \(E = kQ/r^2\). The two expressions agree at \(r = R\) (continuity).

Numbers (\(kQ = 71.9\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}\)):

\[ E(2.0\ \text{cm}) = \frac{(71.9)(0.020)}{(0.050)^3} = 1.2\times10^4\ \text{N/C}, \qquad E(10\ \text{cm}) = \frac{71.9}{(0.10)^2} = 7.2\times10^3\ \text{N/C}. \]

Check: the field grows linearly inside, peaks at the surface (\(2.9\times10^4\ \text{N/C}\)), then falls as \(1/r^2\).

Example 3.2 — Coaxial cable

A long coaxial cable has an inner conductor carrying \(+\lambda\) per unit length and an outer cylindrical shell carrying \(-\lambda\). Find \(E\) everywhere, and evaluate between the conductors at \(r = 2.0\ \text{mm}\) for \(\lambda = 50\ \text{nC/m}\).

Show solution

Cylindrical symmetry: \(\vec{E} = E(r)\hat{r}\). Use a coaxial Gaussian cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(\ell\). The end caps carry no flux (\(\vec{E}\) radial), so \(\oint\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = E\cdot2\pi r\ell\).

  • Inside the inner conductor: \(E = 0\) (it is a conductor).
  • Between conductors: \(q_{\text{enc}} = \lambda\ell\), so \(E = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\).
  • Outside the shell: \(q_{\text{enc}} = \lambda\ell - \lambda\ell = 0\), so \(E = 0\).
\[ E(2.0\ \text{mm}) = \frac{2k\lambda}{r} = \frac{2(8.988\times10^9)(50\times10^{-9})}{0.0020}\ \text{N/C} = 4.5\times10^5\ \text{N/C}. \]

The field is confined between the conductors: a coax does not radiate or pick up external electrostatic fields, which is the whole point of the design.

Example 3.3 — Charge inside a conducting shell

A point charge \(+3.0\ \mu\text{C}\) sits at the centre of a thick conducting spherical shell whose net charge is \(-5.0\ \mu\text{C}\). Find the charge on the inner and outer surfaces of the shell, and the field outside.

Show solution

A Gaussian sphere inside the metal of the shell has \(E = 0\), so it encloses zero net charge: the inner surface carries \(-3.0\ \mu\text{C}\). Charge conservation for the shell leaves \(-5.0 - (-3.0) = -2.0\ \mu\text{C}\) on the outer surface.

Outside, a Gaussian sphere encloses \(+3.0 - 5.0 = -2.0\ \mu\text{C}\): \(E = k(2.0\ \mu\text{C})/r^2\), pointing radially inward.

Note: if the inner charge were moved off-centre, the inner surface charge would redistribute but the outer surface charge would stay uniform: the outside world cannot tell where inside the cavity the charge is.

Practice problems

  1. A hemisphere of radius \(R\) (open, no flat base) sits in a uniform field \(\vec{E}\) parallel to its symmetry axis. What is the flux through the curved surface?
    Answer

    Close it with the flat disk: total flux is zero (no charge). The disk carries \(\pm E\pi R^2\), so the curved surface carries \(\pi R^2 E\) in magnitude.

  2. A point charge \(q\) sits at one corner of a cube. What is the flux through the cube? Through each face not touching the corner?
    Answer

    Eight such cubes surround the corner symmetrically, so the cube gets \(q/(8\varepsilon_0)\). The three faces touching the corner have \(\vec{E}\) in their plane (zero flux); the other three share equally: \(q/(24\varepsilon_0)\) each.

  3. An infinite slab of thickness \(d\) has uniform charge density \(\rho\). Find \(E\) inside and outside, measuring \(x\) from the mid-plane.
    Answer

    Symmetric pillbox from \(-x\) to \(x\): \(\varepsilon_0 E\cdot2A = \rho\cdot2xA \Rightarrow E = \rho x/\varepsilon_0\) for \(|x| \lt d/2\). Outside: \(E = \rho d/(2\varepsilon_0)\), uniform.

  4. The surface charge density on a conductor is \(2.0\ \mu\text{C/m}^2\). What is the field just outside?
    Answer

    \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0 = 2.0\times10^{-6}/8.854\times10^{-12} = 2.3\times10^5\ \text{N/C}\), perpendicular to the surface.

  5. A sphere of radius \(R\) has nonuniform density \(\rho = \rho_0 r/R\). Find \(E\) inside.
    Answer

    \(q_{\text{enc}} = \int_0^r \rho_0\frac{r'}{R}4\pi r'^2\,dr' = \pi\rho_0 r^4/R\). Then \(\varepsilon_0E\,4\pi r^2 = \pi\rho_0r^4/R \Rightarrow E = \rho_0 r^2/(4\varepsilon_0R)\).

4. Electric potential

Halliday Ch. 24 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 7

The electrostatic force is conservative, just like gravity: the work it does on a charge moving between two points does not depend on the path. That means we can assign a potential energy to every configuration, and dividing by the test charge gives the electric potential \(V\), a scalar field measured in volts. Scalars are far easier to add than vectors, so it is often simplest to compute \(V\) first and then get \(\vec{E}\) by differentiating. For an engineer, potential is the familiar "voltage": what a voltmeter reads is a difference in \(V\) between two points.

4.1 Potential energy & potential

Definition (Electric potential)

If a test charge \(q_0\) has electric potential energy \(U\) at a point, the potential there is \(V = U/q_0\) (units J/C = volt, V). Only differences are physical:

\[ \Delta V = V_f - V_i = \frac{\Delta U}{q_0} = -\frac{W_{\text{field}}}{q_0}. \]

The electron-volt is the energy gained by charge \(e\) falling through \(1\ \text{V}\): \(1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\).

Positive charges accelerate from high to low potential; negative charges (electrons) accelerate from low to high. Energy conservation for a charge moving only under electric forces: \(\Delta K = -q\,\Delta V\).

4.2 Potential from the field; equipotentials

Potential difference as a line integral

\[ V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{s} \]

Path-independent in electrostatics (\(\oint\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{s} = 0\)). In a uniform field along \(x\): \(\Delta V = -E\,\Delta x\), so the field points "downhill" in potential, and \(E = |\Delta V|/d\) (hence the unit V/m).

Equipotential surfaces are surfaces of constant \(V\). No work is done moving a charge along one, so \(\vec{E}\) is everywhere perpendicular to the equipotentials. For a point charge they are concentric spheres; for a uniform field, parallel planes. The surface of a conductor in equilibrium is an equipotential, and so is its entire volume.

4.3 Point charges & dipoles

Derivation. Integrate the point-charge field radially inward from infinity, where we set \(V = 0\):

\[ V(r) - 0 = -\int_\infty^r \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r'^2}\,dr' = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}. \]

Potential of point charges and a dipole

\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r}, \qquad V = \sum_j \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_j}{r_j}, \qquad V_{\text{dipole}} \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2}\ (r \gg d). \]

Signs matter here: \(V\) is a signed scalar sum with no directions to track.

4.4 Continuous distributions

\(V = \displaystyle\int \frac{k\,dq}{r}\), a scalar integral, with no components to project. Two standard results:

  • Ring on axis: every element is at distance \(\sqrt{z^2+R^2}\), so \(V = kQ/\sqrt{z^2+R^2}\).
  • Rod on its axis: rod from \(x = 0\) to \(L\), field point at distance \(d\) beyond the end: \(V = \int_0^L \frac{k\lambda\,dx}{L + d - x} = k\lambda\ln\frac{L+d}{d}\).

Common mistake

Setting \(V = 0\) at infinity for an infinite line or sheet. Their potential diverges at infinity (e.g. \(V = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln r + C\)), so choose the reference at some finite radius instead. And remember: \(V = 0\) at a point does not mean \(\vec{E} = 0\) there, nor the reverse.

4.5 Field from potential

Gradient relation

\[ \vec{E} = -\nabla V \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x},\ E_y = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial y},\ E_z = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}. \]

In spherical symmetry, \(E_r = -dV/dr\). The field points in the direction of steepest decrease of \(V\), perpendicular to equipotentials, with magnitude equal to the rate of decrease.

Study tip

To get an on-axis field from \(V\), you need \(V\) as a function of the coordinate you differentiate with respect to. The on-axis ring potential \(V(z)\) gives \(E_z\) correctly, but it cannot give \(E_x\) because you only know \(V\) along the axis, not off it.

4.6 Potential energy of systems; isolated conductors

Energy of a charge configuration

\[ U = \sum_{\text{pairs } i \lt j} \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_i q_j}{r_{ij}} \]

This is the work required to assemble the charges from infinite separation. A negative total means the system is bound: you would have to do work to pull it apart.

Conductors. Because a conductor is an equipotential, charge on an irregularly shaped conductor concentrates where the surface is most sharply curved. Model two spheres of radii \(R_1, R_2\) joined by a long wire: equal potentials \(kq_1/R_1 = kq_2/R_2\) imply \(\sigma_1/\sigma_2 = R_2/R_1\). The smaller sphere has the larger surface density and hence the larger field \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\). That is why sharp points discharge into the air (corona discharge, lightning rods), and why high-voltage hardware uses smooth, rounded electrodes.

Example 4.1 — Accelerating an electron

An electron starts from rest and is accelerated through a potential difference of \(500\ \text{V}\). Find its final kinetic energy and speed.

Show solution

The electron moves from low to high potential: \(\Delta V = +500\ \text{V}\), \(q = -e\), so \(\Delta U = q\Delta V = -500\ \text{eV}\) and \(K = 500\ \text{eV} = 8.01\times10^{-17}\ \text{J}\).

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{2K}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(8.01\times10^{-17})}{9.109\times10^{-31}}}\ \text{m/s} = 1.33\times10^7\ \text{m/s}. \]

Check: \(v/c \approx 0.044\), so the non-relativistic formula is accurate to better than 0.1%. (Rule of thumb: relativity matters for electrons above roughly 10 keV.)

Example 4.2 — Disk potential, then its field

Find the potential on the axis of a uniformly charged disk (radius \(R\), density \(\sigma\)), and differentiate to recover the field from Chapter 2.

Show solution

Use rings of radius \(s\): \(dq = \sigma\,2\pi s\,ds\), all at distance \(\sqrt{z^2+s^2}\).

\[ V(z) = \int_0^R \frac{k\sigma2\pi s\,ds}{\sqrt{z^2+s^2}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\Big[\sqrt{z^2+s^2}\Big]_0^R = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(\sqrt{z^2+R^2} - z\right)\quad (z \gt 0). \]

Differentiate:

\[ E_z = -\frac{dV}{dz} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\right), \]

exactly the result found by vector integration, with far less work. Check: for \(z\gg R\), \(\sqrt{z^2+R^2} - z \approx R^2/(2z)\), giving \(V \approx kQ/z\).

Example 4.3 — Assembling three charges

Charges \(+1.0\), \(-2.0\) and \(+3.0\ \mu\text{C}\) are placed at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side \(0.10\ \text{m}\). How much work is needed to assemble them from infinity?

Show solution

All three pairs are at the same distance \(a\):

\[ U = \frac{k}{a}(q_1q_2 + q_1q_3 + q_2q_3) = \frac{8.988\times10^9}{0.10}\big[(1)(-2) + (1)(3) + (-2)(3)\big]\times10^{-12}\ \text{J} = -0.45\ \text{J}. \]

The negative result means the assembled system is bound: the external agent does \(-0.45\ \text{J}\) of work (the field does positive work), and \(0.45\ \text{J}\) would be needed to disassemble it.

Example 4.4 — Field from a potential function

In a region, \(V(x,y) = 3x^2y - 5y\) (volts, with \(x, y\) in metres). Find \(\vec{E}\) at \((1, 2)\ \text{m}\).

Show solution \[ E_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = -6xy = -12\ \text{V/m}, \qquad E_y = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial y} = -(3x^2 - 5) = 2\ \text{V/m}. \]

\(\vec{E} = (-12\,\hat{\imath} + 2\,\hat{\jmath})\ \text{V/m}\), magnitude \(12.2\ \text{V/m}\). Check: \(\partial E_x/\partial y = -6x = \partial E_y/\partial x\), so \(\nabla\times\vec{E} = 0\), as any electrostatic field must satisfy.

Practice problems

  1. Find the potential difference \(V(0.10\ \text{m}) - V(0.20\ \text{m})\) near a \(5.0\ \text{nC}\) point charge.
    Answer

    \(kq(1/0.10 - 1/0.20) = (44.9)(5.0)\ \text{V} = 225\ \text{V}\).

  2. Charges \(+q, +q, -q, -q\) sit at the corners of a square. Find \(V\) at the centre. Is \(\vec{E}\) zero there?
    Answer

    \(V = k(q+q-q-q)/(a/\sqrt2) = 0\). But \(\vec{E} \neq 0\) if like charges are adjacent: the fields add toward the negative side. Zero potential does not imply zero field.

  3. Air breaks down at about \(3\times10^6\ \text{V/m}\). What is the maximum potential and charge of an isolated conducting sphere of radius \(15\ \text{cm}\)?
    Answer

    At the surface \(E = kQ/R^2\) and \(V = kQ/R = ER\). \(V_{\max} = (3\times10^6)(0.15) = 4.5\times10^5\ \text{V}\); \(Q_{\max} = ER^2/k = 7.5\ \mu\text{C}\).

  4. An alpha particle (\(q = 2e\)) moves from a point at \(+250\ \text{V}\) to one at \(-150\ \text{V}\). Find its change in kinetic energy.
    Answer

    \(\Delta K = -q\Delta V = -(2e)(-400\ \text{V}) = +800\ \text{eV} = 1.28\times10^{-16}\ \text{J}\).

  5. Two conducting spheres of radii \(1.0\ \text{cm}\) and \(3.0\ \text{cm}\) are joined by a long thin wire and charged. Find the ratio of their surface fields.
    Answer

    Equal \(V\) gives \(q_1/q_2 = R_1/R_2\), and \(E = kq/R^2\), so \(E_1/E_2 = R_2/R_1 = 3\). The small sphere has three times the field.

  6. Using \(V = kQ/\sqrt{z^2+R^2}\) for a ring, derive the on-axis field.
    Answer

    \(E_z = -dV/dz = kQz/(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}\), matching Section 2.3.

5. Capacitance

Halliday Ch. 25 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 8

A capacitor is any pair of conductors separated by an insulator. Charge one conductor \(+Q\) and the other \(-Q\) and a potential difference \(V\) appears between them; for a given geometry the two are proportional, and the constant of proportionality is the capacitance. Physically, a capacitor stores energy in the electric field between its plates. The physics question is how geometry and the insulating material set \(C\); the engineering payoff is every bypass cap, timing circuit, filter, DRAM cell and touchscreen.

5.1 Capacitors

Definition (Capacitance)

\[ C = \frac{Q}{V} \qquad (\text{units: farad, } 1\ \text{F} = 1\ \text{C/V}) \]

\(Q\) is the magnitude of charge on either conductor and \(V\) the magnitude of the potential difference between them. \(C\) depends only on geometry and the insulating material, not on \(Q\) or \(V\) (for linear dielectrics).

5.2 Calculating capacitance

Study tip (the four-step method)

(1) Put \(+Q\) and \(-Q\) on the conductors. (2) Find \(\vec{E}\) between them with Gauss' law. (3) Integrate \(V = \int_-^+ \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{s}\) from the negative to the positive conductor (magnitude). (4) \(C = Q/V\); the \(Q\) must cancel. If it does not, something went wrong.

Parallel plates (area \(A\), gap \(d \ll \sqrt{A}\)): \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0 = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)\) between the plates, so \(V = Ed = Qd/(\varepsilon_0A)\) and

\[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}. \]

Cylindrical (coaxial, radii \(a \lt b\), length \(L\)): Gauss gives \(E = Q/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 L r)\). Then

\[ V = \int_a^b \frac{Q\,dr}{2\pi\varepsilon_0Lr} = \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0L}\ln\frac{b}{a} \;\Rightarrow\; C = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0 L}{\ln(b/a)}. \]

Spherical (concentric shells, radii \(a \lt b\)): \(E = kQ/r^2\), so \(V = kQ\left(\frac1a - \frac1b\right)\) and

\[ C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\frac{ab}{b-a}. \]

Letting \(b\to\infty\) gives an isolated sphere: \(C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a\).

Check all three: capacitance is always \(\varepsilon_0\times(\text{a length})\). Larger plates and smaller gaps raise \(C\). As \(b \to a\), the cylindrical and spherical formulas both reduce to \(\varepsilon_0 A/d\) with \(d = b - a\).

5.3 Series & parallel combinations

Equivalent capacitance

\[ C_{\text{par}} = \sum_j C_j \qquad\qquad \frac{1}{C_{\text{ser}}} = \sum_j \frac{1}{C_j} \]

Parallel: same \(V\) across each, charges add. Series: same \(Q\) on each (charge on the isolated inner plates must sum to zero), voltages add. Note these are the opposite rules from resistors.

5.4 Energy stored & energy density

Derivation. Charging moves charge \(dq\) across a potential difference \(v = q/C\) that grows as charge accumulates. The work for each increment is \(dW = v\,dq = (q/C)\,dq\), so

\[ U = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \tfrac12 QV. \]

The factor \(\tfrac12\) arises because the average voltage during charging is \(V/2\). For parallel plates, divide by the volume \(Ad\) between them:

\[ u = \frac{U}{Ad} = \frac{\tfrac12(\varepsilon_0A/d)(Ed)^2}{Ad} = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2. \]

Energy density of the electric field

\[ u_E = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2 \quad (\text{J/m}^3) \]

Although derived for parallel plates, this holds for any electric field in vacuum: energy is stored in the field itself. In a linear dielectric, \(u_E = \tfrac12\kappa\varepsilon_0E^2\).

5.5 Dielectrics

Definition (Dielectric constant)

Filling the space between the conductors completely with an insulating material multiplies the capacitance by the dielectric constant \(\kappa \geq 1\): \(C = \kappa C_0\). Typical values: vacuum 1 (exactly), air 1.00054, polyethylene 2.3, glass 4–7, water 80, ceramic (barium titanate) hundreds to thousands. Equivalently, replace \(\varepsilon_0\) by \(\varepsilon = \kappa\varepsilon_0\) in every electrostatic formula.

Microscopic picture. The external field polarizes the material, either by stretching nonpolar molecules into induced dipoles or by partially aligning polar molecules. The net effect is a layer of bound charge on each face of the dielectric, opposite in sign to the adjacent plate, which partially cancels the field. With the plate charge \(Q\) fixed, the field inside falls to \(E_0/\kappa\), so \(V\) falls and \(C = Q/V\) rises.

Gauss' law with a dielectric

\[ \varepsilon_0\oint\kappa\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = q_{\text{free,enc}} \]

Only the free charge (the charge you put on the conductors) appears; the bound charge is accounted for by \(\kappa\). Valid for linear, isotropic dielectrics.

Each dielectric also has a dielectric strength, the maximum field before breakdown (about \(3\ \text{MV/m}\) for dry air, tens of MV/m for good polymers). This, not just \(C\), sets a capacitor's voltage rating.

Common mistake

Forgetting what is held constant. Battery disconnected: \(Q\) is fixed, so inserting a dielectric lowers \(V\) and lowers \(U = Q^2/(2C)\). Battery connected: \(V\) is fixed, so inserting a dielectric raises \(Q\) and raises \(U = \tfrac12 CV^2\) (the battery supplies the extra energy, and twice as much as is stored). Always identify the invariant first.

Example 5.1 — Parallel-plate capacitor

Two plates of \(2.0\ \text{cm}\times3.0\ \text{cm}\) are separated by \(0.10\ \text{mm}\) of air and connected to a \(12\ \text{V}\) source. Find \(C\), \(Q\), \(E\) and \(U\).

Show solution

\(A = 6.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\), \(d = 1.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}\).

\[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_0A}{d} = \frac{(8.854\times10^{-12})(6.0\times10^{-4})}{1.0\times10^{-4}}\ \text{F} = 53\ \text{pF}. \] \[ Q = CV = 6.4\times10^{-10}\ \text{C}, \qquad E = \frac{V}{d} = 1.2\times10^5\ \text{V/m}, \qquad U = \tfrac12CV^2 = 3.8\times10^{-9}\ \text{J}. \]

Check: \(E\) is well below air's breakdown field. Note how small the capacitance is: a farad of parallel-plate capacitance with a 0.1 mm gap would need about 11 km\(^2\) of plate area, which is why practical capacitors use thin films, high-\(\kappa\) dielectrics, or (in supercapacitors) nanometre-scale double layers.

Example 5.2 — Capacitance of a coaxial cable

A coax has inner conductor radius \(0.45\ \text{mm}\), outer conductor inner radius \(1.47\ \text{mm}\), and polyethylene dielectric (\(\kappa = 2.25\)). Find the capacitance per metre.

Show solution \[ \frac{C}{L} = \frac{2\pi\kappa\varepsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)} = \frac{2\pi(2.25)(8.854\times10^{-12})}{\ln(1.47/0.45)}\ \text{F/m} = \frac{1.25\times10^{-10}}{1.184}\ \text{F/m} = 106\ \text{pF/m}. \]

Check: this is close to the roughly 100 pF/m printed on datasheets for common 50 \(\Omega\) coax. The logarithm means \(C\) depends only weakly on the radius ratio. (Practice problem 10.6 completes this cable's analysis.)

Example 5.3 — Capacitor network

\(C_1 = 2.0\ \mu\text{F}\) and \(C_2 = 4.0\ \mu\text{F}\) are in series, and that pair is in parallel with \(C_3 = 3.0\ \mu\text{F}\). The combination is across \(12\ \text{V}\). Find the charge and voltage on each.

Show solution

Series pair: \(C_{12} = \dfrac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2} = \dfrac{(2)(4)}{6} = 1.33\ \mu\text{F}\). Total: \(C_{\text{eq}} = 1.33 + 3.0 = 4.33\ \mu\text{F}\).

The series branch has \(12\ \text{V}\) across it: \(Q_1 = Q_2 = (1.33\ \mu\text{F})(12\ \text{V}) = 16\ \mu\text{C}\). Then \(V_1 = 16/2 = 8.0\ \text{V}\), \(V_2 = 16/4 = 4.0\ \text{V}\) (sum 12 V, consistent).

\(C_3\): \(V_3 = 12\ \text{V}\), \(Q_3 = 36\ \mu\text{C}\). Check: total charge from the source \(16 + 36 = 52\ \mu\text{C} = (4.33\ \mu\text{F})(12\ \text{V})\). In the series pair, the smaller capacitor gets the larger voltage.

Example 5.4 — Inserting a dielectric

A \(10\ \text{pF}\) air capacitor is charged to \(100\ \text{V}\). A slab with \(\kappa = 4.0\) is then slid in to fill the gap. Find the new voltage and stored energy (a) if the battery was disconnected first, (b) if the battery stays connected.

Show solution

Initially \(Q_0 = 1.0\ \text{nC}\) and \(U_0 = \tfrac12(10\times10^{-12})(100)^2 = 5.0\times10^{-8}\ \text{J}\). New capacitance \(C = 40\ \text{pF}\).

(a) \(Q\) fixed: \(V = Q_0/C = 25\ \text{V}\); \(U = Q_0^2/(2C) = U_0/\kappa = 1.25\times10^{-8}\ \text{J}\). The missing \(3.75\times10^{-8}\ \text{J}\) went into the mechanical work of the field pulling the slab in (fringing fields attract the slab).

(b) \(V = 100\ \text{V}\) fixed: \(Q = 4.0\ \text{nC}\), \(U = \kappa U_0 = 2.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{J}\). The battery delivered \(\Delta Q\,V = (3.0\ \text{nC})(100\ \text{V}) = 3.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{J}\); half raised the stored energy and half did work on the slab.

6. Current & resistance

Halliday Ch. 26 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 9

Electrostatics assumed charges at rest, with \(\vec{E} = 0\) inside conductors. Connect a battery across a wire and that equilibrium is broken: a field exists inside the metal, and the free electrons drift along it. The drift is astonishingly slow (fractions of a millimetre per second), yet the signal travels near light speed, because the field is established everywhere along the wire almost at once. Collisions with the lattice act like friction, so the drift velocity is proportional to the field. That proportionality, at the microscopic level, is Ohm's law, and the "friction" turns electrical energy into heat.

6.1 Current & current density

Definitions (Current, current density)

Current is the rate at which charge passes through a cross-section: \(I = dq/dt\) (units ampere, \(1\ \text{A} = 1\ \text{C/s}\)). Its direction is, by convention, the direction positive charge would flow, opposite to electron motion in metals.

Current density \(\vec{J}\) is current per unit area, a vector field, with \(I = \int\vec{J}\cdot d\vec{A}\). For carriers of charge \(q\), number density \(n\) and drift velocity \(\vec{v}_d\):

\[ \vec{J} = nq\vec{v}_d, \qquad I = n|q|v_dA\ \text{(uniform } J\text{)}. \]

Charge conservation in steady state means the current is the same through every cross-section of a single wire, and the currents into any junction sum to the currents out (Kirchhoff's junction rule). In differential form, \(\nabla\cdot\vec{J} = -\partial\rho/\partial t\) (the continuity equation).

6.2 Resistance & resistivity

Definitions (Resistance, resistivity)

The resistance of a conductor between two terminals is \(R = V/I\) (ohm, \(\Omega\)). The resistivity \(\rho\) is the material property: \(\vec{E} = \rho\vec{J}\), with conductivity \(\sigma = 1/\rho\). For a uniform wire of length \(L\) and cross-section \(A\):

\[ R = \rho\frac{L}{A}. \]

Derivation of \(R = \rho L/A\). A uniform field \(E = V/L\) drives a uniform current density \(J = I/A\). Then \(\rho = E/J = (V/L)/(I/A) = RA/L\).

Temperature dependence. For metals near room temperature, \(\rho - \rho_0 \approx \rho_0\alpha(T - T_0)\), with \(\alpha\) of a few \(\times10^{-3}\ \text{K}^{-1}\) (copper: about \(3.9\times10^{-3}\) to \(4.3\times10^{-3}\ \text{K}^{-1}\) depending on the reference table). Hotter lattices vibrate more and scatter electrons more often. Copper's room-temperature resistivity is about \(1.69\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\text{·m}\).

Ohm's law (and its limits)

A device obeys Ohm's law if its resistance is independent of the applied voltage: \(I \propto V\). This is a property of some materials (metals at fixed temperature), not a law of nature. Diodes, transistors, gas discharges and filament lamps (whose temperature changes) are non-ohmic. The equation \(R = V/I\) is always a definition; Ohm's law is the claim that \(R\) is constant.

6.3 A microscopic view of Ohm's law

Model the conduction electrons (Drude model) as a gas moving randomly at high speed (around \(10^6\ \text{m/s}\), a quantum effect: the Fermi speed), colliding with the lattice on average every \(\tau\) seconds. Between collisions the field accelerates each electron at \(a = eE/m\); after a collision its velocity is randomised. The average extra velocity picked up is the drift velocity:

\[ v_d = a\tau = \frac{eE\tau}{m} \;\Rightarrow\; J = nev_d = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m}E \;\Rightarrow\; \rho = \frac{m}{ne^2\tau}. \]

Since \(\tau\) does not depend on \(E\) (the drift is a tiny perturbation on the much faster random motion), \(\rho\) is independent of \(E\): that is Ohm's law.

6.4 Power, semiconductors & superconductors

Electrical power

\[ P = IV \quad(\text{any device}), \qquad P = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}\quad(\text{resistor}). \]

Charge \(dq\) falling through potential difference \(V\) releases energy \(V\,dq\); per unit time that is \(IV\). In a resistor it becomes thermal energy (Joule heating).

Semiconductors (silicon, germanium) have far fewer carriers than metals (\(n \sim 10^{16}\ \text{m}^{-3}\) intrinsic silicon at room temperature versus \(10^{29}\ \text{m}^{-3}\) in copper), and their resistivity decreases with temperature because heating frees more carriers. Doping changes \(n\) by many orders of magnitude, which is the basis of all solid-state electronics. Superconductors lose all resistance below a critical temperature; currents in a superconducting loop persist for years.

Common mistake

Choosing the wrong power formula when comparing devices. On a fixed-voltage supply, \(P = V^2/R\): the lower-resistance bulb is brighter. In series (same current), \(P = I^2R\): the higher-resistance bulb is brighter. Decide which quantity is common before picking a formula.

Example 6.1 — Drift speed in household wire

A copper wire of diameter \(2.05\ \text{mm}\) (12 AWG) carries \(10\ \text{A}\). Copper has \(n = 8.49\times10^{28}\) free electrons per m\(^3\). Find the drift speed.

Show solution

\(A = \pi(1.025\times10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2 = 3.30\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2\).

\[ v_d = \frac{I}{neA} = \frac{10}{(8.49\times10^{28})(1.602\times10^{-19})(3.30\times10^{-6})}\ \text{m/s} = 2.2\times10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}. \]

About a fifth of a millimetre per second: an electron would take over an hour to travel a metre. Check units: \(\text{A}/(\text{m}^{-3}\cdot\text{C}\cdot\text{m}^2) = (\text{C/s})/(\text{C/m}) = \text{m/s}\).

Example 6.2 — Resistance and heating

Find the resistance of \(50\ \text{m}\) of the wire in Example 6.1 at \(20^\circ\text{C}\) and at \(60^\circ\text{C}\) (take \(\alpha = 3.9\times10^{-3}\ \text{K}^{-1}\)), and the power dissipated at \(10\ \text{A}\) when cold.

Show solution \[ R_{20} = \frac{\rho L}{A} = \frac{(1.69\times10^{-8})(50)}{3.30\times10^{-6}}\ \Omega = 0.256\ \Omega, \qquad R_{60} = R_{20}\big[1 + \alpha(40\ \text{K})\big] = 0.296\ \Omega. \]

\(P = I^2R = (10)^2(0.256) = 26\ \text{W}\) spread over 50 m, and a voltage drop \(IR = 2.6\ \text{V}\), about 2% of a 120 V supply. Electrical codes limit wire length and current for exactly this reason.

Example 6.3 — Mean free time in copper

Using the Drude result, estimate the mean time between collisions for conduction electrons in copper.

Show solution \[ \tau = \frac{m}{ne^2\rho} = \frac{9.109\times10^{-31}}{(8.49\times10^{28})(1.602\times10^{-19})^2(1.69\times10^{-8})}\ \text{s} = 2.5\times10^{-14}\ \text{s}. \]

Combined with the Fermi speed \(v_F \approx 1.6\times10^6\ \text{m/s}\), the mean free path is \(v_F\tau \approx 40\ \text{nm}\), roughly 150 atomic spacings: electrons in a good metal are not scattered by every atom, a fact only quantum mechanics explains.

7. DC circuits

Halliday Ch. 27 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 10

A resistor turns electrical energy into heat, so a steady current needs a source that continually does work on the charges, pushing them "uphill" in potential. That source is an emf device: a battery (chemical energy), a generator (mechanical), a solar cell (light). Circuit analysis then reduces to two conservation laws: charge is conserved at every junction, and energy is conserved around every loop. Most of this will be familiar to an EE; the physics worth dwelling on is why the rules hold, and the RC circuit, our first system with a time constant.

7.1 EMF & internal resistance

Definition (EMF)

The emf \(\mathcal{E}\) of a source is the work it does per unit charge moving charge from its low-potential terminal to its high-potential terminal: \(\mathcal{E} = dW/dq\) (volts). Despite the name ("electromotive force") it is not a force. A real source behaves like an ideal emf in series with an internal resistance \(r\), so its terminal voltage is

\[ V_{\text{terminal}} = \mathcal{E} - Ir \quad(\text{discharging}). \]

With a load \(R\): \(I = \mathcal{E}/(R + r)\), and the power to the load, \(P = \mathcal{E}^2R/(R+r)^2\), is maximised when \(R = r\) (set \(dP/dR = 0\)). This is the DC version of impedance matching; at the matched point the efficiency is only 50%, which is why power utilities never operate there, while RF systems do.

7.2 Series & parallel resistors

Equivalent resistance

\[ R_{\text{ser}} = \sum_j R_j \qquad\qquad \frac{1}{R_{\text{par}}} = \sum_j\frac{1}{R_j}\quad\left(\text{two: } \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}\right) \]

Series: same current, voltages add. Parallel: same voltage, currents add. The parallel equivalent is always smaller than the smallest branch.

7.3 Kirchhoff's rules

Kirchhoff's rules

Junction rule (charge conservation): the sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum leaving.

Loop rule (energy conservation / conservative \(\vec{E}\)): the algebraic sum of potential changes around any closed loop is zero, \(\sum\Delta V = 0\).

Sign conventions for the loop rule: crossing a resistor in the direction of the assumed current gives \(-IR\); against it, \(+IR\). Crossing an emf from \(-\) to \(+\) gives \(+\mathcal{E}\); from \(+\) to \(-\), \(-\mathcal{E}\).

Validity: lumped circuits, where the circuit is small compared with the wavelength of any signal and there is no changing magnetic flux through the loop. With changing flux (Chapter 10) the loop integral of \(\vec{E}\) is no longer zero; the standard fix is to treat inductors as circuit elements with their own voltage drop.

Study tip

Guess a direction for every branch current and do not agonise over it: a negative answer just means the current flows the other way. For \(b\) branches and \(n\) nodes you need \(n-1\) junction equations and \(b-n+1\) independent loop equations. Engineers will recognise that nodal analysis (one unknown per node voltage) is usually faster; it is the same physics. Use one method to solve and the other to check.

Meters. An ideal ammeter (in series) has zero resistance; an ideal voltmeter (in parallel) has infinite resistance. Real ones perturb the circuit, and the size of the perturbation is set by comparing the meter resistance with the circuit resistances it is placed in or across.

7.4 RC circuits

Charging: derivation. Close a switch connecting an emf \(\mathcal{E}\), resistor \(R\) and initially uncharged capacitor \(C\) in series. The loop rule at any instant:

\[ \mathcal{E} - iR - \frac{q}{C} = 0, \qquad i = \frac{dq}{dt} \;\Rightarrow\; R\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{q}{C} = \mathcal{E}. \]

Separate variables: \(\dfrac{dq}{C\mathcal{E} - q} = \dfrac{dt}{RC}\). Integrating from \(q = 0\) at \(t = 0\): \(-\ln\dfrac{C\mathcal{E}-q}{C\mathcal{E}} = \dfrac{t}{RC}\), so

\[ q(t) = C\mathcal{E}\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right), \qquad i(t) = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}e^{-t/RC}, \qquad v_C(t) = \mathcal{E}\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right). \]

Discharging. With the source removed, \(R\,dq/dt + q/C = 0\) gives \(q(t) = Q_0e^{-t/RC}\) and \(i = -(Q_0/RC)e^{-t/RC}\).

Time constant

\[ \tau = RC \qquad (\Omega\cdot\text{F} = \text{s}) \]

After \(1\tau\) a charging capacitor reaches 63.2% of its final charge; after \(5\tau\), 99.3%. At \(t = 0\) the uncharged capacitor acts like a short circuit; as \(t\to\infty\) it acts like an open circuit.

Energy bookkeeping. During a full charge the source delivers \(Q\mathcal{E} = C\mathcal{E}^2\). The capacitor stores \(\tfrac12C\mathcal{E}^2\). The resistor dissipates \(\int_0^\infty i^2R\,dt = \frac{\mathcal{E}^2}{R}\cdot\frac{RC}{2} = \tfrac12C\mathcal{E}^2\), exactly half, independent of \(R\). (This 50% loss is the reason charge-pump and switched-capacitor designs care about how they charge.)

Common mistake

Using the full circuit resistance for \(\tau\) when the capacitor sees something different. The correct \(R\) is the Thévenin resistance seen by the capacitor with sources zeroed. A capacitor charged through \(R_1\) and discharged through \(R_2\) has two different time constants.

Example 7.1 — Battery with internal resistance

A battery with \(\mathcal{E} = 12.0\ \text{V}\) and \(r = 0.50\ \Omega\) drives a \(5.5\ \Omega\) load. Find the current, terminal voltage, and the power delivered to the load and wasted inside the battery.

Show solution \[ I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R+r} = \frac{12.0}{6.0} = 2.0\ \text{A}, \qquad V_t = \mathcal{E} - Ir = 11.0\ \text{V}. \]

\(P_{\text{load}} = I^2R = 22\ \text{W}\), \(P_r = I^2r = 2.0\ \text{W}\). Check: total \(24\ \text{W} = \mathcal{E}I\). Energy conserved.

Example 7.2 — Two-loop circuit

Three branches connect node \(a\) (top) to node \(b\) (bottom). Left branch: \(\mathcal{E}_1 = 12\ \text{V}\) (positive terminal toward \(a\)) in series with \(R_1 = 4.0\ \Omega\). Middle branch: \(R_3 = 6.0\ \Omega\). Right branch: \(\mathcal{E}_2 = 6.0\ \text{V}\) (positive terminal toward \(a\)) in series with \(R_2 = 2.0\ \Omega\). Find all branch currents.

Show solution

Assume \(I_1\) flows up through the left branch into \(a\), \(I_2\) up through the right branch into \(a\), and \(I_3\) down through the middle. Junction \(a\): \(I_1 + I_2 = I_3\).

Left loop (up the left, down the middle): \(\mathcal{E}_1 - I_1R_1 - I_3R_3 = 0 \Rightarrow 12 = 4I_1 + 6I_3\).

Right loop (up the right, down the middle): \(\mathcal{E}_2 - I_2R_2 - I_3R_3 = 0 \Rightarrow 6 = 2I_2 + 6I_3\).

Substitute \(I_3 = I_1 + I_2\): \(12 = 10I_1 + 6I_2\) and \(6 = 6I_1 + 8I_2\). Solving, \(I_1 = 15/11\ \text{A} = 1.36\ \text{A}\), \(I_2 = -3/11\ \text{A} = -0.27\ \text{A}\), \(I_3 = 12/11\ \text{A} = 1.09\ \text{A}\).

The negative \(I_2\) means current is driven down through the 6 V source: the 12 V battery is charging it.

Check by nodal analysis (\(V_b = 0\)): \(\frac{12 - V_a}{4} + \frac{6-V_a}{2} = \frac{V_a}{6} \Rightarrow 72 = 11V_a\), \(V_a = 6.55\ \text{V}\), and \(I_3 = V_a/6 = 12/11\ \text{A}\). ✓

Example 7.3 — RC charging

A \(47\ \mu\text{F}\) capacitor charges through \(10\ \text{k}\Omega\) from a \(9.0\ \text{V}\) source. Find \(\tau\), the time to reach \(6.0\ \text{V}\), and the initial current.

Show solution

\(\tau = RC = (10^4\ \Omega)(47\times10^{-6}\ \text{F}) = 0.47\ \text{s}\). Solve \(v_C = \mathcal{E}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\) for \(t\):

\[ t = -\tau\ln\left(1 - \frac{v_C}{\mathcal{E}}\right) = (0.47\ \text{s})\ln 3 = 0.52\ \text{s}. \]

\(i(0) = \mathcal{E}/R = 0.90\ \text{mA}\). Check: \(6/9 = 67\%\), a bit more than the 63% reached at \(t = \tau\), so \(t\) slightly above \(\tau\) makes sense.

Example 7.4 — Energy during discharge

How long after discharge begins does a capacitor retain half of its stored energy, in units of \(\tau\)?

Show solution

\(U = q^2/(2C) \propto e^{-2t/\tau}\). Set \(e^{-2t/\tau} = \tfrac12\): \(t = \tfrac12\tau\ln2 = 0.35\tau\). Energy decays with time constant \(\tau/2\), twice as fast as charge, because it goes as the square.

8. Magnetic fields & forces

Halliday Ch. 28 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 11

Moving charges feel a second kind of force, one that depends on their velocity and acts sideways to it. We describe it with a magnetic field \(\vec{B}\). Because the magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, it changes a charge's direction but never its speed: magnetic fields do no work on point charges. That single fact explains circular orbits in cyclotrons and mass spectrometers, the helical paths of particles in Earth's field, and (via the forces on the electrons inside a wire) the torque that drives every electric motor. This chapter takes \(\vec{B}\) as given; Chapter 9 asks where it comes from.

8.1 The magnetic force on a moving charge

Law (Lorentz force)

\[ \vec{F} = q\vec{E} + q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}, \qquad |\vec{F}_B| = |q|vB\sin\phi \]

where \(\phi\) is the angle between \(\vec{v}\) and \(\vec{B}\). The magnetic part is perpendicular to both \(\vec{v}\) and \(\vec{B}\) (right-hand rule, reversed for negative \(q\)), and vanishes for motion along \(\vec{B}\). This equation defines \(\vec{B}\): its SI unit is the tesla, \(1\ \text{T} = 1\ \text{N/(A·m)}\). Also used: \(1\ \text{gauss} = 10^{-4}\ \text{T}\). Earth's field is about \(50\ \mu\text{T}\); a hospital MRI magnet, 1.5–3 T.

No work: the power delivered by the magnetic force is \(\vec{F}_B\cdot\vec{v} = q(\vec{v}\times\vec{B})\cdot\vec{v} = 0\). Kinetic energy is unchanged; only direction changes.

Magnetic field lines, unlike electric field lines, have no beginning or end: they form closed loops (exiting a magnet's north pole, re-entering at the south pole, and continuing through the magnet). There are no magnetic monopoles, which is Gauss' law for magnetism (Chapter 12).

8.2 Crossed fields: velocity selector & Hall effect

When \(\vec{E}\) and \(\vec{B}\) are perpendicular and arranged so that the electric and magnetic forces oppose, they cancel for one particular speed:

\[ qE = qvB \;\Rightarrow\; v = \frac{E}{B}. \]

Faster particles are deflected one way, slower ones the other: a velocity selector. J.J. Thomson used crossed fields in 1897 to measure \(e/m\) for the electron.

Hall effect. Pass a current along a flat conducting strip in a perpendicular magnetic field. The carriers are pushed sideways and pile up on one edge until the resulting transverse electric field \(E_H\) balances the magnetic force: \(eE_H = ev_dB\). With strip width \(w\) and thickness \(t\), and using \(v_d = I/(nqwt)\):

Hall voltage

\[ V_H = E_Hw = \frac{IB}{n\,q\,t} \]

The sign of \(V_H\) reveals the sign of the carriers (the Hall effect proved that conduction in some materials is by positive "holes"); its size gives \(n\). Because \(V_H \propto 1/n\), semiconductor Hall sensors give millivolts where metals give microvolts; they are used for contactless current sensing, brushless-motor commutation and position sensing.

8.3 Circular motion: the cyclotron & mass spectrometer

A charge moving perpendicular to a uniform \(\vec{B}\) experiences a constant-magnitude force always perpendicular to its velocity, i.e. a centripetal force. Newton's second law:

\[ |q|vB = \frac{mv^2}{r} \;\Rightarrow\; r = \frac{mv}{|q|B}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi m}{|q|B}, \qquad f = \frac{|q|B}{2\pi m}. \]

The period does not depend on speed (for \(v \ll c\)): faster particles move in bigger circles but take the same time. If \(\vec{v}\) has a component along \(\vec{B}\), that component is unaffected and the path is a helix with pitch \(v_\parallel T\). Charged particles spiralling along Earth's field lines toward the poles produce the aurora.

Cyclotron. Two hollow D-shaped electrodes sit in a uniform \(\vec{B}\). An alternating voltage at the cyclotron frequency \(f\) kicks the particle each time it crosses the gap. Since \(f\) is speed-independent, a fixed-frequency oscillator stays in step as the orbit grows. The maximum kinetic energy is set by the dee radius \(R\): \(K = q^2B^2R^2/(2m)\). (Once relativistic mass growth matters, the resonance drifts, requiring synchrocyclotrons and synchrotrons.)

Mass spectrometer. Ions accelerated through \(V\) have \(v = \sqrt{2qV/m}\); entering a field \(B\) they follow radius \(r = \frac{1}{B}\sqrt{\frac{2mV}{q}}\), which separates isotopes by mass.

8.4 Force on a current-carrying wire

A current is many moving charges, so a wire in a field feels the sum of their magnetic forces. A segment of length \(L\) contains charge \(q = nAL\,e\) drifting at \(v_d\); the total force is \(nALe\,v_dB\sin\phi = ILB\sin\phi\).

Force on a current

\[ \vec{F} = I\vec{L}\times\vec{B} \quad(\text{straight wire, uniform } B), \qquad d\vec{F} = I\,d\vec{L}\times\vec{B}\quad(\text{general}). \]

\(\vec{L}\) points along the conventional current. In a uniform field the force on any curved wire equals that on a straight wire joining its endpoints; the force on any closed loop is zero.

8.5 Torque on a current loop; the magnetic dipole

In a uniform field, a rectangular loop feels zero net force, but forces on opposite sides form a couple. For a loop with sides \(a\) (perpendicular to the rotation axis) and \(b\), the two sides of length \(b\) each feel \(IbB\) with lever arm \((a/2)\sin\theta\), giving \(\tau = IabB\sin\theta = IAB\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between the loop normal and \(\vec{B}\).

Definition (Magnetic dipole moment)

For a flat coil of \(N\) turns, area \(A\), current \(I\): \(\vec{\mu} = NIA\,\hat{n}\), with \(\hat{n}\) given by the right-hand rule (fingers along the current, thumb along \(\hat{n}\)). Units A·m\(^2\) = J/T.

Torque and energy of a magnetic dipole

\[ \vec{\tau} = \vec{\mu}\times\vec{B}, \qquad U = -\vec{\mu}\cdot\vec{B} \]

Exactly parallel to the electric dipole (\(\vec{\tau} = \vec{p}\times\vec{E}\), \(U = -\vec{p}\cdot\vec{E}\)). A compass needle, a current loop, an electron's spin and a bar magnet are all magnetic dipoles that tend to align with \(\vec{B}\).

Motors. A DC motor is a coil in a field. Left alone, the torque would just rotate the coil to alignment and stop; a commutator reverses the current every half-turn so the torque keeps the same sense. The galvanometer (the original analogue meter) balances \(\tau = NIAB\) against a spring torque, giving a deflection proportional to current.

Common mistake

Forgetting the sign of the charge in \(q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}\). Use your right hand for \(\vec{v}\times\vec{B}\), then flip the result for electrons. Also, \(\theta\) in \(\tau = \mu B\sin\theta\) is measured from the loop's normal, not from its plane: the torque is maximum when the plane of the loop is parallel to \(\vec{B}\).

Example 8.1 — Proton in a magnetic field

A proton moves with \(\vec{v} = (2.0\times10^5\ \text{m/s})\,\hat{\imath}\) through \(\vec{B} = (0.50\ \text{T})\,\hat{\jmath}\). Find the force and the radius of its orbit.

Show solution \[ \vec{F} = e\vec{v}\times\vec{B} = (1.602\times10^{-19})(2.0\times10^5)(0.50)\,(\hat{\imath}\times\hat{\jmath})\ \text{N} = (1.6\times10^{-14}\ \text{N})\,\hat{k}. \] \[ r = \frac{mv}{eB} = \frac{(1.673\times10^{-27})(2.0\times10^5)}{(1.602\times10^{-19})(0.50)}\ \text{m} = 4.2\ \text{mm}. \]

Check: the force is \(10^{12}\) times the proton's weight, so ignoring gravity is fine. An electron at the same speed would circle the opposite way with radius smaller by \(m_e/m_p \approx 1/1836\).

Example 8.2 — Hall voltage in copper

A copper strip \(0.10\ \text{mm}\) thick carries \(5.0\ \text{A}\) in a perpendicular field of \(1.2\ \text{T}\). Find \(V_H\).

Show solution \[ V_H = \frac{IB}{net} = \frac{(5.0)(1.2)}{(8.49\times10^{28})(1.602\times10^{-19})(1.0\times10^{-4})}\ \text{V} = 4.4\ \mu\text{V}. \]

Tiny, because \(n\) is huge. A doped silicon sensor with \(n \sim 10^{21}\ \text{m}^{-3}\) would give a voltage about \(10^8\) times larger for the same geometry, which is why practical Hall sensors are semiconductors (and run at much lower currents).

Example 8.3 — Cyclotron

A cyclotron with dee radius \(0.50\ \text{m}\) and \(B = 1.5\ \text{T}\) accelerates protons. Find the oscillator frequency and the maximum kinetic energy.

Show solution \[ f = \frac{eB}{2\pi m_p} = \frac{(1.602\times10^{-19})(1.5)}{2\pi(1.673\times10^{-27})}\ \text{Hz} = 22.9\ \text{MHz}. \] \[ K = \frac{e^2B^2R^2}{2m_p} = \frac{(1.602\times10^{-19})^2(1.5)^2(0.50)^2}{2(1.673\times10^{-27})}\ \text{J} = 4.3\times10^{-12}\ \text{J} = 27\ \text{MeV}. \]

Check: 27 MeV is about 3% of the proton rest energy (938 MeV), so the non-relativistic treatment is marginal but acceptable; at higher energies the orbit falls out of step with the fixed frequency.

Example 8.4 — Torque on a coil

A 100-turn rectangular coil, \(4.0\ \text{cm}\times6.0\ \text{cm}\), carries \(0.20\ \text{A}\) in a \(0.30\ \text{T}\) field. Its normal makes \(60^\circ\) with \(\vec{B}\). Find \(\mu\), \(\tau\) and \(U\).

Show solution

\(\mu = NIA = (100)(0.20)(2.4\times10^{-3}) = 0.048\ \text{A·m}^2\).

\[ \tau = \mu B\sin60^\circ = (0.048)(0.30)(0.866) = 1.2\times10^{-2}\ \text{N·m}, \qquad U = -\mu B\cos60^\circ = -7.2\times10^{-3}\ \text{J}. \]

Rotating it to alignment (\(U = -14.4\ \text{mJ}\)) would release \(7.2\ \text{mJ}\).

9. Magnetic fields due to currents

Halliday Ch. 29 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 12

Currents feel magnetic forces, and currents also create magnetic fields: Oersted noticed in 1820 that a current-carrying wire deflects a compass. This chapter is the magnetic analogue of Chapters 2 and 3. The Biot–Savart law plays the role of Coulomb's law (integrate over the sources), and Ampère's law plays the role of Gauss' law (a shortcut for symmetric situations). The new feature is geometry: the field of a current element wraps around it rather than pointing away from it, so cross products appear everywhere.

9.1 The Biot–Savart law

Law (Biot–Savart)

A current element \(I\,d\vec{s}\) produces at displacement \(\vec{r}\) (from the element to the field point) the field

\[ d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,d\vec{s}\times\hat{r}}{r^2}, \qquad \mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T·m/A}\ (\approx 1.2566\times10^{-6}). \]

Magnitude \(dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,ds\sin\theta}{r^2}\). Validity: steady currents (magnetostatics). The total field is the vector integral over the whole circuit. (Since the 2019 SI redefinition, \(\mu_0\) is measured rather than exact, but equals \(4\pi\times10^{-7}\) to about one part in \(10^{10}\).)

Like Coulomb's law it is inverse-square, but the cross product makes the field perpendicular to both the current and \(\hat{r}\). Points directly along the line of a current element get no field from it.

9.2 Wires, arcs & loops

Straight wire: derivation. Wire along \(x\), field point at perpendicular distance \(R\). An element at \(x\) has \(r = \sqrt{x^2+R^2}\) and \(\sin\theta = R/r\). Every element gives \(d\vec{B}\) in the same direction (circling the wire), so magnitudes add:

\[ B = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{R\,dx}{(x^2+R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi R}\left[\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+R^2}}\right]_{x_1}^{x_2}. \]

For an infinite wire the bracket is \(1-(-1) = 2\):

\[ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi R}. \]

The field lines are circles around the wire; direction by the right-hand rule (thumb along the current, fingers curl along \(\vec{B}\)). For a semi-infinite wire ending opposite the field point, the result is half: \(\mu_0I/(4\pi R)\).

Circular arc at its centre. Every element is at distance \(R\) with \(d\vec{s}\perp\hat{r}\): \(B = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi R^2}\int ds = \frac{\mu_0I\phi}{4\pi R}\) for an arc subtending angle \(\phi\). A full loop (\(\phi = 2\pi\)) gives \(B = \mu_0I/(2R)\).

Loop on its axis. Each element is at \(r = \sqrt{R^2+z^2}\), and \(d\vec{s}\perp\hat{r}\). The components perpendicular to the axis cancel; the axial component carries a factor \(R/r\):

\[ B_z = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{R}{r^3}\oint ds = \frac{\mu_0IR^2}{2(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}} \;\xrightarrow{z\gg R}\; \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\frac{\mu}{z^3}, \quad \mu = I\pi R^2. \]

Far away, a current loop's field has exactly the form of an electric dipole's field (\(p\to\mu\), \(1/\varepsilon_0\to\mu_0\)): a loop is a magnetic dipole.

9.3 Force between parallel currents

Wire 1 makes a field \(\mu_0I_1/(2\pi d)\) at wire 2, which then feels \(F = I_2LB\). Per unit length:

Parallel currents

\[ \frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1I_2}{2\pi d} \]

Parallel currents attract; antiparallel currents repel (the opposite of the rule for like charges). Until 2019 this formula defined the ampere: 1 A in each of two wires 1 m apart gives \(2\times10^{-7}\ \text{N/m}\).

Engineering consequences: high-current bus bars carrying fault currents experience large forces and must be braced; the "pinch effect" squeezes plasma columns and lightning channels.

9.4 Ampère's law

Law (Ampère)

\[ \oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{s} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} \]

The line integral is around any closed loop (an "Amperian loop"); \(I_{\text{enc}}\) is the net current piercing any surface bounded by it, with sign by the right-hand rule (curl fingers along the integration direction; current along the thumb counts positive). Validity: steady currents. For changing electric fields a displacement-current term must be added (Chapter 12). Differential form: \(\nabla\times\vec{B} = \mu_0\vec{J}\).

Infinite wire, the easy way. By symmetry \(\vec{B}\) circles the wire with magnitude depending only on \(r\). Take a circular loop of radius \(r\): \(B\cdot2\pi r = \mu_0I\), so \(B = \mu_0I/(2\pi r)\), the Biot–Savart result in one line.

Inside a thick wire (radius \(R\), uniform current density): \(I_{\text{enc}} = I r^2/R^2\), so

\[ B = \frac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi R^2}\ (r \lt R), \qquad B = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\ (r \gt R). \]

Study tip

Ampère's law, like Gauss', is always true but only useful with symmetry: infinite straight wires and cylinders, infinite solenoids, toroids, infinite current sheets. Choose the loop so that along each piece \(\vec{B}\) is either parallel to \(d\vec{s}\) with constant magnitude, or perpendicular to it, or zero.

9.5 Solenoids & toroids

Ideal solenoid (\(n\) turns per unit length, current \(I\)). Symmetry and the near-cancellation of fields between adjacent turns make the interior field uniform and axial, and the exterior field negligible. Take a rectangular Amperian loop with one side of length \(h\) inside along the axis and the opposite side outside. Only the inside side contributes:

\[ Bh = \mu_0(nh)I \;\Rightarrow\; B = \mu_0 nI. \]

Independent of the radius and of the position inside: the solenoid is to magnetism what the parallel-plate capacitor is to electrostatics, a source of uniform field.

Toroid (\(N\) turns wound on a doughnut). A circular loop of radius \(r\) inside the core encloses \(NI\): \(B\cdot2\pi r = \mu_0NI\), so \(B = \mu_0NI/(2\pi r)\). Outside the core, the loop encloses zero net current and \(B = 0\). This field confinement is why toroidal inductors and transformers produce little stray field.

Common mistake

Using \(n\) as total turns. In \(B = \mu_0nI\), \(n = N/\ell\) is turns per unit length. And in the toroid formula \(B\) varies with \(r\) across the core; it is not uniform.

Example 9.1 — Field near household wiring

A long straight wire carries \(10\ \text{A}\). Find \(B\) at \(5.0\ \text{cm}\) and compare with Earth's field (\(\approx 50\ \mu\text{T}\)).

Show solution \[ B = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r} = \frac{(2\times10^{-7}\ \text{T·m/A})(10\ \text{A})}{0.050\ \text{m}} = 4.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T} = 40\ \mu\text{T}. \]

Comparable to Earth's field, so a compass near a single conductor is unreliable. In a real cable the hot and neutral conductors carry opposite currents a few millimetres apart; their fields largely cancel, and the residual (a "line-pair" field) falls off as \(1/r^2\).

Example 9.2 — Field of a coil

A flat circular coil of 50 turns, radius \(10\ \text{cm}\), carries \(2.0\ \text{A}\). Find \(B\) at the centre and on the axis \(10\ \text{cm}\) away.

Show solution \[ B_0 = \frac{N\mu_0I}{2R} = \frac{50(4\pi\times10^{-7})(2.0)}{2(0.10)}\ \text{T} = 6.3\times10^{-4}\ \text{T}. \]

At \(z = R\): \(B = B_0\frac{R^3}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}} = B_0/2^{3/2} = 0.354B_0 = 2.2\times10^{-4}\ \text{T}\).

Check: the field falls off steadily along the axis. Placing two such coils a distance \(R\) apart (a Helmholtz pair) makes the second derivative of the combined field vanish at the midpoint, giving a very uniform field region for calibration work.

Example 9.3 — Inside and outside a thick wire

A wire of radius \(2.0\ \text{mm}\) carries \(20\ \text{A}\) uniformly. Find \(B\) at \(r = 1.0\ \text{mm}\), \(2.0\ \text{mm}\) and \(4.0\ \text{mm}\).

Show solution

\(\mu_0I/(2\pi) = 4.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{T·m}\).

\(r = 1.0\ \text{mm}\) (inside): \(B = (4.0\times10^{-6})\,r/R^2 = (4.0\times10^{-6})(10^{-3})/(4.0\times10^{-6}) = 1.0\ \text{mT}\).

\(r = 2.0\ \text{mm}\) (surface): \(B = 4.0\times10^{-6}/0.0020 = 2.0\ \text{mT}\), the maximum.

\(r = 4.0\ \text{mm}\) (outside): \(B = 4.0\times10^{-6}/0.0040 = 1.0\ \text{mT}\).

Check: continuous at \(r = R\); linear rise inside, \(1/r\) fall outside.

Example 9.4 — Designing a solenoid

A solenoid is wound with 2000 turns per metre. What current gives an interior field of \(50\ \text{mT}\)?

Show solution \[ I = \frac{B}{\mu_0 n} = \frac{0.050}{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(2000)}\ \text{A} = 20\ \text{A}. \]

That is a lot of current, and the \(I^2R\) heating is why strong electromagnets use iron cores (Chapter 12) or superconducting windings.

Practice problems

  1. Two long parallel wires \(4.0\ \text{cm}\) apart carry \(5.0\ \text{A}\) and \(8.0\ \text{A}\) in the same direction. Find the force per metre.
    Answer

    \(F/L = (2\times10^{-7})(5.0)(8.0)/0.040 = 2.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{N/m}\), attractive.

  2. A wire runs in from infinity radially, around a semicircle of radius \(R\), and out radially to infinity. Find \(B\) at the centre.
    Answer

    Radial segments point at the centre (\(d\vec{s}\parallel\hat{r}\)) and contribute nothing. The arc gives \(\mu_0I\pi/(4\pi R) = \mu_0I/(4R)\).

  3. Find \(B\) at the centre of a square loop of side \(a\) carrying current \(I\). Evaluate for \(a = 10\ \text{cm}\), \(I = 1.0\ \text{A}\).
    Answer

    Each side, at distance \(a/2\) with end angles giving \(\sin\) terms of \(1/\sqrt2\): \(\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi(a/2)}\sqrt2\). Four sides: \(B = \frac{2\sqrt2\,\mu_0I}{\pi a} = 1.1\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}\). Check: the inscribed circle (diameter \(a\)) gives \(\mu_0I/a = 1.26\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}\), slightly more, because all of its wire is at the minimum distance \(a/2\) while the square's corners are farther away.

  4. An Amperian loop encloses a wire carrying \(3.0\ \text{A}\) out of the page and another carrying \(5.0\ \text{A}\) into the page. Find \(\oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{s}\) (counter-clockwise).
    Answer

    Counter-clockwise means "out of page" is positive: \(\mu_0(3.0 - 5.0) = -2\mu_0 = -2.5\times10^{-6}\ \text{T·m}\).

  5. A toroid has 500 turns, inner radius \(10\ \text{cm}\), outer radius \(15\ \text{cm}\), and carries \(0.80\ \text{A}\). Find the field range in the core.
    Answer

    \(B = \mu_0NI/(2\pi r) = (8.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T·m})/r\): \(0.80\ \text{mT}\) at the inner edge, \(0.53\ \text{mT}\) at the outer edge.

  6. Why is the magnetic field outside an ideal coaxial cable zero?
    Answer

    The inner and outer conductors carry equal and opposite currents. A circular Amperian loop outside encloses zero net current, and by symmetry \(B\) is uniform on it, so \(B = 0\). Coax confines both its \(\vec{E}\) (Chapter 3) and its \(\vec{B}\).

10. Induction & inductance

Currents make magnetic fields; Faraday asked whether magnetic fields can make currents. They can, but only when something changes: a magnet moving toward a coil, a loop rotating in a field, a current switching on in a neighbouring circuit. A changing magnetic flux induces an emf, and nature always arranges the induced current to oppose the change (Lenz's law), a direct expression of energy conservation. Induction is the principle behind generators, transformers, inductors, wireless charging, induction cooktops and every magnetic sensor, and it is the second half of the mutual field generation that makes light possible.

10.1 Faraday's law

Definition (Magnetic flux)

\[ \Phi_B = \int\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{A} \qquad(\text{weber, } 1\ \text{Wb} = 1\ \text{T·m}^2) \]

For a flat loop of area \(A\) in a uniform field at angle \(\theta\) to the normal, \(\Phi_B = BA\cos\theta\).

Law (Faraday)

\[ \mathcal{E} = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

for a coil of \(N\) tightly wound turns, each linking flux \(\Phi_B\) (\(N\Phi_B\) is the "flux linkage"). The flux can change because \(B\) changes, the area changes, or the orientation changes. The minus sign is Lenz's law.

10.2 Lenz's law

Lenz's law

An induced current flows in the direction such that its own magnetic field opposes the change in flux that produced it.

If the induced current instead aided the change, the flux would grow further, inducing more current: a runaway that creates energy from nothing. Lenz's law is therefore required by energy conservation. Practical consequences: a magnet dropped through a copper pipe falls slowly (eddy currents), induction brakes on trains and roller coasters, and the "back emf" of a spinning motor that limits its current.

Study tip (finding the direction)

(1) Which way does the external \(\vec{B}\) point through the loop? (2) Is its flux increasing or decreasing? (3) The induced \(\vec{B}\) points opposite to the external field if the flux is increasing, along it if decreasing. (4) Right-hand rule converts induced \(\vec{B}\) into a current direction. Opposing the change, not the field, is the key phrase.

10.3 Motional EMF & energy transfer

A conducting rod of length \(L\) slides at speed \(v\) along rails, perpendicular to a uniform \(\vec{B}\), completing a circuit of resistance \(R\). The enclosed area grows at rate \(Lv\), so

\[ |\mathcal{E}| = \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = BLv, \qquad I = \frac{BLv}{R}. \]

Microscopically, each charge in the rod feels \(q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}\) along the rod; this magnetic force per charge, integrated along the rod, is the emf. The current in the rod then feels a retarding force \(F = ILB = B^2L^2v/R\). To keep the rod moving, an agent must supply mechanical power \(Fv = B^2L^2v^2/R\), which equals the electrical power \(I^2R\) dissipated in the circuit. Mechanical work becomes heat, with the magnetic field as intermediary: this is a generator in miniature.

AC generator. A coil of \(N\) turns and area \(A\) rotating at \(\omega\) in uniform \(B\) has \(\Phi_B = BA\cos\omega t\), so \(\mathcal{E} = NBA\omega\sin\omega t\): sinusoidal AC arises naturally from rotation.

10.4 Induced electric fields

If a stationary loop sits in a changing \(\vec{B}\), no \(\vec{v}\times\vec{B}\) force acts, yet a current flows. Something must push the charges: a changing magnetic field creates an electric field, whether or not a wire is present.

Faraday's law (general form)

\[ \oint\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{s} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \nabla\times\vec{E} = -\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t}. \]

The induced \(\vec{E}\) is non-conservative: its field lines form closed loops and its line integral around a loop is not zero. Hence the electric potential is not defined for induced fields, and Kirchhoff's loop rule in its naive form fails for loops threaded by changing flux.

For a long solenoid of radius \(R\) with \(dB/dt\) inside, symmetry makes \(\vec{E}\) circular. A circle of radius \(r\) gives \(E\cdot2\pi r = \pi r^2\,dB/dt\) inside, and \(\pi R^2\,dB/dt\) outside:

\[ E = \frac{r}{2}\left|\frac{dB}{dt}\right|\ (r \lt R), \qquad E = \frac{R^2}{2r}\left|\frac{dB}{dt}\right|\ (r \gt R). \]

The betatron accelerates electrons with exactly this field.

Common mistake

Connecting a voltmeter across a region with changing flux and expecting a unique reading. With a non-conservative \(\vec{E}\), the "voltage" between two points depends on the path, including the path of the meter leads. This is a real measurement issue (lead dress in power electronics), not a textbook curiosity.

10.5 Self-inductance

Definition (Inductance)

A coil's own current produces flux linkage proportional to that current: \(N\Phi_B = LI\). The constant \(L\) is the inductance (henry, \(1\ \text{H} = 1\ \text{Wb/A} = 1\ \text{V·s/A}\)). By Faraday, a changing current induces a self-emf

\[ \mathcal{E}_L = -L\frac{dI}{dt}. \]

Solenoid: derivation. Length \(\ell\), \(N\) turns, cross-section \(A\), \(n = N/\ell\). \(B = \mu_0nI\), so each turn links \(\Phi_B = \mu_0nIA\), and

\[ L = \frac{N\Phi_B}{I} = \mu_0n^2A\ell = \frac{\mu_0N^2A}{\ell}. \]

Like capacitance, inductance depends only on geometry (and on the core material, Chapter 12). Note the \(N^2\): doubling the turns quadruples \(L\), because each turn both makes the field and links it.

10.6 RL circuits

Switch an emf \(\mathcal{E}\) onto a series \(R\) and \(L\). Loop rule (treating the inductor as a drop \(L\,di/dt\)):

\[ \mathcal{E} - iR - L\frac{di}{dt} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; i(t) = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_L}\right), \qquad \tau_L = \frac{L}{R}. \]

(Same separable equation as RC charging.) Removing the source with the current path intact gives decay \(i = I_0e^{-t/\tau_L}\). An inductor resists changes in current: at \(t = 0\) it behaves like an open circuit, as \(t\to\infty\) like a wire.

Common mistake

Opening a switch in series with an inductor "instantly". \(L\,di/dt\) becomes huge, producing a large voltage spike (that is how ignition coils and boost converters work deliberately, and how relay contacts and transistors get destroyed accidentally). The standard fix is a flyback diode that gives the current somewhere to go.

10.7 Energy stored in a magnetic field

Multiply the RL loop equation by \(i\): \(\mathcal{E}i = i^2R + Li\,\frac{di}{dt}\). The last term is the rate energy is stored in the inductor, so \(U_B = \int_0^I Li\,di = \tfrac12LI^2\). For a solenoid, divide by the volume \(A\ell\):

\[ u_B = \frac{\tfrac12\mu_0n^2A\ell I^2}{A\ell} = \frac{(\mu_0nI)^2}{2\mu_0} = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}. \]

Magnetic energy

\[ U_B = \tfrac12LI^2, \qquad u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\quad(\text{J/m}^3) \]

Compare \(U_E = \tfrac12CV^2\) and \(u_E = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2\). Both energy densities hold for any field configuration.

10.8 Mutual inductance

Definition (Mutual inductance)

Current \(I_1\) in coil 1 produces flux linkage \(N_2\Phi_{21}\) in coil 2. The mutual inductance is \(M = N_2\Phi_{21}/I_1\), and the emf induced in coil 2 is \(\mathcal{E}_2 = -M\,dI_1/dt\). A general theorem guarantees \(M_{12} = M_{21} = M\), so you may compute it whichever way is easier.

Mutual inductance is the basis of transformers, wireless chargers, current transformers and RFID; it is also the mechanism of unwanted crosstalk between adjacent traces and cables.

Example 10.1 — Collapsing field

A 200-turn coil of area \(0.010\ \text{m}^2\) sits perpendicular to a uniform field that drops steadily from \(0.60\ \text{T}\) to zero in \(0.15\ \text{s}\). The coil's resistance is \(4.0\ \Omega\). Find the emf and current.

Show solution \[ |\mathcal{E}| = N\frac{\Delta\Phi_B}{\Delta t} = \frac{(200)(0.60\ \text{T})(0.010\ \text{m}^2)}{0.15\ \text{s}} = 8.0\ \text{V}, \qquad I = \frac{8.0}{4.0} = 2.0\ \text{A}. \]

Direction: the flux is decreasing, so the induced current flows to produce field along the original field, trying to sustain it. Units: \(\text{T·m}^2/\text{s} = \text{Wb/s} = \text{V}\).

Example 10.2 — Sliding rod

A rod of length \(0.50\ \text{m}\) slides at \(3.0\ \text{m/s}\) on rails in a \(0.40\ \text{T}\) field; the circuit resistance is \(2.0\ \Omega\). Find the emf, current, retarding force and power. Verify energy conservation.

Show solution

\(\mathcal{E} = BLv = (0.40)(0.50)(3.0) = 0.60\ \text{V}\); \(I = 0.30\ \text{A}\); \(F = ILB = (0.30)(0.50)(0.40) = 0.060\ \text{N}\).

Mechanical power in: \(Fv = 0.18\ \text{W}\). Electrical power dissipated: \(I^2R = (0.09)(2.0) = 0.18\ \text{W}\). ✓

Example 10.3 — Solenoid inductance and RL rise

A solenoid has 400 turns, length \(20\ \text{cm}\), radius \(1.0\ \text{cm}\) (air core). (a) Find \(L\). (b) A separate \(50\ \text{mH}\) inductor with \(10\ \Omega\) total resistance is connected to \(12\ \text{V}\). How long does the current take to reach \(1.0\ \text{A}\), and how much energy is stored at steady state?

Show solution

(a) \(A = \pi(0.010)^2 = 3.14\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\).

\[ L = \frac{\mu_0N^2A}{\ell} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(400)^2(3.14\times10^{-4})}{0.20}\ \text{H} = 3.2\times10^{-4}\ \text{H} = 0.32\ \text{mH}. \]

(b) \(\tau_L = L/R = 5.0\ \text{ms}\), \(I_\infty = 1.2\ \text{A}\).

\[ t = -\tau_L\ln\left(1 - \frac{1.0}{1.2}\right) = (5.0\ \text{ms})\ln6 = 9.0\ \text{ms}, \qquad U = \tfrac12LI_\infty^2 = \tfrac12(0.050)(1.2)^2 = 36\ \text{mJ}. \]

Example 10.4 — Induced field around a solenoid

A long solenoid of radius \(3.0\ \text{cm}\) has its interior field increasing at \(0.10\ \text{T/s}\). Find the induced \(E\) at \(r = 2.0\ \text{cm}\) and \(r = 6.0\ \text{cm}\).

Show solution

Inside: \(E = \tfrac{r}{2}\,dB/dt = (0.010)(0.10) = 1.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{V/m}\).

Outside: \(E = \frac{R^2}{2r}\,dB/dt = \frac{(0.030)^2}{2(0.060)}(0.10) = 7.5\times10^{-4}\ \text{V/m}\).

A field exists outside the solenoid even though \(B\) is zero there: the induced \(\vec{E}\) depends on the enclosed changing flux. Check: at \(r = R\) both give \(1.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{V/m}\).

    Answer

    \(\mathcal{E}_{\max} = NBA\omega = (100)(0.50)(0.020)(2\pi\cdot60) = 377\ \text{V}\).

    Answer

  3. A short coil of 50 turns is wound tightly around the middle of a long solenoid (1000 turns/m, radius \(1.0\ \text{cm}\)). Find \(M\), and the emf in the coil when the solenoid current changes at \(100\ \text{A/s}\).

    Flux per coil turn \(= \mu_0n I\pi r^2\): \(M = \mu_0nN\pi r^2 = (4\pi\times10^{-7})(1000)(50)(3.14\times10^{-4}) = 20\ \mu\text{H}\). \(\mathcal{E} = M\,dI/dt = 2.0\ \text{mV}\).

    Answer

    The magnet's field points away from its north pole, into the page, and that flux is increasing. The induced field must point out of the page, which requires a counter-clockwise current as you see it. Equivalently, the loop presents a north face toward the approaching north pole and repels it.

Transmission-line theory is electromagnetism in disguise.

11. Electromagnetic oscillations & alternating current

Halliday Ch. 31 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 14–15

Connect a charged capacitor to an inductor and energy sloshes back and forth between the capacitor's electric field and the inductor's magnetic field, exactly as a mass on a spring trades potential and kinetic energy. Add resistance and the oscillation decays; add a sinusoidal driving source and you get resonance. Most of this is the bread and butter of circuit theory, so the emphasis here is on the physics: the mechanical analogy, the energy flow, and why phasors work.

11.1 LC oscillations

Derivation. With no resistance the total energy is constant:

\[ U = \frac{q^2}{2C} + \frac12Li^2, \qquad \frac{dU}{dt} = \frac{q}{C}\frac{dq}{dt} + Li\frac{di}{dt} = 0. \]

With \(i = dq/dt\), divide by \(i\):

\[ L\frac{d^2q}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{C}q = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; q(t) = Q\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}. \]

Mechanical analogy

Spring–massLC circuit
position \(x\)charge \(q\)
velocity \(v\)current \(i\)
mass \(m\)inductance \(L\)
spring constant \(k\)\(1/C\)
damping \(b\)resistance \(R\)
\(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\)\(\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}\)
\(\tfrac12kx^2 + \tfrac12mv^2\)\(q^2/(2C) + \tfrac12Li^2\)

The current \(i = -\omega Q\sin(\omega t+\phi)\) is a quarter-cycle out of step with the charge: when the capacitor is fully charged, no current flows and all energy is electric; a quarter period later the capacitor is empty, the current is at its maximum \(I = \omega Q\), and all energy is magnetic.

11.2 Damped RLC oscillations

Adding series resistance: \(L\ddot{q} + R\dot{q} + q/C = 0\). For small \(R\) (underdamped, \(R \lt 2\sqrt{L/C}\)):

\[ q(t) = Qe^{-Rt/2L}\cos(\omega' t + \phi), \qquad \omega' = \sqrt{\omega^2 - \left(\frac{R}{2L}\right)^2}. \]

The amplitude decays with time constant \(2L/R\), and the energy (proportional to amplitude squared) with \(L/R\). At \(R = 2\sqrt{L/C}\) the circuit is critically damped (fastest non-oscillating return); above it, overdamped. The quality factor \(Q_f = \omega_0L/R\) measures how many cycles the oscillation survives (roughly \(2\pi\times\) stored energy / energy lost per cycle).

11.3 AC & phasors: R, C and L

Drive each element with \(i = I\sin\omega_dt\) and look at the voltage across it:

  • Resistor: \(v_R = IR\sin\omega_dt\). In phase.
  • Capacitor: \(q = \int i\,dt = -\frac{I}{\omega_d}\cos\omega_dt\), so \(v_C = \frac{I}{\omega_dC}\sin(\omega_dt - 90^\circ)\). Voltage lags current by \(90^\circ\). Amplitude \(V_C = IX_C\) with capacitive reactance \(X_C = 1/(\omega_dC)\).
  • Inductor: \(v_L = L\,di/dt = \omega_dLI\cos\omega_dt = IX_L\sin(\omega_dt + 90^\circ)\). Voltage leads current by \(90^\circ\). Inductive reactance \(X_L = \omega_dL\).

Study tip

Mnemonic: "ELI the ICE man". In an inductor (L), the emf E comes before the current I; in a capacitor (C), I comes before E. Physicists draw phasors as rotating vectors; engineers write complex impedances \(Z_R = R\), \(Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)\), \(Z_L = j\omega L\). They are the same thing: the phasor is the complex amplitude, and its projection onto the vertical (or real) axis is the instantaneous value. Use whichever you prefer, but be fluent in both.

Why phasors work: the circuit equations are linear with constant coefficients, so a sinusoidal drive produces a sinusoidal steady-state response at the same frequency. Only amplitude and phase need tracking, and differentiation becomes multiplication by \(j\omega\).

11.4 Series RLC: impedance & resonance

A series RLC driven by \(\mathcal{E} = \mathcal{E}_m\sin\omega_dt\) carries a common current \(i = I\sin(\omega_dt - \phi)\). In the phasor diagram the \(V_R\) phasor is along \(I\), \(V_L\) is \(90^\circ\) ahead and \(V_C\) is \(90^\circ\) behind. Adding them as vectors:

\[ \mathcal{E}_m^2 = V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2 = I^2\left[R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2\right]. \]

Series RLC

\[ Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}, \qquad I = \frac{\mathcal{E}_m}{Z}, \qquad \tan\phi = \frac{X_L - X_C}{R}. \]

\(\phi \gt 0\): inductive, current lags emf. \(\phi \lt 0\): capacitive, current leads emf.

Resonance

The current amplitude is maximum when \(X_L = X_C\), i.e. at

\[ \omega_d = \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}. \]

There \(Z = R\), \(\phi = 0\), and the voltages across \(L\) and \(C\) (each \(Q_f\) times the source voltage) cancel. The resonance width (full width at half power) is \(\Delta\omega = R/L = \omega_0/Q_f\). This is the principle of every tuned circuit, from radio receivers to RFID tags.

11.5 Power in AC circuits

Only resistors dissipate average power; ideal L and C store energy and return it each cycle. The average of \(\sin^2\) over a cycle is \(\tfrac12\), so

\[ P_{\text{avg}} = \tfrac12I^2R = I_{\text{rms}}^2R, \qquad I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{I}{\sqrt2},\ V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V}{\sqrt2}. \]

Average power and power factor

\[ P_{\text{avg}} = \mathcal{E}_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\cos\phi, \qquad \cos\phi = \frac{R}{Z}\ (\text{power factor}). \]

The 120 V of a North American outlet is an rms value; the peak is 170 V.

A low power factor means the source must supply more current (and the wiring dissipate more \(I^2R\)) for the same useful power. Utilities penalise industrial loads with poor power factor; the fix for inductive loads (motors) is parallel capacitors.

11.6 Transformers

Two coils share an iron core, so (ideally) every turn of each links the same flux \(\Phi_B\). Faraday's law gives \(V_p = N_p\,d\Phi_B/dt\) and \(V_s = N_s\,d\Phi_B/dt\). For an ideal (lossless) transformer, energy conservation then fixes the currents:

Ideal transformer

\[ \frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}, \qquad I_sV_s = I_pV_p \Rightarrow \frac{I_s}{I_p} = \frac{N_p}{N_s}, \qquad R_{\text{eq}} = \left(\frac{N_p}{N_s}\right)^2R. \]

A load \(R\) on the secondary looks like \(R_{\text{eq}}\) from the primary (impedance transformation, used for matching).

Transformers are why the grid is AC: power is transmitted at hundreds of kilovolts so that the current, and hence the \(I^2R\) line loss, is small, then stepped down near the consumer. DC cannot be transformed this way because a constant current produces no changing flux (modern HVDC links get around this with power electronics).

Common mistake

Adding RLC voltage amplitudes as plain numbers. \(V_R + V_L + V_C \neq \mathcal{E}_m\) in general; amplitudes add as phasors (complex numbers). It is perfectly possible for \(V_L\) or \(V_C\) individually to exceed the source amplitude, as happens dramatically near resonance.

Example 11.1 — LC oscillator

A \(1.0\ \mu\text{F}\) capacitor charged to \(5.0\ \text{V}\) is connected to a \(10\ \text{mH}\) inductor. Find the oscillation frequency, the maximum current, and the time at which the energy is first shared equally.

Show solution \[ \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(0.010)(1.0\times10^{-6})}} = 1.0\times10^4\ \text{rad/s}, \qquad f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = 1.6\ \text{kHz}. \]

\(Q = CV = 5.0\ \mu\text{C}\), so \(I = \omega Q = 0.050\ \text{A}\). Energy check: \(\tfrac12CV^2 = 12.5\ \mu\text{J}\) and \(\tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12(0.010)(0.050)^2 = 12.5\ \mu\text{J}\). ✓

With \(q = Q\cos\omega t\), \(U_E = U_{\text{tot}}\cos^2\omega t\) equals half when \(\omega t = \pi/4\): \(t = \pi/(4\omega) = 79\ \mu\text{s}\) (one eighth of a period).

Example 11.2 — Series RLC at 60 Hz

A series circuit with \(R = 40\ \Omega\), \(L = 0.10\ \text{H}\), \(C = 50\ \mu\text{F}\) is connected to \(120\ \text{V}\) rms at \(60\ \text{Hz}\). Find \(Z\), \(I_{\text{rms}}\), \(\phi\), the power factor, the average power and the resonant frequency.

Show solution

\(\omega_d = 2\pi(60) = 377\ \text{rad/s}\).

\[ X_L = \omega_dL = 37.7\ \Omega, \qquad X_C = \frac{1}{\omega_dC} = \frac{1}{(377)(5.0\times10^{-5})} = 53.1\ \Omega. \] \[ Z = \sqrt{40^2 + (37.7 - 53.1)^2}\ \Omega = 42.8\ \Omega, \qquad I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{120}{42.8} = 2.80\ \text{A}. \]

\(\tan\phi = -15.4/40 \Rightarrow \phi = -21^\circ\): capacitive, the current leads. Power factor \(\cos\phi = R/Z = 0.934\).

\(P = I_{\text{rms}}^2R = (2.80)^2(40) = 314\ \text{W}\). Check: \(\mathcal{E}_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\cos\phi = (120)(2.80)(0.934) = 314\ \text{W}\). ✓

Resonance: \(f_0 = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{5.0\times10^{-6}}} = 71\ \text{Hz}\). We are driving below resonance, where \(X_C \gt X_L\), consistent with the capacitive phase.

Example 11.3 — Step-down transformer

A transformer steps \(120\ \text{V}\) rms down to \(6.0\ \text{V}\) rms. The primary has 400 turns and the secondary feeds a \(3.0\ \Omega\) load. Find \(N_s\), both currents, and the resistance seen by the source.

Show solution

\(N_s = N_p(V_s/V_p) = 400(6.0/120) = 20\) turns. \(I_s = 6.0/3.0 = 2.0\ \text{A}\); \(I_p = I_s(N_s/N_p) = 0.10\ \text{A}\).

\(R_{\text{eq}} = (N_p/N_s)^2R = (20)^2(3.0) = 1200\ \Omega\). Check: \(V_p/I_p = 120/0.10 = 1200\ \Omega\) ✓, and power \(12\ \text{W}\) on both sides.

12. Maxwell's equations & magnetism of matter

Halliday Ch. 32 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 12 (magnetism in matter) and Ch. 16 (Maxwell's equations)

By now we have laws for \(\nabla\cdot\vec{E}\) (Gauss), \(\nabla\times\vec{E}\) (Faraday) and \(\nabla\times\vec{B}\) (Ampère). Two pieces complete the set. First, there are no magnetic charges, so \(\vec{B}\) has no sources. Second, Maxwell noticed that Ampère's law is inconsistent when currents are not steady, and repaired it by adding a term in which a changing electric field produces a magnetic field, the mirror image of Faraday's law. With that symmetry in place, the equations predict self-sustaining electromagnetic waves (Chapter 13). The chapter closes with how materials respond to magnetic fields, which comes down to the magnetic dipole moments of electrons.

12.1 Gauss' law for magnetism

Law (Gauss, magnetic)

\[ \oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{A} = 0 \quad(\text{closed surface}) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \nabla\cdot\vec{B} = 0. \]

The net magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero: there are no magnetic monopoles, and field lines close on themselves. Cutting a bar magnet in half gives two smaller magnets, each with both poles, all the way down to single electrons.

12.2 Induced magnetic fields & displacement current

The problem. A capacitor is charging with current \(i\). Draw an Amperian loop around the wire. If the surface bounded by the loop is a flat disk pierced by the wire, \(I_{\text{enc}} = i\). If instead the surface is a bag that passes between the plates, no conduction current pierces it, so \(I_{\text{enc}} = 0\). The same loop cannot have two values of \(\oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{s}\), so Ampère's law as written must be incomplete.

The fix. Between the plates the electric flux is changing: \(\Phi_E = EA = q/\varepsilon_0\), so \(\varepsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt = dq/dt = i\). Maxwell defined the displacement current

\[ i_d = \varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}, \]

which exactly continues the conduction current through the gap. It is not a flow of charge, but it creates a magnetic field exactly as a real current would.

Law (Ampère–Maxwell)

\[ \oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{s} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} + \mu_0 i_{\text{enc}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \nabla\times\vec{B} = \mu_0\vec{J} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t}. \]

A changing electric flux induces a magnetic field, just as (Faraday) a changing magnetic flux induces an electric field, with the opposite sign of circulation.

Inside a charging capacitor with circular plates of radius \(R\) and uniform field, a loop of radius \(r \lt R\) encloses displacement current \(i_d(r^2/R^2)\), giving \(B = \mu_0i_dr/(2\pi R^2)\); outside the plates, \(B = \mu_0i_d/(2\pi r)\). The formulas mirror those inside and outside a thick wire.

12.3 Maxwell's equations

Maxwell's equations (integral form, vacuum with sources)

\[ \begin{aligned} \oint\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} &= \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} &&\text{Gauss' law (electric)}\\ \oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{A} &= 0 &&\text{Gauss' law (magnetic)}\\ \oint\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{s} &= -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} &&\text{Faraday's law}\\ \oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{s} &= \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} + \mu_0 i_{\text{enc}} &&\text{Ampère–Maxwell law} \end{aligned} \]

The first two are closed-surface integrals; the last two are closed-loop integrals around the boundary of the surface through which the flux is computed. Together with the Lorentz force \(\vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v}\times\vec{B})\), these describe all classical electromagnetic phenomena.

Study tip

Read them as statements about sources and circulation: electric field lines start and end on charges; magnetic field lines never start or end; electric fields circulate around changing magnetic flux; magnetic fields circulate around currents and changing electric flux. In empty space (no \(q\), no \(i\)) the equations become almost symmetric between \(\vec{E}\) and \(\vec{B}\), and that symmetry is what lets a wave sustain itself.

12.4 Magnetism of matter

Every electron carries an intrinsic spin magnetic moment of magnitude close to the Bohr magneton, \(\mu_B = e\hbar/(2m_e) = 9.274\times10^{-24}\ \text{J/T}\), plus an orbital magnetic moment associated with its orbital angular momentum. In most atoms these cancel in pairs. How the remaining moments respond to an applied field \(\vec{B}_0\) sorts materials into three classes. The response is described by the magnetization \(\vec{M}\), the net dipole moment per unit volume (A/m).

Three kinds of magnetic material

  • Diamagnetism (all materials; dominant in water, copper, bismuth, carbon): the applied field slightly alters electron orbits, inducing moments opposite to \(\vec{B}_0\) (a Lenz's-law effect at the atomic scale). Weakly repelled from strong-field regions. Very small effect; superconductors are perfect diamagnets.
  • Paramagnetism (atoms with a net moment: aluminium, oxygen, many transition-metal salts): thermal agitation randomises the moments, and \(\vec{B}_0\) partially aligns them with the field. Weakly attracted. For weak fields, Curie's law: \(M = C\,B_0/T\).
  • Ferromagnetism (iron, nickel, cobalt, and alloys): a quantum effect called exchange coupling aligns neighbouring moments spontaneously within regions called domains. An applied field grows favourably oriented domains, giving magnetizations thousands of times larger than paramagnets. Above the Curie temperature (1043 K for iron) the material becomes paramagnetic.

Hysteresis. The magnetization of a ferromagnet depends on its history: cycling the applied field traces a loop in the \(M\)–\(B_0\) plane. A "hard" material with a wide loop retains magnetization (permanent magnets, magnetic storage). A "soft" material with a narrow loop (silicon steel, ferrites) is easy to re-magnetise, and since the loop area is energy lost per cycle, soft materials are used in transformer and inductor cores. An iron core multiplies a solenoid's field and inductance by the relative permeability (hundreds to many thousands), which is why practical inductors and transformers use magnetic cores.

Earth's field is approximately that of a dipole tilted about \(10^\circ\) from the rotation axis, generated by currents in the molten outer core (a geodynamo), with a surface strength of roughly 25–65 \(\mu\text{T}\). Its geographic north is a magnetic south pole (the north pole of a compass is attracted to it), and the field has reversed many times in geological history, a record preserved in magnetized rock.

Common mistake

Thinking displacement current is a current of charges flowing between capacitor plates. Nothing crosses the gap. The term is "current" only because it enters the Ampère–Maxwell law in the same place as \(i\) and has units of amperes. Its physical content is that a changing \(\vec{E}\) produces \(\vec{B}\).

Example 12.1 — Magnetic field in a charging capacitor

A parallel-plate capacitor with circular plates of radius \(5.0\ \text{cm}\) is charged by a current of \(2.0\ \text{A}\). Find \(d E/dt\) between the plates, and \(B\) at \(r = 3.0\ \text{cm}\) and \(r = 8.0\ \text{cm}\) from the axis.

Show solution

\(A = \pi(0.050)^2 = 7.85\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2\). Since \(i_d = \varepsilon_0A\,dE/dt = i\):

\[ \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{i}{\varepsilon_0A} = \frac{2.0}{(8.854\times10^{-12})(7.85\times10^{-3})} = 2.9\times10^{13}\ \text{V/(m·s)}. \]

Inside: \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0i_dr}{2\pi R^2} = \dfrac{(2\times10^{-7})(2.0)(0.030)}{(0.050)^2} = 4.8\ \mu\text{T}\).

Outside: \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0i_d}{2\pi r} = \dfrac{(2\times10^{-7})(2.0)}{0.080} = 5.0\ \mu\text{T}\).

Check: at \(r = R\) both give \(8.0\ \mu\text{T}\), the same as around the lead wire at that radius. These fields are small, which is why the displacement term was not noticed experimentally before Maxwell predicted it; its dramatic consequence is radiation.

Example 12.2 — Saturation magnetization of iron

Iron has density \(7.9\times10^3\ \text{kg/m}^3\), molar mass \(55.85\ \text{g/mol}\), and contributes about \(2.2\,\mu_B\) per atom when fully aligned. Estimate the saturation magnetization, and the dipole moment of a fully magnetized bar of volume \(5.0\ \text{cm}^3\).

Show solution

Atom density: \(n = \dfrac{\rho N_A}{M} = \dfrac{(7.9\times10^3)(6.022\times10^{23})}{0.05585} = 8.5\times10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}\).

\[ M_{\text{sat}} = n(2.2\mu_B) = (8.5\times10^{28})(2.2)(9.274\times10^{-24})\ \text{A/m} = 1.7\times10^6\ \text{A/m}, \]

in good agreement with measured values. Bar: \(\mu = M_{\text{sat}}V = (1.7\times10^6)(5.0\times10^{-6}) = 8.7\ \text{A·m}^2\). For comparison, a 100-turn coil of area \(10\ \text{cm}^2\) would need 87 A to match it.

Practice problems

  1. The voltage across a \(1.0\ \mu\text{F}\) capacitor is changing at \(1.0\times10^6\ \text{V/s}\). What is the displacement current between its plates?
    Answer

    \(i_d = \varepsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt = \varepsilon_0A\,d(V/d)/dt = C\,dV/dt = 1.0\ \text{A}\), equal to the conduction current in the leads.

  2. In vacuum, \(E = E_0\sin\omega t\) with \(E_0 = 100\ \text{V/m}\) and \(f = 1.0\ \text{MHz}\). Find the amplitude of the displacement current density.
    Answer

    \(J_d = \varepsilon_0\,\partial E/\partial t\), amplitude \(\varepsilon_0\omega E_0 = (8.854\times10^{-12})(2\pi\times10^6)(100) = 5.6\times10^{-3}\ \text{A/m}^2\).

  3. A paramagnetic sample obeying Curie's law is cooled from 300 K to 75 K in the same weak field. How does its magnetization change?
    Answer

    \(M \propto 1/T\): it quadruples.

  4. A closed surface surrounds the north pole of a bar magnet but not the south pole. What is the net magnetic flux through it, and why?
    Answer

    Zero. Field lines leaving the north pole outside the magnet re-enter the surface through the body of the magnet (inside the magnet, \(\vec{B}\) runs from south to north). No surface can isolate a pole.

  5. Show that the displacement-current term in Ampère–Maxwell has units of current.
    Answer

    \(\varepsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt\): \(\frac{\text{C}^2}{\text{N·m}^2}\cdot\frac{\text{N}}{\text{C}}\cdot\frac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}} = \frac{\text{C}}{\text{s}} = \text{A}\).

13. Electromagnetic waves

Halliday Ch. 33 · OpenStax Vol. 2 Ch. 16 · OpenStax Vol. 3 Ch. 1

Faraday's law says a changing \(\vec{B}\) makes an \(\vec{E}\); the Ampère–Maxwell law says a changing \(\vec{E}\) makes a \(\vec{B}\). Put the two together in empty space and you get a disturbance that regenerates itself as it travels: an electromagnetic wave. Maxwell computed its speed from two constants measured with capacitors and coils on a laboratory bench, found it equal to the measured speed of light, and concluded that light is an electromagnetic wave. Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all the same phenomenon at different frequencies. Waves carry energy and momentum, so they heat what absorbs them and push on what they hit.

13.1 The wave equation from Maxwell's equations

Derivation. In vacuum (no charges or currents), look for a wave travelling along \(x\) with \(\vec{E} = E_y(x,t)\,\hat{\jmath}\) and \(\vec{B} = B_z(x,t)\,\hat{k}\). Apply Faraday's law to a thin rectangle in the \(xy\)-plane of width \(dx\) and height \(h\). The line integral of \(\vec{E}\) picks up only the two vertical sides: \([E_y(x+dx) - E_y(x)]h = \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x}dx\,h\). The flux through the rectangle is \(B_z h\,dx\). So

\[ \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial t}. \]

Apply the Ampère–Maxwell law (no conduction current) to a thin rectangle in the \(xz\)-plane in the same way:

\[ -\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial t}. \]

Differentiate the first with respect to \(x\), the second with respect to \(t\), and eliminate the mixed derivative of \(B_z\):

\[ \frac{\partial^2E_y}{\partial x^2} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2E_y}{\partial t^2}, \]

and identically for \(B_z\). This is the one-dimensional wave equation \(\partial^2f/\partial x^2 = (1/v^2)\,\partial^2f/\partial t^2\), with speed

Speed of light

\[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1.2566\times10^{-6})(8.854\times10^{-12})}}\ \text{m/s} = 2.998\times10^8\ \text{m/s}. \]

In a non-magnetic medium with dielectric constant \(\kappa\), the speed is \(c/\sqrt{\kappa}\), so the index of refraction is \(n = \sqrt{\kappa}\) (evaluated at the wave's frequency).

13.2 Properties of a plane wave

The sinusoidal solution is \(E = E_m\sin(kx - \omega t)\), \(B = B_m\sin(kx - \omega t)\), with \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\), \(\omega = 2\pi f\), and \(c = \omega/k = f\lambda\). Substituting into \(\partial E/\partial x = -\partial B/\partial t\) gives \(kE_m = \omega B_m\), so:

Properties of an EM plane wave in vacuum

  1. \(\vec{E}\) and \(\vec{B}\) are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel (transverse wave).
  2. \(\vec{E}\times\vec{B}\) points in the direction of propagation.
  3. \(\vec{E}\) and \(\vec{B}\) oscillate in phase, with \(E = cB\) at every instant.
  4. No medium is required; the speed is \(c\) for every frequency and every (inertial) observer.

Such waves are produced by accelerating charges. An antenna is a conductor in which an AC source drives charges back and forth; a half-wave dipole antenna is about \(\lambda/2\) long.

13.3 Energy transport: the Poynting vector

The energy density is \(u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2 + B^2/(2\mu_0)\). With \(B = E/c\) and \(c^2 = 1/(\mu_0\varepsilon_0)\), the magnetic term equals the electric term: the energy is shared equally. The energy flowing per unit time per unit area is \(uc\), which motivates:

Poynting vector and intensity

\[ \vec{S} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec{E}\times\vec{B}, \qquad S = \frac{EB}{\mu_0} = \frac{E^2}{c\mu_0}, \qquad I = S_{\text{avg}} = \frac{E_m^2}{2c\mu_0} = \frac{E_{\text{rms}}^2}{c\mu_0}. \]

\(\vec{S}\) (W/m\(^2\)) points along the direction of energy flow. A point source of power \(P_s\) radiating isotropically gives \(I = P_s/(4\pi r^2)\).

The combination \(c\mu_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} \approx 377\ \Omega\) is the impedance of free space, the ratio \(E/H\) for a plane wave, the same role that \(\sqrt{L'/C'}\) plays for a coax line.

13.4 Radiation pressure

Electromagnetic waves carry momentum as well as energy: energy \(\Delta U\) carries momentum \(\Delta U/c\). If a surface of area \(A\) absorbs a wave of intensity \(I\), momentum arrives at rate \(IA/c\), producing a force and pressure:

\[ F = \frac{IA}{c},\quad p_r = \frac{I}{c}\ (\text{total absorption}); \qquad F = \frac{2IA}{c},\quad p_r = \frac{2I}{c}\ (\text{total reflection back along the path}). \]

The forces are tiny on Earth, but they shape comet tails, power solar sails, and are used in optical tweezers to hold single cells and molecules.

13.5 Polarization

Definition (Polarization)

The polarization of a wave is the direction of its \(\vec{E}\) oscillation. Light from ordinary sources (the Sun, a bulb) is unpolarized: the direction changes randomly. A polarizing sheet transmits only the component of \(\vec{E}\) along its polarizing axis.

Polarizer rules

One-half rule: unpolarized light of intensity \(I_0\) through an ideal polarizer emerges polarized with intensity \(I_0/2\).

Malus's law: polarized light through a polarizer whose axis makes angle \(\theta\) with the polarization emerges with

\[ I = I_0\cos^2\theta, \]

because the transmitted amplitude is \(E_0\cos\theta\) and intensity is proportional to amplitude squared.

Light also becomes partially polarized on reflection (glare from water and roads is mostly horizontally polarized, which polarized sunglasses block) and by scattering (blue sky light is strongly polarized at \(90^\circ\) from the Sun). LCD displays, 3D cinema glasses and photographic filters all exploit polarization.

13.6 Reflection & refraction

Laws of reflection and refraction

At a boundary between media of indices \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) (\(n = c/v\)), with angles measured from the normal:

\[ \theta_1' = \theta_1\ (\text{reflection}), \qquad n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2\ (\text{Snell's law}). \]

Total internal reflection: going from higher to lower index, there is no refracted wave beyond the critical angle \(\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1\). Brewster's angle: reflected light is completely polarized perpendicular to the plane of incidence when \(\tan\theta_B = n_2/n_1\) (then the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular).

Since \(n\) depends weakly on wavelength (dispersion), different colours refract by different amounts: prisms and rainbows. Total internal reflection guides light down optical fibres, the backbone of modern communications.

Common mistake

Using the peak field in \(I = E^2/(c\mu_0)\). With peak amplitude \(E_m\), the time-averaged intensity has a factor \(\tfrac12\): \(I = E_m^2/(2c\mu_0)\). With rms values there is no \(\tfrac12\). Also, the frequency of light never changes on entering a new medium; the speed and wavelength do.

Example 13.1 — Laser pointer

A \(5.0\ \text{mW}\) laser produces a beam \(2.0\ \text{mm}\) in diameter. Find the intensity, the peak fields, and the force on a black surface that absorbs the beam.

Show solution \[ I = \frac{P}{\pi r^2} = \frac{5.0\times10^{-3}}{\pi(1.0\times10^{-3})^2} = 1.6\times10^3\ \text{W/m}^2 \]

(slightly above full sunlight, which is why you must never look into the beam).

\[ E_m = \sqrt{2c\mu_0I} = \sqrt{2(377\ \Omega)(1.59\times10^3)} = 1.1\times10^3\ \text{V/m}, \qquad B_m = \frac{E_m}{c} = 3.7\ \mu\text{T}. \]

Force: \(F = P/c = 5.0\times10^{-3}/3.0\times10^8 = 1.7\times10^{-11}\ \text{N}\). Check: fields of order kV/m in a beam you can hold, but the radiation force is minuscule; this is why radiation pressure needs huge areas (solar sails) or tiny objects (optical tweezers).

Example 13.2 — Sunlight on Earth

The Sun radiates \(3.83\times10^{26}\ \text{W}\) and is \(1.496\times10^{11}\ \text{m}\) away. Find the intensity at Earth and the total radiation force on Earth (radius \(6.37\times10^6\ \text{m}\)), assuming full absorption.

Show solution \[ I = \frac{P_s}{4\pi r^2} = \frac{3.83\times10^{26}}{4\pi(1.496\times10^{11})^2} = 1.36\times10^3\ \text{W/m}^2. \]

Earth intercepts the beam with its cross-section \(\pi R_E^2 = 1.27\times10^{14}\ \text{m}^2\) (not its surface area):

\[ F = \frac{I\pi R_E^2}{c} = \frac{(1.36\times10^3)(1.27\times10^{14})}{3.00\times10^8}\ \text{N} = 5.8\times10^8\ \text{N}. \]

Check: the Sun's gravitational pull on Earth is about \(3.5\times10^{22}\ \text{N}\), roughly \(6\times10^{13}\) times larger.

Example 13.3 — Three polarizers

Unpolarized light of intensity \(I_0\) passes through three polarizers. The first has its axis vertical, the second at \(30^\circ\) from vertical, and the third horizontal. Find the transmitted intensity. What happens if the middle polarizer is removed?

Show solution

After 1: \(I_0/2\). After 2 (\(30^\circ\) relative): \(\tfrac12I_0\cos^230^\circ = 0.375I_0\). After 3 (\(60^\circ\) relative to polarizer 2): \(0.375I_0\cos^260^\circ = 0.094I_0\).

Without the middle sheet, the vertical and horizontal polarizers are crossed and transmit nothing. Adding a filter increases the transmitted light, a striking demonstration that a polarizer does not merely block but re-projects the field onto its axis.

Practice problems

  1. What is the wavelength of a 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi signal?
    Answer

    \(\lambda = c/f = 3.0\times10^8/2.4\times10^9 = 12.5\ \text{cm}\).

  2. A plane wave has \(E_m = 30\ \text{V/m}\). Find \(B_m\) and the intensity.
    Answer

    \(B_m = E_m/c = 1.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{T}\). \(I = E_m^2/(2c\mu_0) = 900/754 = 1.2\ \text{W/m}^2\).

  3. A 100 W isotropic source: find the intensity at 2.0 m.
    Answer

    \(I = 100/(4\pi\cdot4.0) = 2.0\ \text{W/m}^2\).

  4. Find Brewster's angle for light in air reflecting from glass with \(n = 1.50\).
    Answer

    \(\theta_B = \arctan1.50 = 56.3^\circ\).

  5. Find the critical angle for light inside glass (\(n = 1.50\)) meeting a glass–air surface.
    Answer

    \(\theta_c = \arcsin(1/1.50) = 41.8^\circ\). A 45° prism therefore totally reflects, which is how binocular prisms work.

  6. Verify from the plane-wave relations that the electric and magnetic energy densities are equal.
    Answer

    \(u_B = B^2/(2\mu_0) = E^2/(2\mu_0c^2) = E^2\mu_0\varepsilon_0/(2\mu_0) = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2 = u_E\).

14. Images

Halliday Ch. 34 · OpenStax Vol. 3 Ch. 2

When the objects light interacts with are much larger than its wavelength, we can forget the wave nature and follow rays: straight lines that bend only at reflections and refractions. An image forms where rays from a point on an object converge again (a real image, which can be projected on a screen) or where they appear to diverge from (a virtual image, which you can see but not project). Mirrors and lenses are devices for redirecting rays so that images form where we want them, at the size we want. This chapter is geometry plus one sign convention, and the sign convention is where nearly all mistakes happen.

14.1 Plane mirrors

Rays from a point object reflect from a flat mirror with equal angles and appear to come from a point as far behind the mirror as the object is in front: the image is virtual, upright, the same size, and front-to-back reversed. In the sign convention used here, \(i = -p\) (virtual images have negative image distance). A mirror need only be half your height to show your full body, regardless of distance.

14.2 Spherical mirrors

A concave mirror (reflecting surface caved in toward the object) converges parallel rays to a real focal point; a convex mirror diverges them so they appear to come from a virtual focal point behind it. For rays close to the axis (paraxial), the focal length is half the radius of curvature: \(f = r/2\).

Mirror & thin-lens equations

\[ \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{i} = \frac{1}{f}, \qquad m = -\frac{i}{p} \]

\(p\): object distance; \(i\): image distance; \(m\): lateral magnification (\(m \lt 0\): inverted). The same equation serves for mirrors and thin lenses. (OpenStax writes \(d_o\), \(d_i\) for \(p\), \(i\).)

Sign convention (real is positive)

  • \(p \gt 0\) for a real object (the usual case: object on the side light comes from).
  • \(i \gt 0\) for a real image: in front of a mirror, or on the far side of a lens. \(i \lt 0\) for a virtual image.
  • \(f \gt 0\) for concave mirrors and converging lenses; \(f \lt 0\) for convex mirrors and diverging lenses.
  • \(m \gt 0\): upright; \(m \lt 0\): inverted; \(|m| \gt 1\): enlarged.

Ray tracing (mirrors). Three easy rays from the top of the object: (1) parallel to the axis, reflects through \(F\); (2) through \(F\), reflects parallel to the axis; (3) through the centre of curvature \(C\), reflects back on itself. Two rays suffice to locate the image; draw the third as a check.

Spherical mirrors focus only paraxial rays exactly (spherical aberration); a parabolic mirror focuses all rays parallel to its axis, which is why telescope mirrors and satellite dishes are paraboloids.

14.3 Refraction at a spherical surface

Applying Snell's law with small-angle approximations to a single curved boundary between media \(n_1\) (object side) and \(n_2\):

\[ \frac{n_1}{p} + \frac{n_2}{i} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{r}, \]

with \(r \gt 0\) when the centre of curvature is on the far (refracted-light) side. A flat surface is \(r\to\infty\), giving \(i = -(n_2/n_1)p\): the familiar "apparent depth" of objects under water.

14.4 Thin lenses

A lens is two refracting surfaces. Applying the single-surface result twice and letting the thickness go to zero gives the lens equation \(1/p + 1/i = 1/f\) with the focal length set by:

Lensmaker's equation

\[ \frac{1}{f} = (n - 1)\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right) \]

for a thin lens of index \(n\) in air. \(r_1\) is the surface the light meets first. Each radius is positive if its centre of curvature is on the outgoing-light side. Validity: thin lens, paraxial rays. A lens thicker in the middle is converging (in air); thinner in the middle, diverging.

Ray tracing (lenses). (1) Parallel to the axis, then through the far focal point \(F_2\) (converging) or as if from the near focal point \(F_1\) (diverging). (2) Through the centre of the lens, undeviated. (3) Through (or toward) \(F_1\), emerging parallel.

Systems of lenses. The image formed by the first lens is the object for the second. If that image lies beyond the second lens, it is a virtual object with negative \(p\). The overall magnification is the product \(m = m_1m_2\). Optometrists use the power \(P = 1/f\) in dioptres (\(\text{m}^{-1}\)); thin lenses in contact add powers.

Study tip

Always sketch a quick ray diagram before plugging numbers. It tells you in advance whether the image is real or virtual, upright or inverted, larger or smaller, which catches sign errors immediately. Also memorise the landmark cases for a converging element: object beyond \(2f\) gives a reduced real image; at \(2f\), same size; between \(f\) and \(2f\), enlarged real; inside \(f\), enlarged upright virtual (magnifier).

Common mistake

Carrying over a sign convention from another book. Some texts (and most optical-design software) use a Cartesian convention where distances to the left are negative, and the lens equation becomes \(1/s' - 1/s = 1/f\). Both are self-consistent; mixing them is fatal. Here: real is positive, and \(1/p + 1/i = 1/f\).

14.5 Optical instruments

What matters for seeing detail is the angle an object subtends at the eye. The unaided eye can focus no closer than the near point, conventionally \(25\ \text{cm}\).

Instrument magnifications

  • Simple magnifier (object at \(F\), image at infinity): \(m_\theta \approx \dfrac{25\ \text{cm}}{f}\).
  • Compound microscope (objective forms a real enlarged image at the eyepiece's focal point; tube length \(s\) between the inner focal points): \(M = -\dfrac{s}{f_{\text{ob}}}\cdot\dfrac{25\ \text{cm}}{f_{\text{ey}}}\).
  • Refracting telescope (objective images a distant object at its focal point, coinciding with the eyepiece's focal point): \(m_\theta = -\dfrac{f_{\text{ob}}}{f_{\text{ey}}}\).

A telescope's magnification is only half the story; its aperture sets how much light it gathers and, through diffraction (Chapter 16), the finest detail it can resolve.

Example 14.1 — Concave mirror, two object positions

A concave mirror has \(f = 10\ \text{cm}\). Locate and describe the image for an object at (a) \(15\ \text{cm}\), (b) \(5.0\ \text{cm}\).

Show solution

(a) \(\dfrac1i = \dfrac1{10} - \dfrac1{15} = \dfrac1{30}\Rightarrow i = +30\ \text{cm}\). Real (in front of the mirror). \(m = -30/15 = -2\): inverted, twice the size.

(b) \(\dfrac1i = \dfrac1{10} - \dfrac15 = -\dfrac1{10}\Rightarrow i = -10\ \text{cm}\). Virtual, 10 cm behind the mirror. \(m = -(-10)/5 = +2\): upright, twice the size (a shaving or makeup mirror).

Check with landmarks: (a) has the object between \(f\) and \(2f\) (enlarged real image), (b) has it inside \(f\) (enlarged virtual image).

Example 14.2 — Convex security mirror

A convex mirror with \(f = -20\ \text{cm}\) views a shopper \(1.0\ \text{m}\) away. Find the image position and magnification.

Show solution \[ \frac1i = -\frac1{20} - \frac1{100} = -\frac{6}{100}\Rightarrow i = -16.7\ \text{cm}, \qquad m = -\frac{i}{p} = +0.17. \]

Virtual, upright and about one sixth size: a convex mirror always gives a reduced upright virtual image, trading size for a wide field of view.

Example 14.3 — Lensmaker

A biconvex glass lens (\(n = 1.50\)) has surface radii \(20\ \text{cm}\) (first surface) and \(30\ \text{cm}\). Find its focal length in air, and in water (\(n_w = 1.33\)).

Show solution

Biconvex: first surface's centre is on the far side (\(r_1 = +20\ \text{cm}\)), second surface's centre on the near side (\(r_2 = -30\ \text{cm}\)).

\[ \frac1f = 0.50\left(\frac1{20} + \frac1{30}\right) = 0.50\cdot\frac{5}{60} = \frac1{24}\Rightarrow f = 24\ \text{cm}. \]

In water, replace \((n-1)\) by \((n/n_w - 1) = 0.128\): \(f = 24(0.50/0.128) = 94\ \text{cm}\). The lens is much weaker underwater, which is why your eyes (whose cornea does most of the focusing) blur when you open them while swimming.

Example 14.4 — Two-lens system

Converging lenses with \(f_1 = 10\ \text{cm}\) and \(f_2 = 15\ \text{cm}\) are \(40\ \text{cm}\) apart. An object is \(15\ \text{cm}\) in front of lens 1. Find the final image and the overall magnification.

Show solution

Lens 1: \(\dfrac1{i_1} = \dfrac1{10} - \dfrac1{15} = \dfrac1{30}\Rightarrow i_1 = 30\ \text{cm}\), \(m_1 = -2\).

That image is \(40 - 30 = 10\ \text{cm}\) in front of lens 2: \(p_2 = 10\ \text{cm}\), inside \(f_2\).

Lens 2: \(\dfrac1{i_2} = \dfrac1{15} - \dfrac1{10} = -\dfrac1{30}\Rightarrow i_2 = -30\ \text{cm}\) (virtual, 30 cm on the incoming side of lens 2, i.e. between the lenses, 10 cm past lens 1). \(m_2 = -(-30)/10 = +3\).

\(m = m_1m_2 = -6\): the final image is virtual, inverted relative to the object, six times larger. This is a compound microscope in miniature: lens 2 acts as a magnifier viewing lens 1's real image.

15. Interference

Halliday Ch. 35 · OpenStax Vol. 3 Ch. 3

Geometric optics fails when light passes through openings or films comparable to its wavelength. Then the wave nature shows: two waves arriving at the same point add as fields, not as intensities, so they can reinforce (bright) or cancel (dark) depending on their phase difference. Interference is behind the colours of soap bubbles and oil slicks, anti-reflection coatings on lenses, the Michelson interferometer (which measures distances to a fraction of a wavelength and detected gravitational waves), and the double-slit experiment that settled the wave nature of light. The whole chapter rests on one idea: find the phase difference.

15.1 Light as a wave

Huygens' principle: every point on a wavefront acts as a source of spherical wavelets; the new wavefront is their envelope. It reproduces the laws of reflection and refraction and, more importantly here, explains how waves spread past edges.

In a medium of index \(n\) the speed is \(c/n\) and the frequency is unchanged, so the wavelength shortens: \(\lambda_n = \lambda/n\). A thickness \(L\) of material therefore contains \(nL/\lambda\) wavelengths, versus \(L/\lambda\) in vacuum. The optical path length \(nL\) is what determines phase.

Phase difference and interference conditions

\[ \phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,\Delta L_{\text{optical}} \]

Fully constructive: \(\Delta L = m\lambda\) (\(\phi = 2\pi m\)). Fully destructive: \(\Delta L = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda\) (\(\phi = (2m+1)\pi\)), with \(m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\)

Coherence. A stable interference pattern needs sources with a constant phase relationship. Two separate light bulbs are incoherent: their relative phase fluctuates randomly on nanosecond scales, so their intensities simply add. The standard trick is to split light from one source into two paths (two slits, two surfaces of a film, two arms of an interferometer). Lasers are highly coherent.

15.2 Young's double-slit experiment

Light of wavelength \(\lambda\) passes through two narrow slits separated by \(d\), onto a screen a distance \(D \gg d\) away. To a point on the screen at angle \(\theta\) from the central axis, the rays are essentially parallel, and the path from the lower slit is longer by \(\Delta L = d\sin\theta\).

Double-slit fringes

\[ d\sin\theta = m\lambda\ (\text{bright}), \qquad d\sin\theta = \left(m + \tfrac12\right)\lambda\ (\text{dark}), \qquad m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \ldots \]

For small angles, \(y = D\tan\theta \approx D\sin\theta\), so bright fringes sit at \(y_m = m\lambda D/d\), equally spaced by \(\Delta y = \lambda D/d\).

The fringe spacing grows with \(\lambda\) and \(D\) and shrinks with \(d\): closely spaced slits give widely spaced fringes. Young used this in 1801 to make the first measurement of the wavelength of light.

15.3 Double-slit intensity

Derivation. Each slit alone gives field amplitude \(E_0\) (intensity \(I_0\)) at the screen. The two fields are \(E_1 = E_0\sin\omega t\) and \(E_2 = E_0\sin(\omega t + \phi)\), with \(\phi = \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta\). Using \(\sin a + \sin b = 2\sin\frac{a+b}{2}\cos\frac{a-b}{2}\):

\[ E = E_1 + E_2 = 2E_0\cos\frac{\phi}{2}\,\sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\phi}{2}\right). \]

The resultant amplitude is \(2E_0\cos(\phi/2)\). Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared:

Two-source interference intensity

\[ I = 4I_0\cos^2\frac{\phi}{2}, \qquad \phi = \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta. \]

Maxima of \(4I_0\) (not \(2I_0\)!) where \(\phi = 2\pi m\), zeros where \(\phi = (2m+1)\pi\). The average over many fringes is \(2I_0\): energy is redistributed, not created.

(This assumes infinitely narrow slits. Real slits of finite width modulate the pattern with a diffraction envelope, Section 16.4.) The same phasor-addition method used in AC circuits works here: add two phasors of length \(E_0\) separated by angle \(\phi\).

15.4 Thin-film interference

Light reflecting from the top and bottom surfaces of a thin film interferes. Two effects set the phase difference:

  1. Path difference: at near-normal incidence the lower ray travels an extra \(2L\) inside the film, i.e. \(2L/\lambda_n = 2nL/\lambda\) wavelengths.
  2. Reflection phase shift: reflection from a medium of higher index adds a half-wavelength shift (\(\pi\) phase); reflection from a lower index adds none. (Analogous to a pulse on a string reflecting from a heavier or lighter rope, or to a transmission line terminated in a low or high impedance.)

Thin-film conditions (normal incidence)

One reflection phase shift (e.g. soap film in air, oil on water):

\[ 2nL = \left(m + \tfrac12\right)\lambda\ \text{bright}, \qquad 2nL = m\lambda\ \text{dark}. \]

Zero or two phase shifts (e.g. coating with \(n_{\text{air}} \lt n_{\text{film}} \lt n_{\text{glass}}\)):

\[ 2nL = m\lambda\ \text{bright}, \qquad 2nL = \left(m + \tfrac12\right)\lambda\ \text{dark}. \]

Study tip

Never memorise "which formula is bright". Instead: (1) count the reflection phase shifts at the two surfaces; (2) if they are the same (0 or 2), the path difference \(2nL\) alone decides; (3) if they differ (1), swap the bright and dark conditions. And use \(\lambda\) in vacuum together with \(n\), or \(\lambda_n\) without \(n\), but not both.

A film much thinner than the wavelength (\(L\to0\)) with one phase shift reflects destructively for all colours: the top of a draining soap film turns black just before it pops.

15.5 The Michelson interferometer

A beam splitter sends light along two perpendicular arms to mirrors \(M_1\) and \(M_2\); the returning beams recombine and interfere at a detector. Moving one mirror a distance \(\Delta d\) changes that arm's round-trip path by \(2\Delta d\), shifting the pattern by

\[ N = \frac{2\Delta d}{\lambda}\ \text{fringes}. \]

Inserting a transparent slab of thickness \(L\) and index \(n\) into one arm changes the optical path by \(2L(n-1)\), shifting \(N = 2L(n-1)/\lambda\) fringes. Michelson and Morley used this instrument in 1887 to search for motion through the "ether" and found none, a key experimental foundation for special relativity. The LIGO gravitational-wave detectors are Michelson interferometers with 4 km arms that sense length changes of about \(10^{-18}\ \text{m}\).

Common mistake

Forgetting the factor of 2 in Michelson and thin-film problems. Light traverses the arm (or film) twice, out and back, so the path difference is twice the mirror displacement or film thickness.

Example 15.1 — Double-slit fringes

Red light (\(633\ \text{nm}\)) illuminates slits \(0.15\ \text{mm}\) apart, with a screen \(2.0\ \text{m}\) away. Find the fringe spacing and the position of the third bright fringe.

Show solution \[ \Delta y = \frac{\lambda D}{d} = \frac{(633\times10^{-9})(2.0)}{1.5\times10^{-4}}\ \text{m} = 8.4\ \text{mm}, \qquad y_3 = 3\Delta y = 25\ \text{mm}. \]

Check small angles: \(\theta_3 \approx 0.025/2.0 = 0.013\ \text{rad}\), so \(\sin\theta\approx\tan\theta\) is excellent.

Example 15.2 — Intensity between fringes

For the setup of Example 15.1, what fraction of the maximum intensity reaches a point \(2.0\ \text{mm}\) from the central maximum?

Show solution \[ \phi = \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\frac{y}{D} = \frac{2\pi(1.5\times10^{-4})(2.0\times10^{-3})}{(633\times10^{-9})(2.0)} = 1.49\ \text{rad}. \] \[ \frac{I}{I_{\max}} = \cos^2\frac{\phi}{2} = \cos^2(0.744) = 0.54. \]

Check: \(2.0\ \text{mm}\) is just under a quarter of the 8.4 mm fringe spacing, where \(\phi = \pi/2\) would give exactly 0.50. Slightly closer to the centre gives slightly more.

Example 15.3 — Soap film and anti-reflection coating

(a) What is the thinnest soap film (\(n = 1.33\), air on both sides) that strongly reflects \(550\ \text{nm}\) light? (b) What is the thinnest MgF\(_2\) coating (\(n = 1.38\)) on glass (\(n = 1.50\)) that minimises reflection at \(550\ \text{nm}\)?

Show solution

(a) Top reflection (air to film) shifts; bottom (film to air) does not: one shift. Bright: \(2nL = \tfrac12\lambda\) for \(m = 0\):

\[ L = \frac{\lambda}{4n} = \frac{550\ \text{nm}}{4(1.33)} = 103\ \text{nm}. \]

(b) Both reflections (air to MgF\(_2\), MgF\(_2\) to glass) go to higher index: two shifts, which cancel. Dark: \(2nL = \tfrac12\lambda\):

\[ L = \frac{\lambda}{4n} = \frac{550\ \text{nm}}{4(1.38)} = 99.6\ \text{nm}. \]

Both are "quarter-wave" layers, but for opposite reasons. Designed for mid-spectrum green, the coating reflects a little red and blue, giving coated lenses their purple tint. (It is the optical twin of a quarter-wave impedance transformer.)

Example 15.4 — Counting Michelson fringes

One mirror of a Michelson interferometer illuminated at \(632.8\ \text{nm}\) is moved \(0.100\ \text{mm}\). How many fringes pass the detector?

Show solution \[ N = \frac{2\Delta d}{\lambda} = \frac{2(1.00\times10^{-4})}{632.8\times10^{-9}} = 316. \]

Counting fringes measures the displacement to within a fraction of a wavelength, about \(0.3\ \mu\text{m}\) per fringe.

16. Diffraction

Halliday Ch. 36 · OpenStax Vol. 3 Ch. 4

Diffraction is interference among the infinitely many Huygens wavelets coming from different parts of a single opening. Its practical message is a limit: a wave passing through an aperture of width \(a\) spreads by an angle of roughly \(\lambda/a\). That spreading blurs every image, so no lens, telescope, microscope, camera or antenna can resolve detail finer than about \(\lambda/(\text{aperture})\) in angle. The same physics, exploited deliberately with many regularly spaced openings, gives the diffraction grating (the heart of spectrometers) and, with the atomic planes of a crystal as the "grating", X-ray crystallography.

16.1 Single-slit diffraction: locating the minima

Derivation by pairing. Light of wavelength \(\lambda\) passes through a slit of width \(a\) to a distant screen. Divide the slit into two halves. For each point in the upper half there is a partner exactly \(a/2\) below it in the lower half. Their path difference to angle \(\theta\) is \((a/2)\sin\theta\). If this equals \(\lambda/2\), every pair cancels and the screen is dark:

\[ \frac{a}{2}\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{2}\;\Rightarrow\; a\sin\theta = \lambda. \]

Dividing the slit into 4, 6, ... zones and pairing adjacent zones in the same way gives the general result:

Single-slit minima

\[ a\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = \pm1, \pm2, \pm3, \ldots\ (\text{not } m = 0) \]

The central maximum (between \(m = \pm1\)) is twice as wide as the others. On a screen at distance \(D\), its full width is \(2\lambda D/a\) for small angles. Narrower slit, wider pattern.

Common mistake

Confusing \(a\sin\theta = m\lambda\) with the double-slit formula \(d\sin\theta = m\lambda\). They look identical but mean opposite things: for a single slit of width \(a\) the condition gives dark fringes (with \(m \neq 0\)); for two slits separated by \(d\) it gives bright fringes. Always note which length the formula contains.

16.2 Single-slit intensity

Derivation by integration. Let \(y\) run across the slit from \(-a/2\) to \(a/2\). A strip of width \(dy\) contributes a wavelet of amplitude proportional to \(dy\) and phase \(ky\sin\theta\) relative to the centre, with \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\). Using complex phasors:

\[ E_\theta \propto \int_{-a/2}^{a/2} e^{iky\sin\theta}\,dy = \frac{2\sin\left(\tfrac12ka\sin\theta\right)}{k\sin\theta} = a\,\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta. \]

Single-slit intensity

\[ I(\theta) = I_m\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^2, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta \]

Zeros where \(\alpha = m\pi\), i.e. \(a\sin\theta = m\lambda\), matching Section 16.1. The secondary maxima are near (not exactly at) \(\alpha \approx (m + \tfrac12)\pi\), with intensities of only 4.7%, 1.6%, 0.8%, ... of \(I_m\).

Engineers will recognise the sinc function: the far-field (Fraunhofer) pattern of an aperture is the Fourier transform of its transmission function. A rectangular aperture gives a sinc, just as a rectangular pulse in time has a sinc spectrum. Antenna-array and radar beam patterns are computed the same way.

16.3 Circular apertures & the Rayleigh criterion

A circular aperture of diameter \(d\) (a lens, a pupil, a telescope mirror, a dish antenna) produces a central bright disk (the Airy disk) surrounded by faint rings. The first minimum is at

\[ \sin\theta = 1.22\frac{\lambda}{d}. \]

The factor 1.22 comes from the zeros of a Bessel function, the circular analogue of the sinc.

Rayleigh criterion

Two point sources are just resolvable when the central maximum of one falls on the first minimum of the other, i.e. when their angular separation is at least

\[ \theta_R \approx 1.22\frac{\lambda}{d}\quad(\text{small angles, radians}). \]

This is a convention (a reasonable one), not a hard physical boundary. Shorter wavelengths and larger apertures give finer resolution: the motivation for large telescopes, for electron microscopes (very short de Broglie wavelengths) and for UV lithography.

16.4 Double slit with finite slit width

Real double slits each have width \(a\). The pattern is the two-slit interference term multiplied by the single-slit diffraction envelope:

\[ I(\theta) = I_m\cos^2\beta\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^2, \qquad \beta = \frac{\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta, \quad \alpha = \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta. \]

The fine fringes (spacing set by \(d\)) are modulated by a broad envelope (width set by \(a\lt d\)). Where an interference maximum coincides with a diffraction minimum, that bright fringe is missing: this happens when \(d/a\) is an integer, for orders \(m = (d/a)\times\)integer.

16.5 Diffraction gratings

A grating has \(N\) slits (or rulings) with spacing \(d\). Maxima still occur where adjacent slits are in phase, but with many slits they become extremely sharp, because adding \(N\) phasors gives a large resultant only when they are almost exactly aligned.

Grating relations

\[ d\sin\theta = m\lambda\ (\text{principal maxima, "lines"}), \qquad \Delta\theta_{\text{hw}} = \frac{\lambda}{Nd\cos\theta}\ (\text{half-width of a line}) \] \[ R = \frac{\lambda_{\text{avg}}}{\Delta\lambda} = Nm\ (\text{resolving power}), \qquad D = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta\lambda} = \frac{m}{d\cos\theta}\ (\text{dispersion}). \]

Resolving power depends on the total number of illuminated rulings; dispersion (how far apart lines are spread) depends on the spacing. A grating is specified as lines per mm: \(d = 1/(\text{lines per unit length})\).

Since \(\sin\theta \le 1\), the highest observable order is the largest integer less than \(d/\lambda\). The rainbow sheen of a CD or DVD is grating diffraction from its closely spaced data tracks.

16.6 X-ray diffraction

X-ray wavelengths (around 0.1 nm) are comparable to atomic spacings, so a crystal acts as a three-dimensional grating. Treat each family of parallel atomic planes, spacing \(d\), as a set of partial mirrors. Rays reflecting from adjacent planes differ in path by \(2d\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is measured from the planes (not the normal).

Bragg's law

\[ 2d\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]

Strong reflection ("Bragg peaks") occurs only at these angles. Measuring the angles gives \(d\) and hence the crystal structure; the intensities reveal what is in the unit cell. This technique determined the structure of DNA and of countless proteins and materials.

Study tip

Collect the "one-liners" for this chapter: single-slit minimum \(a\sin\theta = m\lambda\); circular first minimum \(1.22\lambda/d\); grating maxima \(d\sin\theta = m\lambda\); Bragg \(2d\sin\theta = m\lambda\) with \(\theta\) from the planes. Before solving, identify which geometry you have and which length (\(a\), \(d\), aperture diameter, plane spacing) appears.

Example 16.1 — Width of the central maximum

Light at \(550\ \text{nm}\) passes through a slit \(0.040\ \text{mm}\) wide onto a screen \(1.5\ \text{m}\) away. Find the width of the central bright band.

Show solution \[ w = \frac{2\lambda D}{a} = \frac{2(550\times10^{-9})(1.5)}{4.0\times10^{-5}}\ \text{m} = 4.1\ \text{cm}. \]

Check: \(\theta_1 = \lambda/a = 0.014\ \text{rad}\), small, so the approximation is fine. Halving the slit width would double the band.

Example 16.2 — Resolution of the eye and of a telescope

(a) A pupil is \(3.0\ \text{mm}\) across. At \(550\ \text{nm}\), what is its diffraction-limited resolution, and at what distance could two car headlights \(1.5\ \text{m}\) apart just be resolved? (b) Repeat the angle for a \(2.4\ \text{m}\) space telescope.

Show solution

(a) \(\theta_R = 1.22(550\times10^{-9})/(3.0\times10^{-3}) = 2.2\times10^{-4}\ \text{rad}\). Separation over distance \(L\): \(1.5/L = \theta_R \Rightarrow L = 6.7\ \text{km}\). (In practice the eye does somewhat worse because of aberrations and the spacing of retinal cones.)

(b) \(\theta_R = 1.22(550\times10^{-9})/2.4 = 2.8\times10^{-7}\ \text{rad}\), about 0.06 arcseconds: some 800 times finer than the eye.

Example 16.3 — Resolving the sodium doublet

A grating with 600 lines/mm is illuminated by sodium light containing \(589.0\ \text{nm}\) and \(589.6\ \text{nm}\). (a) At what angles is the first-order \(589.0\ \text{nm}\) line found, and what is the highest order? (b) How many rulings must be illuminated to resolve the doublet in second order?

Show solution

\(d = 1/(600\ \text{mm}^{-1}) = 1.667\ \mu\text{m}\).

(a) \(\sin\theta_1 = 589.0\times10^{-9}/1.667\times10^{-6} = 0.353 \Rightarrow \theta_1 = 20.7^\circ\). Second order: \(\sin\theta_2 = 0.707\Rightarrow\theta_2 = 45.0^\circ\). Third order would need \(\sin\theta = 1.06\): impossible, so \(m_{\max} = 2\).

(b) Required \(R = \lambda_{\text{avg}}/\Delta\lambda = 589.3/0.6 = 982\). With \(m = 2\): \(N = R/m = 491\) rulings, i.e. a beam only about \(0.82\ \text{mm}\) wide on this grating.

Example 16.4 — Bragg reflection from NaCl

X-rays of wavelength \(0.154\ \text{nm}\) reflect from planes in a sodium chloride crystal with spacing \(0.282\ \text{nm}\). Find the first- and second-order Bragg angles.

Show solution \[ \sin\theta_1 = \frac{\lambda}{2d} = \frac{0.154}{0.564} = 0.273\Rightarrow\theta_1 = 15.8^\circ, \qquad \sin\theta_2 = 0.546\Rightarrow\theta_2 = 33.1^\circ. \]

The angle between incident and diffracted beams is \(2\theta\), which is what a diffractometer actually records (the "\(2\theta\)" axis of an X-ray pattern).

  4. A grating has 5000 lines/cm. What is the highest order visible for \(500\ \text{nm}\) light?
    Answer

    \(d = 2.0\ \mu\text{m}\), \(d/\lambda = 4\). Order 4 would need \(\theta = 90^\circ\) (grazing, not observable), so the highest visible order is 3.

  5. A 25 m radio dish observes the 21 cm hydrogen line. What is its angular resolution?
    Answer

    \(\theta_R = 1.22(0.21)/25 = 0.010\ \text{rad} = 0.59^\circ\), about the angular size of the full Moon. That is why radio astronomers combine dishes into interferometers spanning continents.

  6. First-order Bragg reflection of \(0.10\ \text{nm}\) X-rays occurs at \(12.0^\circ\). Find the plane spacing.
    Answer

    \(d = \lambda/(2\sin12.0^\circ) = 0.10/(2\cdot0.208) = 0.24\ \text{nm}\).
